1. Overview: Specification and Data Problems in Regression

Chapter 8 treated heteroskedasticity as a relatively minor failure of the Gauss-Markov assumptions. Heteroskedasticity does not cause bias or inconsistency in ordinary least squares (OLS) estimators, and valid inference can be recovered through adjusted standard errors, t and F statistics, or weighted least squares. This chapter turns to a more serious problem: correlation between the error term, $u$, and one or more of the explanatory variables. When the error is correlated with an explanatory variable $x_j$ for any reason, $x_j$ is called an endogenous explanatory variable.

The chapter organizes the sources of endogeneity into three broad categories. First, omitting a key variable causes correlation between the error and included regressors, which generally biases and makes inconsistent all OLS estimators. When the omitted variable is a function of an included explanatory variable, the model suffers from functional form misspecification. Second, the unobserved variable can be proxied by an observable variable that is related to it, reducing or eliminating omitted-variable bias. Third, measurement error in the dependent or independent variables changes the estimable model in specific ways. All procedures in this chapter remain based on OLS estimation of a single cross section; problems that cannot be solved this way are deferred to Part 3.

Population Multiple Regression Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \]

Here $y$ is the dependent variable, $x_1, \dots, x_k$ are the explanatory variables, $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\beta_j$ is the partial effect of $x_j$ holding other regressors fixed, and $u$ is the unobserved error term. OLS delivers unbiased and consistent estimators when the error has zero mean and is uncorrelated with every explanatory variable (zero conditional mean assumption, MLR.4). The present chapter is devoted to cases where this assumption fails because $u$ contains an omitted variable, a mismeasured variable, or a nonlinear function of the regressors, making $x_j$ endogenous.

In practice, the researcher should first ask whether the zero conditional mean assumption is plausible. If it is not, the next question is which specific data problem generates the correlation. The choice of remedy—adding a quadratic term, applying RESET, using a proxy variable, including a lagged dependent variable, or acknowledging measurement error—depends on the diagnosis. The chapter emphasizes that obtaining a statistically significant coefficient does not establish causality; the conditions under which the coefficient can be given a causal interpretation must be defended.

Example

The wage equation $\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 abil + u$ illustrates the omitted-variable problem: ability is unobserved, and if $educ$ is correlated with $abil$, then $educ$ is endogenous. The housing price models in Section 9-1 illustrate functional form misspecification, and the savings function in Section 9-4 illustrates measurement error in the dependent variable.

Analogy

Heteroskedasticity is like a bathroom scale that is sometimes imprecise but not systematically wrong: your weight readings are noisy, but the noise does not distort the average trend. Endogeneity is like a scale that reads higher whenever you stand on the left side of the mat: the measurement instrument itself is correlated with your position, so the recorded relationship between position and weight is systematically misleading.

Key Insight

Heteroskedasticity affects only the precision of OLS estimates; endogeneity affects their target. If $u$ is correlated with $x_j$, OLS is biased and inconsistent, and no amount of robust standard errors can fix the problem. The researcher must address the source of endogeneity.

Key Takeaways

  • An endogenous explanatory variable is one that is correlated with the error term.
  • Endogeneity arises from omitted variables, functional form misspecification, measurement error, and other sources.
  • Heteroskedasticity is a minor problem; endogeneity is a serious problem requiring specification changes, proxies, or alternative estimators.
  • All methods in this chapter use OLS on a single cross section; structural remedies appear in later chapters.

2. Functional Form Misspecification

A multiple regression model suffers from functional form misspecification when it does not properly account for the relationship between the dependent variable and the observed explanatory variables. The classic example is a wage equation that should include a quadratic experience term but omits it. If the true model is $\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 exper^2 + u$ and the researcher estimates the model without $exper^2$, then all OLS estimators of $\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2$ are generally biased. The amount of bias depends on the size of $\beta_3$ and on the correlations among $educ$, $exper$, and $exper^2$.

Omitting a quadratic term has especially severe consequences for the variable that enters nonlinearly. Even if an unbiased estimator of $\beta_2$ were available, the return to experience is not $\beta_2$; it is $\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 exper$ (in decimal form). Using the biased estimator of $\beta_2$ alone is misleading, particularly at extreme values of $exper$. The same logic applies to omitted interaction terms: if the return to education depends on gender through the interaction $female \cdot educ$, omitting that interaction misspecifies the functional form, and it is unclear what "return to education" the regression would recover.

Functional form problems are not limited to omitting functions of included variables. If the true model uses $\log(wage)$ as the dependent variable but the researcher uses $wage$, the partial effects will not be unbiased or consistent. The tests in this section detect many such problems. The chapter stresses, however, that in one respect functional form misspecification is a minor problem: the researcher has data on all necessary variables to estimate a flexible functional relationship. This contrasts with the omitted-variable problem of Section 9-2, where a key variable simply cannot be collected.

2.1 Consequences of Omitting Nonlinear Functions

True Log-Wage Model with Interaction
\[ \log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 exper^2 + \beta_4 female + \beta_5 (female \cdot educ) + u \tag{9.1} \]

In equation (9.1), $educ$ is years of education, $exper$ is years of labor market experience, $female$ is a binary indicator equal to 1 for women, $u$ is the error term, and $\beta_5$ allows the return to education to differ by gender. The partial effect of another year of education equals $\beta_1 + \beta_5 female$. If $female \cdot educ$ is omitted, the model is misspecified. In general, all remaining slope estimators are biased, and because the return to education is gender-dependent, a single-number estimate of the "return to education" obtained from the misspecified model is not well defined.

This example illustrates the general principle: the marginal effect of a variable in a model with quadratics or interactions depends on the values of the other regressors. Functional form choices are therefore not merely technical details; they determine what quantity is actually identified by OLS. Practical use of such models requires reporting marginal effects at relevant values (for example, at the mean of $exper$) and, when interactions are present, reporting effects separately by group.

Example

If the true model is $\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 exper^2 + u$ but $exper^2$ is omitted, the estimated return to education is biased. The return to experience is $\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 exper$, not $\beta_2$. Practical lesson: when a variable enters as a quadratic, interpret its effect only through the derivative and evaluate it over the support of the data.

Analogy

Suppose a car's fuel efficiency first improves as speed rises to 60 mph, then deteriorates at higher speeds. A model that includes only a linear "speed" term would conclude that speed always hurts efficiency, missing the true parabolic relationship. Omitting $speed^2$ is a functional form misspecification that biases the estimated effect of speed everywhere.

Key Insight

Omitting a quadratic or interaction term biases all coefficients, not just the coefficient on the nonlinear term. The researcher should specify marginal effects as derivatives of the conditional expectation, not as individual coefficients, whenever the model contains powers or products of regressors.

3.1 Using Quadratic Terms to Detect Functional Form Problems

Crime Model with Quadratic Terms
\[ narr86 = \beta_0 + \beta_1 pcnv + \beta_2 avgsen + \beta_3 tottime + \beta_4 ptime86 + \beta_5 qemp86 + \beta_6 inc86 + \beta_7 black + \beta_8 hispan + \delta_1 pcnv^2 + \delta_2 ptime86^2 + \delta_3 inc86^2 + u \]

A powerful and simple detection tool is the F test for joint exclusion restrictions. It often makes sense to add quadratic terms for the significant explanatory variables and jointly test their significance. If the quadratics are jointly significant, the model is misspecified. In the crime model of Example 9.1, the dependent variable $narr86$ is the number of arrests in 1986; $pcnv$ is the proportion of prior convictions, $avgsen$ average sentence length, $tottime$ total time in prison, $ptime86$ months in prison in 1986, $qemp86$ a discrete employment measure, $inc86$ legal income in hundreds of dollars, and $black$ and $hispan$ are race indicators.

Estimating the model without quadratics and then adding $pcnv^2$, $ptime86^2$, and $inc86^2$ produces a joint $F$ statistic of 31.37 with 3 and 2,713 degrees of freedom, essentially a zero p-value. The significances of individual squared terms indicate that the initial linear model overlooked important nonlinearities. Significant quadratics, however, can be symptomatic of other problems, such as using a level when a logarithm is more appropriate. In practice, adding logarithms of key variables and quadratic terms is often sufficient to detect important nonlinear economic relationships.

Example

Whether to add squares of $black$ and $hispan$: these are binary variables, so including their squares is unnecessary; a squared dummy equals the dummy itself. More useful would be interactions of $black$ and $hispan$ with other regressors, because race may modify the effects of conviction, employment, or income on arrests.

Analogy

A linear model is like describing a hill by its average slope. Adding quadratics is like acknowledging that the hill first climbs and then descends; the "effect" of moving forward depends on where you are standing. The turning point of a quadratic is the point where the hill switches from rising to falling.

Key Insight

Significant quadratic terms signal functional form misspecification, but they do not identify the reason. The correct response is to interpret marginal effects at relevant points, verify whether turning points lie inside the sample range, and consider alternative functional forms such as logarithms.

2.3 RESET as a General Test for Functional Form Misspecification

Original Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \tag{9.2} \]
RESET Expanded Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + \delta_1 \hat{y}^2 + \delta_2 \hat{y}^3 + error \tag{9.3} \]

Ramsey's (1969) regression specification error test (RESET) is a general test for functional form misspecification. If the original model satisfies MLR.4 (zero conditional mean of $u$), then no nonlinear functions of the independent variables should be significant when added to equation (9.2). Because $\hat{y}$, the OLS fitted value from equation (9.2), is a linear combination of the $x_j$, the powers $\hat{y}^2$ and $\hat{y}^3$ are nonlinear functions of the regressors. Testing $H_0: \delta_1 = 0, \delta_2 = 0$ in equation (9.3) with an $F$ statistic therefore tests whether the original model missed important nonlinearities.

Symbolically, $\hat{y}^2$ and $\hat{y}^3$ are the explanatory variables added to the expanded regression; their coefficients are $\delta_1$ and $\delta_2$. Under the null hypothesis and the Gauss-Markov assumptions, the $F$ statistic is approximately distributed as $F_{2, n-k-3}$ in large samples, because the expanded model uses $k + 2$ slope parameters plus an intercept, leaving $n - k - 3$ degrees of freedom. A Lagrange multiplier version with a chi-square distribution and two degrees of freedom is available, and the test can be made robust to heteroskedasticity using Section 8-2 methods. In practice, RESET is computed by: (1) estimate the original model, (2) save the fitted values, (3) run the expanded regression including $\hat{y}^2$ and $\hat{y}^3$, and (4) test their joint significance.

A significant RESET statistic suggests some functional form problem but provides no direction for repair: rejecting a linear housing price model does not by itself indicate that a log-log model is next. Moreover, RESET is not a general misspecification test for everything. It has no power to detect omitted variables whose expectations are linear in the included regressors, and if the functional form is correct, RESET has no power to detect heteroskedasticity. RESET is a functional form test and nothing more.

Example

Using 88 houses in HPRICE1, two models are compared. The level model $price = \beta_0 + \beta_1 lotsize + \beta_2 sqrft + \beta_3 bdrms + u$ yields a RESET statistic of 4.67, which is $F_{2,82}$ with p-value .012: the level model is rejected. The log-log model $lprice = \beta_0 + \beta_1 llotsize + \beta_2 lsqrft + \beta_3 bdrms + u$ yields a RESET statistic of 2.56 with p-value .084, so it is not rejected at the 5% level (though it is at 10%). On the basis of RESET, the log-log model is preferred.

Analogy

RESET is like a smoke detector: it alerts you that something is burning (misspecification), but it does not tell you whether the fire is in the kitchen (needs a quadratic), the bedroom (needs a log), or the wiring (omitted variable with linear expectation—which RESET cannot detect at all).

Key Insight

RESET detects neglected nonlinearities by asking whether powers of the fitted values add explanatory power. Its limitation is equally important: it cannot detect omitted variables with linear conditional expectations, and it does not suggest the correct alternative model.

2.4 Tests against Nonnested Alternatives

Nonnested Competing Models
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u \tag{9.6} \]
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(x_1) + \beta_2 \log(x_2) + u \tag{9.7} \]
\[ y = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 x_1 + \gamma_2 x_2 + \gamma_3 \log(x_1) + \gamma_4 \log(x_2) + u \tag{9.8} \]

When the researcher must decide whether a variable enters in level or logarithmic form, the two models are nonnested: neither is a restricted version of the other, so a standard $F$ test cannot be applied. Two approaches are standard. The Mizon-Richard (1986) approach constructs a comprehensive model, equation (9.8), that contains both (9.6) and (9.7) as special cases. Testing $H_0: \gamma_3 = 0, \gamma_4 = 0$ tests model (9.6); testing $H_0: \gamma_1 = 0, \gamma_2 = 0$ tests model (9.7). The Davidson-MacKinnon (1981) approach proceeds differently: to test model (9.6), estimate model (9.7), obtain its fitted values $\check{y}$, and add $\check{y}$ to equation (9.6). The $t$ statistic on $\check{y}$ is the test statistic; a significant $t$ rejects (9.6). The roles are reversed to test (9.7).

Nonnested tests have known practical problems. First, a clear winner need not emerge: both models may be rejected, or neither may be rejected. If neither is rejected, the adjusted R-squared can be used to choose; if both are rejected, more modeling is required. Second, rejecting one model does not establish that the other is correct—the true model may be some entirely different functional form. Third, when the competing models have different dependent variables, such as $y$ versus $\log(y)$, these simple tests do not apply directly and goodness-of-fit comparisons require special care. In practice, it is wise to check whether the estimated effects of key variables differ materially across specifications; if they do not, the functional form choice is not consequential for the substantive conclusions.

Example

For a model of $y$ on $x_1, x_2$ versus $y$ on $\log(x_1), \log(x_2)$, the comprehensive model includes both sets of regressors. The joint test on the level terms and the joint test on the log terms allow separate evaluation of each specification. The Davidson-MacKinnon version requires estimating each model, saving fitted values, and testing whether the other model's fitted values are significant in the first model.

Analogy

Nonnested testing is like comparing two rival recipes for the same dish. A comprehensive tasting includes ingredients from both recipes and asks whether each set of ingredients matters. The Davidson-MacKinnon version asks a different question: once I have tasted the rival dish, does its flavor add anything to my assessment of your dish?

Key Insight

Nonnested tests can reject one model without confirming the other. They are most useful when combined with substantive judgment: if the implied marginal effects are similar across competing forms, the functional form debate is economically irrelevant.

Key Takeaways

  • Functional form misspecification occurs when the model omits nonlinear functions such as quadratics or interactions, or uses the wrong dependent variable transformation.
  • Omitting $exper^2$ biases all coefficients; the return to experience is $\beta_2 + 2\beta_3 exper$, so individual coefficients are not the marginal effects.
  • Adding quadratic terms and testing their joint significance is a simple detection method (crime example: joint F = 31.37).
  • RESET adds $\hat{y}^2$ and $\hat{y}^3$ and tests their joint significance; it detects nonlinearities but gives no repair direction.
  • Nonnested tests (comprehensive model, Davidson-MacKinnon) compare level versus log specifications, but rejection of one model does not validate the other.
  • RESET has no power against omitted variables with linear expectations and no power against heteroskedasticity when the functional form is correct.

3. Using Proxy Variables for Unobserved Explanatory Variables

A more difficult problem arises when a key variable is omitted because data on it are unavailable. In the wage equation, ability clearly affects $\log(wage)$ and is likely correlated with education. If ability is placed in the error term, OLS estimates of the return to education are biased. The researcher does not care about estimating the intercept $\beta_0$ or the coefficient on ability $\beta_3$; the goal is consistent estimation of $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ while holding ability fixed. A proxy variable is something related to the unobserved variable that the researcher would like to control for. IQ score, for example, can proxy for ability. The proxy does not have to equal the unobserved variable; it only needs to be correlated with it in the specific way described below.

3.1 The Plug-In Solution

Wage Equation with Unobserved Ability
\[ \log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + \beta_2 exper + \beta_3 abil + u \tag{9.9} \]
Model with an Unobserved Regressor
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_3^* + u \tag{9.10} \]
Proxy Variable Relationship
\[ x_3^* = \delta_0 + \delta_3 x_3 + v_3 \tag{9.11} \]

In equation (9.10), $x_3^*$ is the unobserved explanatory variable, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are observed (for example, $educ$ and $exper$), and $x_3$ is the observed proxy (for example, IQ). Equation (9.11) describes the proxy relationship: $\delta_0$ allows the proxy and the unobserved variable to be measured on different scales, $\delta_3$ measures the association between the proxy and the unobserved variable, and $v_3$ is the error from the proxy not being exactly related to $x_3^*$. If $\delta_3 = 0$, the proxy is not suitable. Typically $\delta_3 > 0$ because the proxy and the unobserved variable are positively related.

The plug-in solution is to ignore the distinction between $x_3^*$ and $x_3$ and simply run the OLS regression of $y$ on $x_1, x_2, x_3$. This seems sensible if $x_3$ is truly related to $x_3^*$, but the conditions under which it consistently estimates $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ are specific and must be checked.

3.2 Assumptions for Consistency of the Plug-In Solution

Key Assumption on the Proxy
\[ E(x_3^* | x_1, x_2, x_3) = E(x_3^* | x_3) = \delta_0 + \delta_3 x_3 \tag{9.13} \]

Two assumptions are needed. First, the error $u$ in equation (9.10) is uncorrelated with $x_1, x_2, x_3^*$, and additionally uncorrelated with the proxy $x_3$. This is essentially true by definition: $x_3^*$ directly affects $y$, while $x_3$ is irrelevant in the population model once $x_3^*$ is included. Second, the error $v_3$ in equation (9.11) is uncorrelated with $x_1, x_2$, and $x_3$. This is the "good proxy" condition. In terms of conditional expectations, it requires $E(x_3^* | x_1, x_2, x_3) = E(x_3^* | x_3) = \delta_0 + \delta_3 x_3$. The first equality is the crucial one: once $x_3$ is controlled for, the expected value of $x_3^*$ does not depend on $x_1$ or $x_2$. In the wage example, $E(abil | educ, exper, IQ) = E(abil | IQ)$: average ability changes with IQ but not with education or experience.

To see why these assumptions suffice, substitute (9.11) into (9.10). The composite error is $e = u + \beta_3 v_3$, which has zero mean and is uncorrelated with $x_1, x_2$, and $x_3$ given the two assumptions. Consequently, OLS estimation of $y$ on $x_1, x_2, x_3$ yields unbiased or consistent estimators of the parameters in the rewritten model.

3.3 What OLS Actually Estimates

The Estimated Model after Substitution
\[ y = (\beta_0 + \beta_3 \delta_0) + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 \delta_3 x_3 + u + \beta_3 v_3 \]

The equation after substitution has a new intercept $\alpha_0 = \beta_0 + \beta_3 \delta_0$ and a new slope on the proxy $\alpha_3 = \beta_3 \delta_3$. Thus the plug-in regression does not estimate $\beta_0$ or $\beta_3$. It estimates $\alpha_0, \beta_1, \beta_2$, and $\alpha_3$. The parameters of interest, $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$, are recovered consistently because they appear unchanged in the rewritten equation. The coefficient on the proxy, $\alpha_3$, is the product of the effect of the unobserved variable and the proxy-relationship slope; in the wage application, $\alpha_3$ measures the return to wage from one more IQ point, which is often more interpretable than $\beta_3$ itself.
