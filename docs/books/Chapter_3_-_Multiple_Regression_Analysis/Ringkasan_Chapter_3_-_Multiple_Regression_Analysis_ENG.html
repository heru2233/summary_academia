1. Motivation for Multiple Regression

1.1 The Model with Two Independent Variables

Interpretation

Simple regression analysis is fundamentally limited when the goal is to estimate causal or ceteris paribus effects. The core assumption SLR.4 requires that all unobserved factors affecting the dependent variable be uncorrelated with the single explanatory variable—an assumption that is rarely credible in empirical economics. For example, if we regress hourly wage on education alone, we are implicitly assuming that factors such as innate ability, family background, and labor market experience are uncorrelated with education. Given that more educated workers also tend to have different experiences and abilities, this assumption almost certainly fails, producing biased estimates of the return to education.

Multiple regression analysis solves this problem by explicitly including additional explanatory variables in the model. The simplest extension is the two-independent-variable model: wage = β₀ + β₁educ + β₂exper + u. By controlling for experience, we can isolate the effect of education on wage while holding experience constant. Similarly, in studying the effect of per-student spending on test scores, we can include average family income to control for socioeconomic background. The key insight is that multiple regression allows us to "hold fixed" other observable factors, mimicking a controlled experiment even when the data come from nonexperimental settings.

The two-variable model can be written generally as y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + u, where β₁ measures the change in y with respect to one-unit change in x₁, holding x₂ fixed. This partial effect interpretation is the central advantage of multiple regression. The zero conditional mean assumption extends naturally: E(u|x₁, x₂) = 0, meaning that, after controlling for x₁ and x₂, the average of unobserved factors is zero.

Two-Variable Population Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u \]

Example

Consider the wage equation wage = β₀ + β₁educ + β₂exper + u. In a simple regression of wage on educ, experience is relegated to the error term, and if more educated workers also tend to have more experience, the education coefficient will be biased upward. By including exper explicitly, we compare workers with the same years of experience but different education levels — a far more credible comparison. Likewise, in the education production function avgscore = β₀ + β₁expend + β₂avginc + u, per-student spending is likely correlated with family income because school districts with higher property taxes spend more. Controlling for income allows us to estimate the policy-relevant effect of spending on test scores.

Analogy

Think of a doctor trying to determine whether a new drug lowers blood pressure. A simple regression would compare patients who took the drug to those who did not, but if older patients are more likely to take the drug and also have higher blood pressure, the effect would be masked. Multiple regression is like the doctor controlling for age, weight, and diet — comparing patients of the same age and weight who differ only in whether they took the drug.

Key Insight

The central rationale for multiple regression is that it allows ceteris paribus conclusions using nonexperimental data by explicitly controlling for confounding factors. Simple regression puts these factors in the error term, where they cause bias if correlated with the regressor of interest.

Key Takeaways

  • Simple regression fails for ceteris paribus analysis when omitted factors are correlated with the included regressor.
  • Multiple regression explicitly controls for additional observable factors, yielding partial effect interpretations.
  • The zero conditional mean assumption E(u|x₁,x₂)=0 extends SLR.4 to the multiple regression setting.

1.2 The Model with k Independent Variables

Interpretation

The multiple regression framework generalizes naturally to k independent variables. The population model is y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ... + βₖxₖ + u, where β₀ is the intercept and β₁ through βₖ are slope parameters associated with each regressor. The error term u contains all unobserved factors affecting y that are not captured by the k included variables. No matter how many controls we include, some factors will always remain unobserved, and these are collectively housed in u.

The terminology is standard: y is the dependent variable, explained variable, or regressand; x₁, ..., xₖ are independent variables, explanatory variables, or regressors. The model is "linear" in the parameters, meaning it is linear in the β's, not necessarily linear in the underlying variables. For instance, log(salary) = β₀ + β₁log(sales) + β₂ceoten + β₃ceoten² + u is a multiple linear regression model even though salary is a nonlinear function of tenure. This flexibility is a major advantage of the MLR framework.

The key assumption for the general model is the zero conditional mean condition: E(u|x₁, x₂, ..., xₖ) = 0. This requires that all omitted factors are mean-independent of the included explanatory variables. It also implies that we have correctly specified the functional form — if we incorrectly model a quadratic relationship as linear, then u will be correlated with the regressors and the assumption fails. This assumption is the foundation for unbiasedness of OLS.

General Multiple Linear Regression Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \]

Example

The CEO salary model log(salary) = β₀ + β₁log(sales) + β₂ceoten + β₃ceoten² + u has k = 3 independent variables: x₁ = log(sales), x₂ = ceoten, and x₃ = ceoten². The parameter β₁ is the elasticity of salary with respect to sales, holding tenure constant. If β₃ = 0, then 100·β₂ is approximately the percentage increase in salary for an additional year of CEO tenure. More generally, the effect of tenure on salary depends on β₂, β₃, and the level of tenure.

Analogy

Imagine baking a cake. Simple regression is like trying to determine the effect of oven temperature on cake quality while ignoring baking time — but time and temperature interact. Multiple regression is like a full recipe that accounts for temperature, time, ingredient quantities, and altitude, allowing you to isolate the effect of each ingredient or setting.

Key Insight

Multiple regression accommodates nonlinear relationships among underlying variables while remaining linear in parameters. This makes the MLR model remarkably flexible for empirical economics.

Key Takeaways

  • The general MLR model has k independent variables and k+1 parameters to estimate.
  • "Linear" refers to linearity in parameters, not in variables.
  • E(u|x₁,...,xₖ)=0 is the critical assumption for causal interpretation.

2. Mechanics and Interpretation of Ordinary Least Squares

2.1 Obtaining the OLS Estimates

Interpretation

The method of ordinary least squares chooses the estimates β̂₀, β̂₁, ..., β̂ₖ to minimize the sum of squared residuals. Given n observations on y and the k independent variables, the objective function is Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ (yᵢ − β̂₀ − β̂₁xᵢ₁ − ... − β̂ₖxᵢₖ)². This is a minimization problem in k+1 unknowns, solved by taking partial derivatives and setting them to zero, producing the OLS first order conditions.

The first order conditions are a system of k+1 linear equations. The first condition states that the sum of residuals is zero; the remaining conditions state that each independent variable has zero sample correlation with the residuals. These conditions mirror the population moment conditions E(u) = 0 and E(xⱼu) = 0, making the method of moments connection clear.

For large samples and many regressors, solving these equations requires a computer. Modern econometrics software can handle thousands of observations and dozens of variables instantly. The only caveat is that the equations must have a unique solution, guaranteed by Assumption MLR.3 (no perfect collinearity).

OLS First Order Conditions
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}\right) = 0 \]
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij}\left(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}\right) = 0, \quad j = 1, \ldots, k \]

Example

In the GPA regression with k = 2 (hsGPA and ACT), the OLS first order conditions solve for three unknowns: β̂₀, β̂₁, β̂₂. The computer finds β̂₀ = 1.29, β̂₁ = 0.453, and β̂₂ = 0.0094, producing the fitted equation colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT with n = 141.

Analogy

OLS is like fitting a plane through a cloud of data points in multidimensional space. The plane is chosen so that the vertical distances from the points to the plane, squared, are as small as possible on average. The slope of the plane in each direction gives the partial effect of that variable.

Key Insight

The OLS first order conditions enforce that residuals have zero sample mean and zero correlation with each regressor. This is what makes the fitted regression line pass through the sample means and ensures the decomposition of variation into explained and unexplained parts.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals, producing k+1 first order conditions.
  • The first order conditions imply zero sample average residuals and zero correlation between residuals and each regressor.
  • Unique solutions require no perfect collinearity (Assumption MLR.3).

2.2 Interpreting the OLS Regression Equation

Interpretation

The OLS regression equation ŷ = β̂₀ + β̂₁x₁ + β̂₂x₂ + ... + β̂ₖxₖ has a clear interpretation. The intercept β̂₀ is the predicted value of y when all regressors are zero, which may or may not be economically meaningful depending on the context. The slope coefficients have partial effect interpretations: β̂₁ measures the change in predicted y for a one-unit increase in x₁, holding all other independent variables fixed.

This ceteris paribus interpretation is the hallmark of multiple regression. Unlike simple regression where the coefficient on x₁ captures both the direct effect and any spurious correlation through omitted variables, multiple regression isolates the effect by controlling for other factors. The interpretation is best understood through the change formula: Δŷ = β̂₁Δx₁ + β̂₂Δx₂ + ... + β̂ₖΔxₖ. When only x₁ changes, the predicted change in y is simply β̂₁Δx₁.

In applied work, it is standard practice to interpret coefficients with specific units in mind. In the GPA example, a one-point increase in high school GPA is associated with a predicted increase of 0.453 points in college GPA, holding ACT score fixed. In a log-wage regression, coefficients represent approximate percentage changes. Careful interpretation requires attention to the units and functional forms of the variables.

Change Formula for Multiple Regression
\[ \Delta \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 \Delta x_2 + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k \Delta x_k \]

Example

In the college GPA regression colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT, the coefficient on hsGPA says that two students with the same ACT score but differing by one point in high school GPA are predicted to differ by 0.453 points in college GPA. The coefficient on ACT is small: a 10-point increase in ACT (a very large change) raises predicted colGPA by only 0.094 points. In the wage equation log(wage) = 0.284 + 0.092educ + 0.0041exper + 0.022tenure, holding experience and tenure fixed, each additional year of education raises predicted wage by approximately 9.2%.

Analogy

Think of a chef adjusting a recipe. The coefficient on each ingredient tells you how much the final dish's quality changes when you add one more unit of that ingredient, keeping everything else constant. This is exactly what OLS does: it holds the "recipe" constant while varying one input at a time.

Key Insight

The ceteris paribus interpretation of OLS slope coefficients is what separates multiple regression from simple regression. It allows economists to answer "what is the effect of X on Y, holding everything else constant?" — even when the data were not collected experimentally.

Key Takeaways

  • Each slope coefficient measures the partial effect of its variable on y, holding all other regressors fixed.
  • The intercept is the predicted y when all x variables are zero (if meaningful).
  • Logarithmic dependent variables lead to percentage interpretations of coefficients.

2.3 OLS Fitted Values and Residuals

Interpretation

For each observation i, the fitted value ŷᵢ = β̂₀ + β̂₁xᵢ₁ + ... + β̂ₖxᵢₖ is the predicted value of y based on the OLS regression line. The residual ûᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is the difference between the actual value and the fitted value. If ûᵢ > 0, the model underpredicts y for that observation; if ûᵢ < 0, the model overpredicts. The sum of squared residuals is minimized by construction, but individual residuals can be large.

Three key algebraic properties of OLS fitted values and residuals follow from the first order conditions. First, the sample average of residuals is exactly zero, so the average fitted value equals the average of y. Second, the sample covariance between each independent variable and the residuals is zero — the regressors contain no information useful for predicting the residuals. Third, the point of sample means (x̄₁, ..., x̄ₖ, ȳ) always lies on the OLS regression line.

The zero covariance between fitted values and residuals is particularly important. It implies that the total variation in y can be decomposed into the explained variation (due to fitted values) and unexplained variation (residuals), leading directly to the definition of R².

Fitted Values and Residuals
\[ \hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} + \hat{\beta}_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik} \]
\[ \hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \]

Example

In the GPA regression, suppose a student has hsGPA = 3.5 and ACT = 24. The fitted value is colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453(3.5) + 0.0094(24) = 1.29 + 1.5855 + 0.2256 = 3.101. If the student's actual college GPA is 3.20, the residual is 3.20 − 3.101 = 0.099, meaning the model underpredicts this student's GPA by 0.099 points.

Analogy

Think of a weather forecast. The fitted value is the predicted temperature, the actual temperature is what the thermometer reads, and the residual is the forecast error. OLS chooses the forecasting rule that makes the average squared forecast error as small as possible across all days in the sample.

Key Insight

OLS residuals are not just afterthoughts — their properties (zero mean, zero correlation with regressors) are built into the estimation method. These properties drive the decomposition of variation and the construction of R².

Key Takeaways

  • The sample average of OLS residuals is always zero.
  • Each independent variable has zero sample correlation with OLS residuals.
  • The point of sample means always lies on the OLS regression line.

2.4 A "Partialling Out" Interpretation

Interpretation

A powerful way to understand multiple regression is the partialling out interpretation, formalized by the Frisch-Waugh theorem. To obtain the coefficient β̂₁ in a regression of y on x₁ and x₂, we can proceed in two steps. First, regress x₁ on x₂ and obtain residuals r̂ᵢ₁. These residuals capture the part of x₁ that is uncorrelated with x₂. Second, regress y on r̂ᵢ₁. The slope from this simple regression equals β̂₁.

This two-step procedure reveals exactly what OLS is doing: it "nettings out" or "partialling out" the effect of x₂ from x₁ before measuring the relationship between x₁ and y. In the general case with k regressors, β̂₁ is obtained by regressing x₁ on x₂, ..., xₖ, taking residuals, and then regressing y on those residuals. The residuals represent x₁ after removing the linear effects of all other regressors.

The partialling out view has both theoretical and practical implications. It clarifies that multicollinearity (correlation among regressors) does not bias OLS; it only makes it harder to precisely estimate individual coefficients because less variation remains in x₁ once other variables are netted out. It also provides a method for computing OLS coefficients in special cases where direct computation is infeasible.

Partialling Out Formula
\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{r}_{i1} y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{r}_{i1}^2} \]
\[ \text{where } \hat{r}_{i1} \text{ are residuals from regressing } x_1 \text{ on } x_2, \ldots, x_k \]

Example

In the wage regression with educ and exper, to find the coefficient on educ, we first regress educ on exper and obtain residuals. These residuals represent the portion of education that is not explained by work experience. Then we regress log(wage) on these residuals. The slope from this second regression equals the OLS coefficient on educ (0.092 in the full model). This demonstrates that the education coefficient measures the wage difference associated with education after removing the linear influence of experience.

Analogy

Think of trying to measure the effect of studying time on exam scores while controlling for prior ability. First, you "remove" the portion of study time that is predictable from ability, leaving only the unpredictable part. Then you see whether that unpredictable variation in study time correlates with exam scores. This is exactly what partialling out does.

Key Insight

The partialling out interpretation shows that β̂₁ measures the effect of x₁ on y after removing the linear influence of all other regressors from x₁. This is why OLS coefficients are called partial effects — they represent the unique contribution of each variable after accounting for the others.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS coefficients can be obtained by a two-step regression procedure: regress x₁ on all other xs, take residuals, regress y on those residuals.
  • This is the Frisch-Waugh theorem, a cornerstone result in econometrics.
  • Correlation among regressors reduces precision but does not bias OLS.

2.5 Comparison of Simple and Multiple Regression Estimates

Interpretation

An important question is: when does simple regression of y on x₁ give the same coefficient as multiple regression of y on x₁ and x₂? The relationship between the two estimates is β̃₁ = β̂₁ + β̂₂·δ̃₁, where β̃₁ is the simple regression slope, β̂₁ and β̂₂ are the multiple regression slopes, and δ̃₁ is the slope from regressing x₂ on x₁.

This decomposition is extremely revealing. The simple regression coefficient equals the multiple regression coefficient plus an "omitted variable bias" term: the partial effect of x₂ on y times the slope from regressing x₂ on x₁. If β̂₂ = 0, then x₂ has no partial effect on y, and both regressions give the same coefficient. If δ̃₁ = 0, then x₁ and x₂ are uncorrelated in the sample, and again the coefficients coincide. In practice, neither condition usually holds exactly, so simple and multiple regression estimates differ, sometimes substantially.

The formula also provides intuition for when the difference will be large: when x₂ is strongly correlated with x₁ (large δ̃₁) and has a substantial partial effect on y (large β̂₂). Conversely, if either the correlation or the partial effect is small, the simple and multiple estimates will be similar.

Relationship Between Simple and Multiple Regression
\[ \tilde{\beta}_1 = \hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 \tilde{\delta}_1 \]
\[ \text{where } \tilde{\delta}_1 \text{ is the slope from } x_2 \text{ on } x_1 \]

Example

In the 401(k) participation example, the multiple regression prate = 80.12 + 5.52mrate + 0.243age gives a coefficient of 5.52 on mrate. The simple regression of prate on mrate gives prate = 83.08 + 5.86mrate, a coefficient of 5.86 — about 6.2% larger. The difference is modest because the sample correlation between mrate and age is only 0.12, and the partial effect of age is small (0.243). In the college GPA example, the simple regression of colGPA on hsGPA yields 0.482, while the multiple regression coefficient is 0.453 — close but not identical, since ACT is correlated with hsGPA (correlation ≈ 0.346) and has a small but nonzero effect.

Analogy

Think about trying to measure the effect of exercise on weight loss. A simple regression might suggest that exercise has a small effect, but if people who exercise also eat healthier (healthy diet is correlated with exercise), the simple regression confounds diet and exercise. Multiple regression separates the two, potentially showing a larger or smaller true effect of exercise after holding diet constant.

Key Insight

The difference between simple and multiple regression estimates is exactly the bias from omitting x₂: β̂₂·δ̃₁. This formula is the foundation for understanding omitted variable bias in empirical work.

Key Takeaways

  • Simple and multiple regression estimates coincide only if the omitted variable has zero partial effect or is uncorrelated with the included regressor.
  • The bias term is the product of the partial effect of the omitted variable and the slope from regressing the omitted variable on the included one.
  • Small correlations or small partial effects produce similar estimates across specifications.

2.6 Goodness-of-Fit

Interpretation

Goodness-of-fit measures how well the OLS regression line explains the variation in the dependent variable. The total sum of squares (SST) measures the total sample variation in y around its mean. The explained sum of squares (SSE) measures the variation in the fitted values around the mean of y. The sum of squared residuals (SSR) measures the unexplained variation. A fundamental identity is SST = SSE + SSR, meaning total variation = explained variation + unexplained variation.

R² is defined as SSE/SST = 1 − SSR/SST. It represents the proportion of the sample variation in y explained by the regression. R² always lies between 0 and 1. Equivalently, R² equals the squared correlation coefficient between the actual values yᵢ and the fitted values ŷᵢ. An R² of 0.176 means the model explains 17.6% of the variation in y.

A critical algebraic property: R² never decreases when an additional regressor is added to the model, and it usually increases. This follows because SSR never increases when more variables are included — the regression can always set the new coefficient to zero, reproducing the previous fit. For this reason, R² is a poor criterion for model selection; the relevant question is whether the added variable has a nonzero partial effect in the population. R² also assumes the same set of observations; with different sample sizes, comparisons are not valid.

SST, SSE, SSR, and R²
\[ SST = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \]
\[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \]
\[ SSR = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{u}_i^2 \]
\[ SST = SSE + SSR \]
\[ R^2 = \frac{SSE}{SST} = 1 - \frac{SSR}{SST} \]

Example

The GPA regression colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT has R² = 0.176, meaning that high school GPA and ACT score together explain about 17.6% of the variation in college GPA. The arrest model narr86 = 0.712 − 0.150pcnv − 0.034ptime86 − 0.104qemp86 has R² = 0.0413; adding avgsen raises it to 0.0422 — a tiny increase. This small increase suggests that average sentence length adds almost nothing to the explanatory power of the model, consistent with its small and statistically insignificant coefficient.

Analogy

R² is like the R² in a sports prediction context: if you predicted basketball game outcomes using only team scoring averages, an R² of 0.30 would mean your model explains 30% of the variation in game outcomes. The remaining 70% is due to game-specific factors, luck, and unmeasured variables.

Key Insight

R² never decreases when variables are added, making it an unreliable tool for deciding which variables belong in a model. What matters is whether the added variable has a nonzero partial effect on y in the population, which requires statistical inference.

Key Takeaways

  • SST = SSE + SSR is the fundamental decomposition of variation.
  • R² measures the proportion of variation in y explained by the model.
  • R² never decreases when regressors are added (with the same sample), so it is not a good model selection criterion.

2.7 Regression through the Origin

Interpretation

Occasionally, economic theory suggests that the intercept β₀ should be zero. In such cases, we estimate a regression through the origin: ŷ̃ = β̃₁x₁ + ... + β̃ₖxₖ. The OLS estimates minimize the sum of squared residuals subject to the constraint that the intercept is zero. This is a simple modification, but it has important consequences.

Regression through the origin loses several properties of standard OLS. Most notably, the residuals no longer have a zero sample average, and the sample covariance between residuals and independent variables is no longer zero. The identity SST = SSE + SSR may fail, and R² defined as 1 − SSR/SST can be negative. Some researchers prefer to compute R² as the squared correlation between actual and fitted values, but no universally accepted convention exists.

The more serious issue is bias: if the true population intercept is nonzero, imposing a zero intercept biases the slope estimates. Conversely, estimating an intercept when β₀ is truly zero inflates the variance of the slope estimators. This trade-off mirrors the general bias-variance trade-off in econometrics: imposing valid restrictions improves efficiency, but imposing invalid ones causes bias.

Regression through the Origin
\[ \tilde{y} = \tilde{\beta}_1 x_1 + \tilde{\beta}_2 x_2 + \cdots + \tilde{\beta}_k x_k \]

Example

Suppose a financial model posits that expected returns are proportional to systematic risk, with no intercept (the capital asset pricing model). A regression of stock returns on market returns through the origin forces the predicted return to be zero when the market return is zero. If in reality a risk-free asset yields a positive return (nonzero intercept), then the estimated beta will be biased.

Analogy

Forcing a regression through the origin is like forcing a line to pass through the origin on a scatter plot when the data clearly cluster around a line with a positive intercept. The imposed constraint twists the fitted line, potentially making it steeper or flatter than the unconstrained best fit.

Key Insight

Regression through the origin is risky: if the true intercept is nonzero, slope estimates are biased. Unless theory strongly justifies a zero intercept, it is safer to include the intercept.

Key Takeaways

  • Regression through the origin suppresses the intercept, changing the properties of OLS residuals and R².
  • R² can be negative when the intercept is omitted and SST is used as the baseline.
  • Estimating a nonzero intercept when β₀ is truly zero only increases variance; the stronger concern is excluding a nonzero intercept.

3. Expected Value of the OLS Estimators

3.1 Assumptions MLR.1 through MLR.4

Interpretation

The statistical properties of OLS are derived under a set of ideal conditions known as the Gauss-Markov assumptions. Assumption MLR.1 states that the population model is linear in parameters: y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + βₖxₖ + u. This is not restrictive in practice because we can include nonlinear functions of underlying variables. MLR.2 requires a random sample of n observations following the population model, ensuring that our sample is representative and independent observations are drawn from the same data-generating process.

Assumption MLR.3—no perfect collinearity—is crucial for unique OLS estimates. It rules out cases where one independent variable is an exact linear combination of others. Common violations include including the same variable in different units (income in dollars and thousands), using log(inc²) when log(inc) is already included (since log(inc²) = 2·log(inc)), or including total spending along with its two components (totexpend = expendA + expendB by definition). Perfect collinearity makes the OLS equations indeterminate and parameter estimates undefined.

Assumption MLR.4—zero conditional mean—states E(u|x₁, ..., xₖ) = 0. This is the most important assumption for unbiasedness. It means that, after controlling for all included variables, the average unobserved factor is zero. This fails when there are omitted variables correlated with included regressors, when there is measurement error in the independent variables, or when the functional form is misspecified.

Key Assumptions for Unbiasedness
\[ \text{MLR.1: } y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \]
\[ \text{MLR.2: Random sample of } n \text{ observations} \]
\[ \text{MLR.3: No perfect collinearity} \]
\[ \text{MLR.4: } E(u|x_1, \ldots, x_k) = 0 \]

Example

Consider the model voteA = β₀ + β₁expendA + β₂expendB + β₃totexpend + u, where totexpend = expendA + expendB by definition. This violates MLR.3 because x₃ is an exact linear combination of x₁ and x₂. Similarly, including both inc and inc² years apart is fine because they're not perfectly collinear; but including log(inc) and log(inc²) violates MLR.3 since log(inc²) = 2·log(inc).

Analogy

Think of perfect collinearity as trying to solve a puzzle with redundant pieces. If one piece is just a mirror image of another, you don't have independent information. Assumption MLR.3 ensures each regressor contributes unique information. Assumption MLR.4 is like ensuring the puzzle wasn't assembled with pieces from a different box — no systematic gaps remain.

Key Insight

MLR.4 is the assumption that makes OLS unbiased; MLR.3 ensures estimates exist; MLR.1 and MLR.2 define the framework. Violating MLR.3 means estimation is impossible; violating MLR.4 means estimation is possible but biased.

Key Takeaways

  • MLR.1-MLR.4 are the four assumptions required for OLS unbiasedness.
  • MLR.3 rules out perfect collinearity, not mere correlation among regressors.
  • MLR.4 (zero conditional mean) is the assumption most often violated in practice through omitted variables.

3.2 Theorem 3.1: Unbiasedness of OLS

Interpretation

Under Assumptions MLR.1 through MLR.4, the OLS estimators are unbiased: E(β̂ⱼ) = βⱼ for all j = 0, 1, ..., k. Unbiasedness means that, across repeated random sampling, the average of the OLS estimates equals the true population parameter. This does not guarantee that any particular sample estimate equals the true value; rather, the sampling distribution of the estimator is centered on the true value.

The proof of unbiasedness relies on the expected value of β̂₁ conditional on the sample values of the independent variables. Substituting the true population model into the partialling out formula, we show that β̂₁ = β₁ + Σᵢ(wᵢuᵢ)/Σᵢ(wᵢ²), where wᵢ are the residuals from regressing x₁ on the other regressors. Taking the expectation conditional on X and using E(u|X) = 0 (from MLR.4) gives E(β̂₁) = β₁. The key step is that MLR.4 implies E(uᵢ|xᵢ₁, ..., xᵢₖ) = 0 for each observation i.

Unbiasedness is a finite-sample property — it holds for any sample size n, not just asymptotically. This distinguishes OLS from some alternative estimators that are only consistent (unbiased as n → ∞). The practical implication is that, if the assumptions hold, OLS has no systematic tendency to over- or underestimate the true parameters, regardless of sample size.

Theorem 3.1: Unbiasedness of OLS
\[ \text{Under MLR.1–MLR.4: } \quad E(\hat{\beta}_j) = \beta_j, \quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, k \]

Example

If we repeatedly draw random samples of 526 workers from the population and estimate the wage equation, the average of the education coefficients across all samples would approach 0.092. But in any single sample, the estimate might be higher or lower than 0.092. Unbiasedness says there is no systematic bias — the sampling distribution centers on the true return to education.

Analogy

Think of a target with a bullseye. An unbiased estimator is like a skilled archer whose shots scatter around the bullseye with no systematic pattern — individual shots miss left or right, but the average shot is dead center. A biased estimator is like using a misaligned bow sight: the shots cluster tightly but off-center.

Key Insight

Unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution, not of any particular estimate. It requires MLR.4, the zero conditional mean assumption, which is satisfied only if all relevant omitted variables are uncorrelated with the included regressors.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS is unbiased under MLR.1-MLR.4, regardless of sample size.
  • Unbiasedness means the sampling distribution is centered on the true parameter.
  • MLR.4 is the critical assumption; without it, OLS is generally biased.

3.3 Omitted Variable Bias

Interpretation

Omitted variable bias is the most common reason why MLR.4 fails. Suppose the true population model includes both x₁ and x₂: y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + u. If we mistakenly estimate the simple regression of y on x₁ alone, we are effectively putting x₂ into the error term. The omitted variable bias in the simple regression coefficient is given by E(β̃₁) = β₁ + β₂·δ₁, where δ₁ is the slope from the population regression of x₂ on x₁.

The bias has an intuitive formula: it is the product of the partial effect of the omitted variable on y (β₂) and the relationship between the omitted variable and the included regressor (δ₁). The sign of the bias depends on both factors. If β₂ and δ₁ have the same sign, the bias is positive; if they have opposite signs, the bias is negative. If either β₂ = 0 or δ₁ = 0, there is no bias.

It is important to distinguish this bias from mere confounding. Omitted variable bias is a systematic distortion of the OLS estimator, not random noise. Even with a very large sample, the bias persists. The only remedies are to include the omitted variable in the regression (if data are available) or to use alternative methods like instrumental variables (discussed in later chapters).

Omitted Variable Bias Formula
\[ E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \delta_1 \]
\[ \text{Bias} = \beta_2 \delta_1 \]
\[ \text{where } \delta_1 = \frac{Cov(x_1, x_2)}{Var(x_1)} \]

Example

Consider the wage model where true equation includes both educ and abil (ability): wage = β₀ + β₁educ + β₂abil + u. If ability is omitted, the bias in the education coefficient is β₂·δ₁. Since ability likely has a positive effect on wage (β₂ > 0) and ability is positively correlated with education (δ₁ > 0), the bias is positive. Thus, simple regression overstates the return to education. In the well-known case of education and ability, this is exactly why researchers worry that simple OLS estimates of education returns are too high.

Analogy

Imagine trying to measure the effect of umbrella use on getting wet. People who use umbrellas also tend to carry them on rainy days. Rain is the "omitted variable" — it is correlated with umbrella use and causally affects getting wet. Without controlling for rain, umbrella use appears to cause getting wet. Multiple regression with a rain control would correctly show the protective effect.

Key Insight

The sign and magnitude of omitted variable bias are determined by the partial effect of the omitted variable (β₂) and its correlation with the included regressor (δ₁). This formula is the most important diagnostic tool for assessing whether reported regression results are credible.

Key Takeaways

  • Omitted variable bias equals β₂·δ₁, where β₂ is the partial effect of the omitted variable and δ₁ is the slope from regressing the omitted variable on the included one.
  • The bias does not shrink as the sample size grows — it is a systematic distortion.
  • Signing the bias requires knowing the signs of β₂ and δ₁.

3.4 The General Omitted Variable Problem

Interpretation

When more than one relevant variable is omitted, the bias formula generalizes. Suppose the true model has k regressors but we estimate a model with only a subset. The OLS estimators of the included variables will generally be biased, with the bias depending on the partial effects of all omitted variables and their relationships with the included regressors after netting out the other included variables.

In the case with two included variables and one omitted variable, the bias in the coefficient on x₁ is β₂·δ₁ (where δ₁ is the slope from regressing the omitted variable on x₁, holding x₂ fixed). This is the "partial" correlation between the omitted variable and x₁ after controlling for x₂, multiplied by the partial effect of the omitted variable. This generalization underscores that the sign of bias cannot always be easily predicted when multiple omitted variables exist, since their combined effects may pull in different directions.

The general omitted variable problem is particularly relevant when assessing the validity of empirical studies. A common strategy is to include a rich set of controls and then examine how coefficients of interest change as controls are added. If coefficients are stable across specifications that include plausible confounders, researchers gain confidence in their estimates. However, this approach is heuristic — the true test requires satisfying MLR.4, which can never be verified with data alone.

Bias with Multiple Omitted Variables
\[ E(\tilde{\beta}_j) = \beta_j + \sum_{q \in \Omega} \beta_q \delta_{qj} \]
\[ \text{where } \Omega \text{ is the set of omitted variables, } \delta_{qj} \text{ is the slope of } x_q \text{ on } x_j \]

Example

In the student performance model, suppose the true model includes expenditure, average income, and school quality (e.g., teacher-student ratio). If we estimate a model with only expenditure and income, school quality is omitted. The bias in the expenditure coefficient depends on whether school quality is correlated with expenditure after controlling for income (δ₁) and whether school quality has a nonzero partial effect on test scores (β₃). If richer school districts have both higher spending and better teachers, the bias is positive.

Analogy

Think of trying to measure the health benefit of a new diet. If you only control for age but not exercise habits, the diet effect might be contaminated if people on the diet also exercise more. Adding exercise as a control removes one source of bias, but other factors—sleep, stress, genetics—might still be omitted.

Key Insight

With multiple omitted variables, the bias in each coefficient is the sum of terms, each being the partial effect of the omitted variable times its partial correlation with the included regressor. This makes signing the bias much harder in practice.

Key Takeaways

  • The omitted variable bias formula generalizes to multiple omitted variables using partial correlations.
  • Adding controls to a regression can reduce omitted variable bias but may also introduce other complications.
  • Stability of coefficients across specifications is suggestive, not definitive, evidence of no omitted variable bias.

3.5 The Assumption of No Perfect Collinearity Revisited

Interpretation

Perfect collinearity arises when one independent variable is an exact linear function of others. The most common examples include: (1) including a variable and a constant multiple of it (e.g., income in dollars and income in thousands); (2) including log(inc) and log(inc²) — since log(inc²) = 2·log(inc); and (3) including total spending and its component parts. In all such cases, the OLS first order conditions have infinitely many solutions, and no unique estimates exist.

It is crucial to distinguish perfect collinearity from high (but not perfect) correlation. High multicollinearity means individual coefficients are estimated imprecisely (large standard errors), but the OLS estimates still exist and are unbiased. Perfect collinearity, by contrast, is a logical violation making estimation impossible.

Examples of Perfect Collinearity
\[ x_3 = x_1 + x_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{totexpend} = \text{expendA} + \text{expendB} \]
\[ x_2 = 2x_1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \log(inc^2) = 2\log(inc) \]

Example

In a campaign spending model, including expendA (spending by candidate A), expendB (spending by candidate B), and totexpend = expendA + expendB as regressors creates perfect collinearity. The model cannot be estimated. The remedy is to drop one of the three variables, depending on the economic question of interest. Similarly, including both annual income and monthly income for the same household violates MLR.3.

Analogy

Perfect collinearity is like trying to solve two equations that are actually the same equation written twice — there is no unique solution. High multicollinearity is like nearly parallel lines in a system of equations: the intersection point exists but is very sensitive to small perturbations.

Key Insight

Perfect collinearity prevents estimation, whereas high correlation merely reduces precision. Never include redundant variables that are exact linear combinations of others.

Key Takeaways

  • Perfect collinearity: one regressor is an exact linear combination of others — no unique OLS estimates.
  • High but imperfect correlation is allowed under MLR.3.
  • Transformations like log(inc²) can inadvertently create perfect collinearity with log(inc).

3.6 Practical Implications and Summary of Section 3-3

Interpretation

The unbiasedness of OLS is a foundational result, but it rests on assumptions that are often violated in practice. The primary threat is omitted variable bias, which occurs when MLR.4 fails. The good news is that we can quantify the direction of the bias when we identify the omitted variable and its correlations. The bad news is that, in many cases, we do not know the full set of relevant omitted factors, so we cannot be certain that MLR.4 holds.

Researchers adopt several strategies to mitigate omitted variable bias. The most straightforward is to include as many relevant controls as data permit. Another is to use panel data methods or instrumental variables, which we study in later chapters. A third is to perform sensitivity analysis — examining how coefficients change when plausible omitted variables are added. The bias formula tells us what to expect: if adding a control changes the coefficient substantially, omitted variable bias is likely present.

The key takeaway from Section 3-3 is that OLS is only as good as the assumption E(u|x) = 0. Estimation itself is mechanical and always works (given no perfect collinearity), but ­interpretation as causal effects requires judgment about whether MLR.4 is plausible. The econometrician's craft lies in collecting enough data, choosing appropriate functional forms, and justifying the zero conditional mean assumption convincingly.

Summary of Unbiasedness Conditions
\[ \text{OLS unbiased } \iff \text{ MLR.1 + MLR.2 + MLR.3 + MLR.4} \]

Example

In the wage equation with education and experience, if we suspect ability is an omitted variable, we can ask: does the bias formula help? Ability is positively correlated with education (δ₁ > 0) and has a positive effect on wage (β₂ > 0), so the bias is positive. The simple regression of log(wage) on education overstates the true return to education. This logic guides researchers to control for ability proxies (IQ scores, test scores, or family background) to reduce the bias.

Analogy

Think of estimating the effect of fertilizer on crop yield. If farmers who use more fertilizer also have better soil quality (unmeasured), naive estimates overstate the fertilizer effect. The bias formula warns us to expect an upward bias if soil quality positively correlates with fertilizer use and positively affects yield.

Key Insight

Unbiasedness is a property of the estimation procedure, not of a particular estimate. No statistical test can prove MLR.4 holds; convincing omitted variable arguments come from economic reasoning, data collection, and robustness analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • The unbiasedness of OLS requires all four MLR assumptions; MLR.4 is the one most often violated.
  • Omitted variable bias persists even in large samples — it is a systematic error.
  • Economic reasoning about omitted variables is necessary for credible causal inference.
