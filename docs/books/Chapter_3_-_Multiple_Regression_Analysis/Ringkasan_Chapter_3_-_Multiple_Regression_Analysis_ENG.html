1. Introduction to Multiple Regression Analysis

Multiple regression analysis is a statistical method that extends simple regression to allow for multiple explanatory variables to affect a dependent variable simultaneously. Its primary advantage over simple regression is that it enables ceteris paribus (all else equal) analysis by explicitly controlling for other factors that influence the dependent variable. In simple regression, the key assumption that all other factors are uncorrelated with the independent variable is often unrealistic, making causal inference difficult. Multiple regression directly addresses this limitation by including those other factors as regressors.

The method is widely used in economics and social sciences for testing theories and evaluating policy effects using nonexperimental data. By incorporating multiple explanatory variables that may be correlated with one another, researchers can hope to infer causality where simple regression would be misleading. Additionally, multiple regression can explain more variation in the dependent variable and accommodate flexible functional forms, such as quadratics and logarithms, within a linear-in-parameters framework.

Example

Consider a wage equation: wage = β0 + β1educ + β2exper + u. By including experience explicitly, we can estimate the effect of education on wage while holding experience fixed. In simple regression, experience would be in the error term, and we would need to assume that experience is uncorrelated with education — a tenuous assumption.

Analogy

Simple regression is like trying to isolate the effect of fertilizer on crop yield without controlling for rainfall, soil quality, and sunlight. Multiple regression is like a controlled experiment where we measure and adjust for these other factors simultaneously, even though we did not physically control them in the data collection.

Key Insight

Multiple regression provides a way to mimic controlled laboratory conditions using nonexperimental data. By including observable confounding factors directly in the model, we can isolate the partial effect of each variable, though causality still requires the zero conditional mean assumption to hold.

Key Takeaways

  • Multiple regression enables ceteris paribus analysis by controlling for multiple factors simultaneously.
  • It is essential for causal inference when using nonexperimental data.
  • It allows for flexible functional forms while remaining linear in parameters.
  • More variation in the dependent variable can be explained by including additional relevant factors.

2. The Multiple Regression Model

2.1 The Model with Two Independent Variables

The multiple regression model with two independent variables is written as:

Two-Variable Multiple Regression Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u \]

Here, y is the dependent variable (explained variable, response variable, regressand). x1 and x2 are the independent variables (explanatory variables, control variables, predictors, regressors). β0 is the intercept parameter. β1 measures the change in y with respect to a one-unit change in x1, holding x2 fixed (the partial effect or ceteris paribus effect of x1 on y). β2 measures the change in y with respect to a one-unit change in x2, holding x1 fixed. The term u is the error term or disturbance, containing all unobserved factors that affect y.

The key assumption for the model is the zero conditional mean condition:

Zero Conditional Mean Assumption (Two Variables)
\[ E(u \mid x_1, x_2) = 0 \]

This means that for any values of x1 and x2 in the population, the average of the unobserved factors is zero. The important part is that the expected value of u is the same for all combinations of x1 and x2 — that this common value is zero is not restrictive as long as the intercept is included. In the wage example, this implies that other factors affecting wage (such as innate ability) are not related on average to education and experience.

Practical use: This assumption is required for the OLS estimators to be unbiased. When assessing whether OLS will produce unbiased estimates, researchers must ask whether average ability levels are the same across all combinations of education and experience in the population.

Example

For the model avgscore = β0 + β1expend + β2avginc + u, the zero conditional mean assumption requires that other factors affecting test scores are, on average, unrelated to per-student spending and average family income. This is likely to be violated if, for example, unobserved school quality is correlated with spending.

Key Insight

The zero conditional mean assumption is the core requirement for causal interpretation of OLS estimates. If it fails, the OLS estimators are biased, and the estimated coefficients do not represent true causal effects. This assumption is stronger than simply requiring that u is uncorrelated with each x — it requires that the conditional mean of u is zero for all values of the regressors.

2.2 The Model with k Independent Variables

The general multiple linear regression (MLR) model extends the two-variable case to include k independent variables. It is written as:

General Multiple Linear Regression Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_3 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \]

β0 is the intercept. β1, β2, …, βk are the slope parameters (though they may not be literal slopes when nonlinear functions of variables are used). The model contains k + 1 unknown population parameters. The error term u contains all factors other than x1, x2, …, xk that affect y.

The key assumption for the general model is:

Zero Conditional Mean Assumption (General)
\[ E(u \mid x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k) = 0 \]

This requires that all factors in the unobserved error term be uncorrelated with the explanatory variables, and that the functional relationships between the explained and explanatory variables have been correctly specified. Any problem that causes u to be correlated with any independent variable causes this assumption to fail. The "linear" in "multiple linear regression" refers to linearity in the parameters βj, not necessarily linearity in the variables themselves. For example, log(salary) = β0 + β1log(sales) + β2ceoten + β3ceoten2 + u is a linear model in the parameters, even though it contains logarithms and a quadratic.

Interpretation of β1 in a log-log model: β1 is the ceteris paribus elasticity of y with respect to x1. For example, if β1 = 0.092, a 1% increase in x1 is associated with a 0.092% increase in y.

Example

Consider the CEO salary model: log(salary) = β0 + β1log(sales) + β2ceoten + β3ceoten2 + u. Here, y = log(salary), x1 = log(sales), x2 = ceoten, and x3 = ceoten2. This model has k = 3 independent variables. β1 is the elasticity of salary with respect to sales. If β3 = 0, then 100β2 is approximately the percentage increase in salary when tenure increases by one year. If β3 ≠ 0, the effect of tenure on salary is more complex and depends on the level of tenure.

Analogy

Think of the multiple regression model as a recipe. The dependent variable y is the final dish. The independent variables x1, x2, …, xk are the ingredients. The coefficients β1, β2, …, βk tell us how much each ingredient contributes to the final dish, assuming we hold all other ingredients constant. The error term u represents the cook's skill, kitchen conditions, and other unmeasured factors that affect the outcome.

Key Insight

The term "linear" in multiple linear regression refers to linearity in the parameters, not in the variables. This allows for great flexibility through transformations like logarithms, squares, and interactions, while keeping the estimation procedure simple and unified. The zero conditional mean assumption is the linchpin for unbiased estimation — if it fails, OLS estimates are biased and cannot be interpreted as causal effects.

Key Takeaways

  • The general MLR model is y = β0 + β1x1 + … + βkxk + u, with k + 1 parameters.
  • The zero conditional mean assumption E(u | x1, …, xk) = 0 is required for unbiasedness.
  • The model is linear in parameters but can capture nonlinear relationships through variable transformations.
  • Each coefficient βj measures the partial effect of xj on y, holding all other variables fixed.

3. Mechanics and Interpretation of Ordinary Least Squares

3.1 Obtaining the OLS Estimates

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is the method used to estimate the parameters of the multiple regression model. The OLS estimates are chosen to minimize the sum of squared residuals. For a model with two independent variables and n observations, the estimated OLS equation is:

OLS Regression Line (Two Variables)
\[ \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 x_2 \]

where \(\hat{\beta}_0\) is the estimate of β0 (the intercept), \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is the estimate of β1 (the slope on x1), and \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is the estimate of β2 (the slope on x2). The estimates are chosen to minimize:

Sum of Squared Residuals (Minimization Objective)
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \hat{\beta}_2 x_{i2})^2 \]

Here, i indexes the observation number (from 1 to n). The second subscript on x distinguishes between different independent variables: xi1 is the value of the first independent variable for observation i, and xi2 is the value of the second independent variable for observation i. In the wage example, xi1 = educi (education of person i) and xi2 = experi (experience of person i).

For the general case with k independent variables, the OLS estimates are chosen to minimize:

General OLS Minimization Problem
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik})^2 \]

This minimization problem yields k + 1 linear equations in k + 1 unknowns, called the OLS first-order conditions:

OLS First-Order Conditions
\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}) &= 0 \\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i1} (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}) &= 0 \\ &\vdots \\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ik} (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \cdots - \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik}) &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

These equations are the sample counterparts of the population moment conditions E(u) = 0 and E(xju) = 0 for j = 1, …, k. The first condition says that the sum of residuals is zero, and the remaining conditions say that each independent variable has zero sample covariance with the residuals. For these equations to have a unique solution, we need Assumption MLR.3 (no perfect collinearity).

Practical use: In practice, these equations are solved by computer software. The researcher does not compute them by hand. However, understanding the first-order conditions is crucial for understanding the properties of OLS and for diagnosing problems.

Example

In the GPA example with colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT (n = 141), the estimates were obtained by solving the three first-order conditions (one for the intercept, one for hsGPA, and one for ACT). The intercept 1.29 is the predicted college GPA when both hsGPA and ACT are zero. The coefficient 0.453 on hsGPA means that holding ACT fixed, a one-point increase in high school GPA is associated with a 0.453-point increase in predicted college GPA.

Key Insight

The OLS first-order conditions are derived from the method of moments. Under the zero conditional mean assumption, the population moments E(u) = 0 and E(xju) = 0 hold. The OLS estimator replaces these population moments with their sample analogs, yielding a system of equations that can be solved uniquely (provided no perfect collinearity exists).

3.2 Interpreting the OLS Regression Equation

The OLS regression line is interpreted as a prediction equation. The intercept \(\hat{\beta}_0\) is the predicted value of y when all independent variables are set to zero. The slope coefficients have ceteris paribus (partial effect) interpretations: \(\hat{\beta}_1\) measures the change in the predicted y for a one-unit increase in x1, holding all other independent variables fixed. This is the key advantage of multiple regression: it provides estimates of partial effects even when the data were not collected in a controlled experimental setting.

From the estimated equation, the predicted change in y given changes in the independent variables is:

Predicted Change in y
\[ \Delta \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 \Delta x_2 + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k \Delta x_k \]

When only x1 changes (\(\Delta x_2 = \cdots = \Delta x_k = 0\)), we have \(\Delta \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x_1\). The intercept does not affect changes in y — it only affects the level. This decomposition allows us to isolate the effect of each variable while controlling for others.

Practical use: In Example 3.2, the estimated log-wage equation is log(wage) = 0.284 + 0.092educ + 0.0041exper + 0.022tenure. The coefficient 0.092 means that, holding experience and tenure fixed, another year of education is predicted to increase log(wage) by 0.092, which translates to approximately a 9.2% increase in wage. This is the estimated ceteris paribus return to education.

Example 3.1: Determinants of College GPA

Estimated equation: colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT, n = 141, R2 = 0.176.

Interpretation: Holding ACT fixed, a one-point increase in high school GPA is associated with a 0.453-point increase in predicted college GPA. The coefficient on ACT is small: a 10-point increase in ACT (a very large change) is associated with only a 0.094-point increase in college GPA, holding hsGPA fixed. This suggests that once high school GPA is accounted for, ACT score is not a strong predictor of college GPA.

Comparison with simple regression: Simple regression of colGPA on ACT alone gives colGPA = 2.40 + 0.0271ACT, where the coefficient on ACT is almost three times larger. This difference arises because the simple regression does not control for hsGPA, which is correlated with both ACT and colGPA.

Key Insight

Simple regression coefficients can be misleading when relevant variables are omitted. The coefficient on ACT in the simple regression is larger because it picks up the effect of hsGPA (which is positively correlated with both ACT and colGPA). Multiple regression removes this confounding by including hsGPA directly in the model. This is why multiple regression is essential for credible causal inference.

3.3 The Meaning of "Holding Other Factors Fixed"

The partial effect interpretation of slope coefficients in multiple regression can cause confusion. The key point is that multiple regression provides ceteris paribus interpretations even though the data were not collected in a ceteris paribus fashion. In Example 3.1, the coefficient on ACT measures the predicted difference in college GPA for two students with the same high school GPA but different ACT scores. This interpretation is valid even though the sample contains students with varying high school GPAs and ACT scores — we did not restrict the sample to students with identical high school GPAs.

The power of multiple regression is that it allows us to do in nonexperimental environments what natural scientists do in a controlled laboratory: keep other factors fixed. The method effectively "partials out" the effects of the control variables from the variable of interest, allowing us to estimate the partial effect.

Analogy

Imagine you want to know how a new teaching method affects test scores. In a lab experiment, you would assign students with the same background to treatment and control groups. In the real world, students differ. Multiple regression is like a statistical matching machine: it compares students who are similar on observed characteristics (like prior GPA) but differ on the variable of interest (like teaching method), effectively creating virtual control groups.

Key Insight

Multiple regression does not require that we actually observe individuals with identical values of the control variables. The method mathematically adjusts for differences in the control variables, providing estimates of what the relationship would be if those variables were held constant. This is the source of its power and also the source of its assumptions — the adjustment is only valid if the model is correctly specified.

3.4 Changing More Than One Independent Variable Simultaneously

Sometimes, we want to change more than one independent variable at the same time to find the resulting effect on the dependent variable. This is straightforward using the predicted change formula. For example, in the wage equation (3.19), if an individual stays at the same firm for another year, both experience and tenure increase by one year. The total estimated effect on log(wage), holding education fixed, is:

Total Effect of Simultaneous Changes
\[ \Delta \widehat{\log(wage)} = 0.0041 \times \Delta exper + 0.022 \times \Delta tenure = 0.0041 + 0.022 = 0.0261 \]

This corresponds to approximately a 2.6% increase in wage. The total effect is simply the sum of the individual coefficients multiplied by their respective changes. This additive property holds because the model is linear in parameters.

Practical use: This approach is useful for scenarios where multiple variables naturally change together, such as when a worker gains a year of experience and tenure simultaneously, or when a policy intervention affects multiple inputs at once.

Key Insight

When multiple variables change simultaneously, the total predicted effect is the sum of the individual partial effects weighted by the respective changes. This linear combination property is a direct consequence of the linear-in-parameters structure of the model. It allows researchers to compute the net effect of complex, multi-dimensional changes.

3.5 OLS Fitted Values and Residuals

After obtaining the OLS regression line, we can compute a fitted value (predicted value) for each observation by plugging the values of the independent variables into the estimated equation:

Fitted Value for Observation i
\[ \hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} + \hat{\beta}_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k x_{ik} \]

The residual for observation i is the difference between the actual value and the fitted value:

Residual for Observation i
\[ \hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i \]

If \(\hat{u}_i > 0\), the actual value is above the predicted value (underprediction). If \(\hat{u}_i < 0\), the actual value is below the predicted value (overprediction). The OLS fitted values and residuals have three important properties:

  1. Zero mean of residuals: The sample average of the residuals is zero, so \(\bar{y} = \bar{\hat{y}}\).
  2. Zero covariance between residuals and each independent variable: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{ij} \hat{u}_i = 0\) for each j. Consequently, the sample covariance between the fitted values and the residuals is also zero.
  3. The point of means lies on the regression line: \(\bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}_1 + \cdots + \hat{\beta}_k \bar{x}_k\).

These properties are direct consequences of the OLS first-order conditions. Property (1) follows from the first equation in (3.13). Property (2) follows from the remaining equations. Property (3) follows from property (1).

Practical use: Property (2) is particularly important for diagnostic checking: if we compute the correlation between residuals and any independent variable and find it to be nonzero, this indicates a computational error or a violation of the OLS assumptions (e.g., misspecification).

Example

In the GPA example, for a student with hsGPA = 3.5 and ACT = 24, the fitted value is colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453(3.5) + 0.0094(24) = 3.101. If the student's actual college GPA is 3.25, the residual is 3.25 - 3.101 = 0.149, meaning the model underpredicted this student's GPA by 0.149 points.

Key Insight

The OLS residuals are guaranteed to have zero sample mean and zero correlation with each independent variable by construction. These are algebraic properties of the OLS estimator, not assumptions. They hold for any OLS regression, regardless of whether the model is correctly specified. This is why residual analysis is useful for model checking — patterns in residuals can reveal specification errors.

3.6 A "Partialling Out" Interpretation of Multiple Regression

For the case with two independent variables, the OLS estimate \(\hat{\beta}_1\) can be expressed in a revealing form:

Partialling Out Formula for \(\hat{\beta}_1\)
\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{r}_{i1} y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{r}_{i1}^2} \]

Here, \(\hat{r}_{i1}\) are the OLS residuals from a simple regression of x1 on x2 (using the same sample). These residuals represent the part of x1 that is uncorrelated with x2 — i.e., x1 after the effects of x2 have been "partialled out" or "netted out." Equation (3.22) says that \(\hat{\beta}_1\) can be obtained by a simple regression of y on these residuals \(\hat{r}_1\).

This representation gives another demonstration of \(\hat{\beta}_1\)'s partial effect interpretation: \(\hat{\beta}_1\) measures the sample relationship between y and x1 after x2 has been partialled out. In the general model with k explanatory variables, the same formula holds, but the residuals \(\hat{r}_{i1}\) come from the regression of x1 on x2, …, xk. This general result is known as the Frisch-Waugh theorem.

Practical use: The partialling out interpretation clarifies that multiple regression can be understood as a two-step procedure: (1) regress the variable of interest on all other control variables and obtain residuals; (2) regress the dependent variable on those residuals. The coefficient from step (2) is the same as the coefficient from the full multiple regression. This is rarely done in practice (since software handles it directly), but it is a powerful conceptual tool.

Example

In the wage equation, to obtain the coefficient on educ while controlling for exper and tenure, one could: (1) regress educ on exper and tenure and obtain residuals; (2) regress log(wage) on those residuals. The coefficient from step (2) would equal 0.092, the same as in the full multiple regression. This shows that the coefficient on educ reflects the variation in educ that is not explained by exper and tenure.

Key Insight

The partialling out interpretation reveals that multiple regression uses only the variation in each independent variable that is not shared with the other independent variables. This is why correlation among regressors is allowed (and common) but perfect collinearity is not — if one variable is perfectly explained by the others, there is no remaining variation to identify its coefficient.

3.7 Comparison of Simple and Multiple Regression Estimates

There is a simple relationship between the simple regression coefficient of y on x1 (denoted \(\tilde{\beta}_1\)) and the multiple regression coefficient of y on x1 and x2 (denoted \(\hat{\beta}_1\)):

Relationship Between Simple and Multiple Regression Coefficients
\[ \tilde{\beta}_1 = \hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 \tilde{\delta}_1 \]

Here, \(\tilde{\delta}_1\) is the slope coefficient from the simple regression of x2 on x1. This equation shows that the simple regression coefficient equals the multiple regression coefficient plus a confounding term: the partial effect of x2 on y (\(\hat{\beta}_2\)) times the slope in the sample regression of x2 on x1 (\(\tilde{\delta}_1\)). The simple and multiple regression estimates are equal in two special cases:

  1. \(\hat{\beta}_2 = 0\): The partial effect of x2 on y is zero in the sample.
  2. \(\tilde{\delta}_1 = 0\): x1 and x2 are uncorrelated in the sample.

In practice, these conditions rarely hold exactly, so simple and multiple regression estimates typically differ. The formula shows that the difference between simple and multiple regression estimates depends on both the correlation between the regressors and the importance of the omitted variable.

Practical use: This formula is useful for understanding why estimates change when variables are added or dropped. If a variable is added to a regression and the coefficient on the original variable changes substantially, it indicates that the added variable is (a) correlated with the original variable and (b) has a nontrivial partial effect on y.

Example 3.3: Participation in 401(k) Pension Plans

Multiple regression: prate = 80.12 + 5.52mrate + 0.243age, n = 1,534.

Simple regression: prate = 83.08 + 5.86mrate.

The simple regression estimate (5.86) is about 6.2% larger than the multiple regression estimate (5.52). The difference is modest because the sample correlation between mrate and age is only 0.12. If the correlation were higher, the discrepancy would be larger.

Analogy

Think of simple regression as a rough measurement and multiple regression as a refined measurement. Simple regression gives you the "gross" relationship between y and x1, which includes the indirect effect through x2. Multiple regression gives you the "net" relationship after removing the influence of x2. The difference between the two tells you how much of the gross relationship is actually due to x2.

Key Insight

The difference between simple and multiple regression estimates is a measure of omitted variable bias. If the simple regression estimate differs substantially from the multiple regression estimate, it suggests that the omitted variable(s) are both correlated with the included variable and have a meaningful effect on the outcome. This is why reporting multiple regression results is essential for credible analysis.

3.8 Goodness-of-Fit

As with simple regression, we can decompose the total variation in the dependent variable into explained and unexplained components. Define:

Sum of Squares Decomposition
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SST} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(Total Sum of Squares)} \\ \text{SSE} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(Explained Sum of Squares)} \\ \text{SSR} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{u}_i^2 \quad \text{(Residual Sum of Squares / Sum of Squared Residuals)} \end{aligned} \]

These satisfy the identity:

SST = SSE + SSR
\[ \text{SST} = \text{SSE} + \text{SSR} \]

The R-squared (R2) is defined as the proportion of the sample variation in y that is explained by the OLS regression line:

R-Squared
\[ R^2 = \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} \]

By definition, R2 is between 0 and 1. It can also be shown to equal the squared correlation coefficient between the actual yi and the fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\):

R-Squared as Squared Correlation
\[ R^2 = \frac{\left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})(\hat{y}_i - \bar{\hat{y}}) \right]^2}{\left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \right] \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - \bar{\hat{y}})^2 \right]} \]

An important algebraic fact is that R2 never decreases, and usually increases, when another independent variable is added to a regression (using the same set of observations). This is because SSR never increases when additional regressors are added. However, this makes R2 a poor tool for deciding whether to add variables — the decision should be based on whether the variable has a nonzero partial effect on y in the population, which is tested using statistical inference (Chapter 4).

Practical use: R2 is useful as a descriptive measure of how well the model fits the data, but it should not be used as the primary criterion for model selection. A low R2 does not necessarily mean the model is useless — the OLS estimates can still be reliable estimates of ceteris paribus effects even if the model explains little of the individual variation in y.

Example 3.4: Determinants of College GPA

colGPA = 1.29 + 0.453hsGPA + 0.0094ACT, n = 141, R2 = 0.176.

This means that hsGPA and ACT together explain about 17.6% of the variation in college GPA. This is not a high percentage, but there are many other factors (family background, personality, quality of high school education, affinity for college) that contribute to college performance. A low R2 here is expected — individual behavior is difficult to predict.

Example 3.5: Explaining Arrest Records

In the model explaining arrests (narr86), adding avgsen to a model with pcnv, ptime86, and qemp86 increased R2 from 0.0413 to 0.0422 — a practically small effect. The four variables together explain only about 4.2% of the variation in arrests. This low R2 reflects the difficulty of predicting individual criminal behavior, but it does not mean the estimates of the ceteris paribus effects are unreliable. A low R2 indicates that it is hard to predict individual outcomes, not that the model is invalid for estimating partial effects.

Key Insight

A low R2 does not invalidate the OLS estimates of ceteris paribus effects. The reliability of OLS estimates depends on the zero conditional mean assumption, not on the proportion of variance explained. A model with a low R2 can still provide unbiased and precise estimates of the partial effects of the included variables. The R2 is a measure of predictive power, not a measure of causal validity.

3.9 Regression through the Origin

Sometimes, economic theory suggests that the intercept should be zero. In such cases, we may estimate a model without an intercept:

Regression through the Origin
\[ \tilde{y} = \tilde{\beta}_1 x_1 + \tilde{\beta}_2 x_2 + \cdots + \tilde{\beta}_k x_k \]

Here, the symbol "~" distinguishes these estimates from OLS estimates obtained with an intercept. When all independent variables are zero, the predicted value is zero. The estimates minimize the sum of squared residuals subject to the intercept being zero.

Important warnings:

  1. The OLS residuals no longer have a zero sample average.
  2. The R2 defined as 1 - SSR/SST can be negative, meaning that the sample average y explains more variation than the model. Some economists prefer to compute R2 as the squared correlation between actual and fitted values.
  3. If the population intercept β0 is nonzero, the slope estimators will be biased — sometimes severely.
  4. Estimating an intercept when β0 = 0 is harmless but increases the variance of the slope estimators.

Practical use: Regression through the origin is rarely used in practice. It is generally safer to include an intercept unless there is a very strong theoretical justification for omitting it. The cost of including an unnecessary intercept is only a loss of efficiency (larger variances), whereas the cost of omitting a necessary intercept is bias.

Key Insight

Regression through the origin is risky and should be avoided unless the researcher has strong theoretical justification that the intercept is exactly zero. The standard properties of OLS (zero mean residuals, unbiasedness, nonnegative R2) do not hold for regression through the origin. Including an intercept is the default and recommended practice.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS estimates minimize the sum of squared residuals and satisfy the first-order conditions (3.13).
  • Each slope coefficient has a ceteris paribus interpretation: it measures the change in predicted y for a one-unit change in that variable, holding all others fixed.
  • Fitted values and residuals satisfy important algebraic properties: zero mean of residuals, zero covariance between residuals and each independent variable, and the point of means lies on the regression line.
  • The partialling out interpretation (Frisch-Waugh theorem) shows that each coefficient uses only the variation in the corresponding variable that is not explained by the other regressors.
  • Simple and multiple regression estimates differ by a confounding term that depends on the correlation between regressors and the importance of the omitted variable.
  • R2 measures the proportion of variance explained but is not a measure of causal validity. A low R2 does not imply that the model is useless.
  • Regression through the origin is generally not recommended due to potential bias and loss of standard properties.

4. Statistical Properties of OLS Estimators

4.1 Assumptions for Unbiasedness (MLR.1–MLR.4)

The OLS estimators have desirable statistical properties (unbiasedness, efficiency) under a set of assumptions. These are extensions of the simple regression assumptions to the multiple regression context. Four assumptions are required for unbiasedness:

Assumption MLR.1: Linear in Parameters
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k + u \]

The population model is linear in the parameters β0, β1, …, βk. This is the definition of the multiple linear regression model. The model can be nonlinear in variables (e.g., logarithms, squares) as long as it is linear in parameters.

Assumption MLR.2: Random Sampling
\[ \{(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, \ldots, x_{ik}, y_i) : i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\} \]

We have a random sample of n observations from the population described by the model in MLR.1. This ensures that each observation is independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) according to the population model. For each observation i, we can write:

Model for Observation i
\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_k x_{ik} + u_i \]

This assumption is typically satisfied by survey data collected using probability sampling. It is violated in cases of nonrandom sampling or when observations are correlated (e.g., time series data with autocorrelation, though different assumptions are used for those settings).

Assumption MLR.3: No Perfect Collinearity
\[ \text{No exact linear relationship among the independent variables} \]

In the sample (and therefore in the population), none of the independent variables is constant, and there are no exact linear relationships among the independent variables. If an independent variable is an exact linear combination of the others, the model suffers from perfect collinearity and cannot be estimated by OLS.

Important nuances:

  • Assumption MLR.3 allows independent variables to be correlated — it only rules out perfect correlation. Correlation among regressors is expected and the primary motivation for using multiple regression.
  • Perfect collinearity can arise from including the same variable measured in different units (e.g., income in dollars and income in thousands of dollars).
  • It can also arise from including a variable that is an exact linear function of others (e.g., including totexpend = expendA + expendB along with expendA and expendB).
  • Including a variable and its square does not violate MLR.3 because x2 is not an exact linear function of x.
  • Including log(x) and log(x2) does violate MLR.3 because log(x2) = 2·log(x), which is an exact linear function.
Assumption MLR.4: Zero Conditional Mean
\[ E(u \mid x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_k) = 0 \]

The error term u has an expected value of zero given any values of the independent variables. This is the key assumption for unbiasedness. It requires that all factors in the unobserved error term be uncorrelated with the explanatory variables and that the functional relationships have been correctly specified. When this assumption holds, we say that the independent variables are exogenous. When it fails, we say that the independent variables are endogenous.

Practical implications: MLR.4 fails if:

  • An important variable is omitted from the model and is correlated with any included variable.
  • The functional form is misspecified (e.g., using a linear model when the true relationship is quadratic).
  • There is measurement error in the independent variables that is correlated with u.
  • There is simultaneity (reverse causality) between y and one or more x variables.

Example: Perfect Collinearity

Consider the model voteA = β0 + β1expendA + β2expendB + β3totexpend + u, where totexpend = expendA + expendB. This violates MLR.3 because totexpend is an exact linear combination of expendA and expendB. The OLS software cannot estimate this model — it will typically drop one of the variables or report an error.

Key Insight

Assumptions MLR.1–MLR.4 are sufficient for the OLS estimators to be unbiased. MLR.4 (zero conditional mean) is the most critical and the most likely to be violated in practice. The other assumptions are typically easier to satisfy: MLR.1 is a modeling choice, MLR.2 is usually satisfied by proper sampling, and MLR.3 can be checked directly from the data.

4.2 Unbiasedness of OLS

Under Assumptions MLR.1 through MLR.4, the OLS estimators are unbiased for the population parameters:

Unbiasedness of OLS Estimators
\[ E(\hat{\beta}_j) = \beta_j, \quad j = 0, 1, \ldots, k \]

This means that, across repeated random sampling, the average of the OLS estimates equals the true population parameter. The unbiasedness property holds for each coefficient individually, including the intercept. The proof relies on the zero conditional mean assumption (MLR.4) and the linearity of the estimator in the dependent variable.

Important caveat: Unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution of the estimator, not a property of a single estimate. It does not guarantee that any particular estimate from a single sample is close to the true value. It means that the estimator is "correct on average" over many hypothetical samples.

Practical use: Unbiasedness is a desirable property because it means that the estimation method does not systematically overestimate or underestimate the true parameters. However, unbiasedness alone does not guarantee that the estimator is precise — we also care about the variance of the estimator (efficiency), which is addressed in Section 3-4 of the source.

Key Insight

Unbiasedness is a property of the estimator, not the estimate. When we say "OLS is unbiased under MLR.1–MLR.4," we mean that if we were to repeatedly draw random samples and compute OLS estimates each time, the average of those estimates would converge to the true population parameters. This is a statement about the reliability of the method across repeated sampling, not about any single estimate.

4.3 Omitted Variable Bias

One of the most important violations of MLR.4 is omitted variable bias. This occurs when a variable that should be in the model (because it affects y) is omitted and is correlated with one or more included variables. The OLS estimators of the included variables then become biased, and the direction of bias can be analyzed.

Consider the true model with two explanatory variables:

True Model (with Two Variables)
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u \]

Suppose we estimate the misspecified model that omits x2:

Misspecified Model (Omitted x2)
\[ \tilde{y} = \tilde{\beta}_0 + \tilde{\beta}_1 x_1 \]

The expected value of the OLS estimator from the misspecified model is:

Expected Value of OLS Estimator under Omitted Variable
\[ E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \delta_1 \]

where δ1 is the slope coefficient from the population regression of x2 on x1 (i.e., x2 = δ0 + δ1x1 + v). The bias in \(\tilde{\beta}_1\) is β2δ1. The sign of the bias depends on the signs of β2 (the partial effect of the omitted variable on y) and δ1 (the relationship between the omitted variable and the included variable).

Direction of bias:

  • If β2 > 0 and δ1 > 0, the bias is positive (upward bias).
  • If β2 > 0 and δ1 < 0, the bias is negative (downward bias).
  • If β2 < 0 and δ1 > 0, the bias is negative.
  • If β2 < 0 and δ1 < 0, the bias is positive.
  • If either β2 = 0 or δ1 = 0, there is no bias.

Practical use: This formula is crucial for understanding the sensitivity of results to omitted variables. Even if the omitted variable is not observable, the researcher can often sign the likely bias based on economic theory. For example, in a wage equation, if ability is omitted from the model and is positively correlated with education (δ1 > 0) and has a positive effect on wages (β2 > 0), then the OLS estimate of the return to education is biased upward. This means that the estimated return to education overstates the true causal effect.

Example: Omitted Variable Bias in Wage Equation

True model: log(wage) = β0 + β1educ + β2abil + u, where abil is innate ability. If we estimate log(wage) = β0 + β1educ + u (omitting ability), the expected value of the OLS estimator of β1 is β1 + β2δ1, where δ1 is from the regression of abil on educ. Since ability is likely positively correlated with education (δ1 > 0) and has a positive effect on wages (β2 > 0), the bias is positive. Thus, the simple regression overestimates the return to education.

Key Insight

Omitted variable bias is one of the most serious threats to causal inference in regression analysis. The bias formula shows that the direction and magnitude of bias depend on the relationship between the omitted variable and the included variable(s) and the importance of the omitted variable. The solution is to include relevant control variables, use proxy variables, or employ instrumental variables methods (Chapter 15).

Key Takeaways

  • Under MLR.1–MLR.4, the OLS estimators are unbiased: E(\(\hat{\beta}_j\)) = βj for all j.
  • MLR.4 (zero conditional mean) is the most critical assumption for unbiasedness.
  • MLR.3 (no perfect collinearity) ensures that the OLS estimates can be uniquely computed.
  • Omitted variable bias occurs when a relevant variable is omitted and is correlated with included variables.
  • The bias formula is E(\(\tilde{\beta}_1\)) = β1 + β2δ1, where δ1 measures the relationship between the omitted and included variables.
  • Unbiasedness is a property of the estimator across repeated sampling, not a property of a single estimate.

5. Omitted Variable Bias

Omitted variable bias is a fundamental concern in multiple regression analysis. It occurs when a variable that belongs in the population model (i.e., has a nonzero partial effect on y) is omitted from the estimated regression and is correlated with one or more of the included explanatory variables. The consequence is that the OLS estimators of the included variables are biased and inconsistent.

The bias formula for the case of omitting x2 from a model with two explanatory variables is:

Omitted Variable Bias Formula
\[ E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 \delta_1 \]

where \(\tilde{\beta}_1\) is the OLS estimator from the misspecified model (omitting x2), β1 is the true partial effect of x1 on y, β2 is the true partial effect of the omitted variable x2 on y, and δ1 is the slope coefficient from the population regression of x2 on x1 (i.e., x2 = δ0 + δ1x1 + v).

The bias in \(\tilde{\beta}_1\) is β2δ1. This bias does not diminish as the sample size increases — it is a systematic bias, not a small-sample issue. Even with an infinite sample, the estimator would converge to β1 + β2δ1 rather than to β1.

Key insights from the bias formula:

  • If the omitted variable has no partial effect on y (β2 = 0), there is no bias, even if the omitted variable is correlated with the included variable.
  • If the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the included variable (δ1 = 0), there is no bias, even if the omitted variable has a large effect on y.
  • Both conditions must be violated for bias to occur: the omitted variable must affect y and be correlated with the included variable.

Direction of bias: The sign of the bias is the product of the signs of β2 and δ1. If both are positive, the bias is positive (the estimator overstates the true effect). If β2 is positive and δ1 is negative, the bias is negative (the estimator understates the true effect). If the signs differ, the bias can be in either direction.

Practical use: The bias formula is used to assess the likely direction and magnitude of bias when an important variable cannot be measured. For example, in estimating the effect of education on wages, researchers know that ability is likely positively correlated with education (δ1 > 0) and positively affects wages (β2 > 0), so the bias is positive. This means that OLS estimates of the return to education are likely overestimates of the true causal effect. This is why researchers often use instrumental variables, fixed effects, or other methods to address omitted variable bias.

Example: Omitted Variable Bias in the GPA Model

In Example 3.1, the simple regression of colGPA on ACT gives a coefficient of 0.0271, while the multiple regression controlling for hsGPA gives a coefficient of 0.0094 on ACT. The difference can be understood using the omitted variable bias formula. If we omit hsGPA from the model, the bias in the OLS estimator of the coefficient on ACT is βhsGPA × δACT, where βhsGPA is the partial effect of hsGPA on colGPA (0.453) and δACT is the slope from the regression of hsGPA on ACT (which is positive, since hsGPA and ACT are positively correlated). The positive bias explains why the simple regression coefficient (0.0271) is larger than the multiple regression coefficient (0.0094).

Analogy

Omitted variable bias is like trying to measure the effect of a new fertilizer on crop yield while ignoring the fact that the fields receiving the fertilizer also received more water. The estimated effect of fertilizer is "contaminated" by the effect of water. The bias formula tells us exactly how much contamination to expect, given the relationship between fertilizer and water and the effect of water on yield.

Key Insight

Omitted variable bias is a violation of the zero conditional mean assumption (MLR.4). When a relevant variable is omitted, the error term effectively includes that variable. If the omitted variable is correlated with any included variable, then E(u | x1, …, xk) ≠ 0, and the OLS estimators are biased. The bias does not disappear with large samples — it is a permanent feature of the misspecified model.

Key Takeaways

  • Omitted variable bias occurs when a relevant variable is omitted from the model and is correlated with included variables.
  • The bias formula is E(\(\tilde{\beta}_1\)) = β1 + β2δ1, where δ1 is from the regression of the omitted variable on the included variable.
  • The bias can be positive or negative, depending on the signs of β2 and δ1.
  • The bias does not vanish with large sample size — it is a systematic bias.
  • If either β2 = 0 (the omitted variable does not affect y) or δ1 = 0 (the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the included variable), there is no bias.
  • Researchers should use economic theory to sign the likely bias and assess the sensitivity of results.

6. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

Multiple regression analysis is a powerful tool, but it is subject to several common mistakes and limitations. The following cautions are essential for proper application and interpretation.

Common Mistakes and Cautions
\[ \text{Be aware of: interpretation, causality, assumptions, measurement, specification} \]

1. Incorrect interpretation of coefficients: The coefficient \(\hat{\beta}_j\) measures the change in the predicted value of y for a one-unit change in xj, holding all other independent variables fixed. It does not measure the total effect of xj on y if other variables are allowed to change in response. For example, in the quadratic model cons = β0 + β1inc + β2inc2 + u, it makes no sense to interpret β1 as the effect of income while holding inc2 fixed, because if income changes, so does its square. The marginal effect of income on consumption is β1 + 2β2inc, which depends on the level of income.

2. Confusing prediction with causality: Obtaining a nonzero coefficient does not establish that xj causes y. The zero conditional mean assumption (MLR.4) is required for causal interpretation, and this assumption is not guaranteed by the data. Correlation does not imply causation, and multiple regression does not change this fundamental principle — it only controls for observed confounders.

3. Ignoring model assumptions: The unbiasedness of OLS depends on MLR.1–MLR.4. If these assumptions are violated, the estimates may be biased and inconsistent. Common violations include omitted variable bias, measurement error, simultaneity, and misspecification of functional form.

4. Misinterpreting the intercept: The intercept is the predicted value of y when all independent variables are zero. This may not be a meaningful scenario in many applications. For example, in the GPA model, setting hsGPA = 0 and ACT = 0 is not realistic for college students. The intercept is still needed for prediction, but its value should not be interpreted out of context.

5. Ignoring units of measurement: The magnitude of a coefficient depends on the units of measurement of both the independent and dependent variables. Changing the units (e.g., from dollars to thousands of dollars) changes the coefficient proportionally. Always interpret coefficients in terms of the actual units used in the model.

6. Confusing error and residual: The error term u is the unobserved disturbance in the population model. The residual \(\hat{u}\) is the observed difference between the actual and fitted values in the sample. The residuals are estimates of the errors, but they are not the same thing. The properties of the residuals (zero mean, zero correlation with x variables) are algebraic properties of OLS, not assumptions about the errors.

7. Relying too heavily on R2: R2 always increases when variables are added, so it is a poor criterion for model selection. A high R2 does not imply a good model, and a low R2 does not imply a bad model. The R2 measures explained variation, not causal validity.

8. Omitted-variable problems: The most common and serious threat to validity is omitting a variable that is correlated with both the dependent variable and one or more included independent variables. The bias formula (Section 5) should be used to assess the likely direction and magnitude of bias.

9. Perfect collinearity: Including variables that are exact linear combinations of each other makes estimation impossible. Common examples: including the same variable in different units, including total and its components, or including log(x) and log(x2).
