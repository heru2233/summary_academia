Chapter 4 Summary

Financial Services: Securities Firms and Investment Banks

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 4. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

Securities firms and investment banks primarily help net suppliers of funds (e.g., households) transfer funds to net users of funds (e.g., businesses) at a low cost and with maximum efficiency. Unlike other types of FIs, they do not transform the securities issued by fund users into claims that are more attractive to fund suppliers, but rather serve as brokers intermediating between the two.

Investment banking involves raising debt and equity securities for corporations or governments, including the origination, underwriting, and placement of securities in money and capital markets. Securities services involve assistance in trading securities in secondary markets (brokerage and market making). The largest companies performing both sets of services are called bulge-bracket investment banks (e.g., BofA Securities), while firms specializing in trading existing securities are called securities firms (the retail side) and firms specializing in originating and underwriting new issues are called boutique investment banks (the commercial side).

The industry has undergone substantial structural changes since the financial crisis, including the acquisition of Bear Stearns by JPMorgan Chase, the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers, and the acquisition of Merrill Lynch by Bank of America. Of the five largest investment banks at the start of 2008, all were gone as investment banks by the end of the year, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley converting to commercial bank holding companies in 2008.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

Because of the emphasis on securities trading and underwriting, industry size is usually measured by equity capital rather than assets, since these activities require no large investment in assets or liability funding. According to SIFMA, equity capital in the securities industry amounted to $223 billion in 2021.

The number of firms expanded from 5,248 (1980) to 9,515 (1987), then declined to 5,052 (2006, down 47 percent since 1987) and continued falling to 3,435 (2020). Electronic trading dramatically increased volumes and liquidity, slashed intermediation costs, and broadened market access, including automated trading, algorithmic trading, high-frequency trading, and the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning to trading logic.

The financial crisis permanently changed the industry's structure. The five largest investment banks at the beginning of 2008 (Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, Merrill Lynch, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley) were all gone as investment banks by the end of the year. Lehman failed, Bear Stearns and Merrill Lynch were acquired (by JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America), and Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley obtained commercial bank charters. In 2021, commercial bank holding companies' fee income from investment banking, advisory, brokerage, and underwriting topped $15.23 billion, up from $9.15 billion in 2012 and $14.10 billion before the crisis.

2.1 Types of Firms in the Industry

The largest firms, called national full-line firms, serve both retail customers (as broker-dealers) and corporate customers (underwriting), and now fall into three subgroups, namely (1) commercial bank holding companies that are the largest full-service investment banks, such as Bank of America (through its acquisition of Merrill Lynch), Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan Chase (which acquired Bear Stearns for $240 million in 2008), (2) national full-line firms focusing more on corporate business and highly active in trading, such as Goldman Sachs and Salomon Brothers/Smith Barney of Citigroup, and (3) large investment banks with limited branch networks and institutional client bases, such as Lazard Ltd. and Greenhill & Co.

The rest of the industry consists of firms serving particular market segments

  1. Regional securities firms, serving customers in a particular region, such as Raymond James Financial
  2. Specialized discount brokers, effecting trades for customers without offering investment advice, such as Charles Schwab
  3. Specialized electronic trading securities firms, providing a platform for trading without a broker, such as E*TRADE
  4. Venture capital firms, pooling money from individual investors and other FIs to fund relatively small and new businesses, such as in biotechnology
  5. Other firms, including research boutiques and designated market makers such as GTS, Virtu Financial, and Citadel Securities

3. Key Activities

Securities firms and investment banks engage in up to seven key activity areas, namely investment banking, venture capital, market making, trading, investing, cash management, and mergers and acquisitions, plus other service functions. Many activities are conducted simultaneously for the same customers.

3.1 Investment Banking

Investment banking refers to underwriting and distributing new issues of debt and equity. Issues can be primary (first-time issues, called IPOs) or secondary (issues of seasoned firms). In 2021, a total of $11.47 trillion of debt and equity was underwritten, up from $4.95 trillion in 2008 and $7.51 trillion in 2007. Of this, $1.29 trillion was global equity proceeds, up 19.0 percent from 2020 and the strongest period since 1980, with global IPO proceeds up 83 percent driven by mega IPOs and unicorn companies, for example Rivian Automotive ($13.7 billion), GlobalFoundries ($2.9 billion), and Bumble ($2.5 billion).

The top five underwriters controlled 22.6 percent of the global debt market and 39.9 percent of the global equity market, showing that the industry is dominated by a handful of top-tier firms. Underwritings can be done through private placements (placing securities with one or a few large institutional investors) or public offerings, which may be on a best-efforts basis (the investment bank acts as an agent for a fee tied to placement success) or a firm commitment basis (the investment bank buys the securities from the issuer at one price and sells them to the public at a slightly higher price, bearing the price risk).

Example 4-1. Best-Efforts versus Firm Commitment

An investment bank agrees to underwrite an issue of 20 million shares of Murray Construction Corp. on a firm commitment basis, paying $15.50 per share to the issuer.

\[ \text{Issuer proceeds} = \$15.50 \times 20{,}000{,}000 = \$310{,}000{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Bank profit} = (\$16.35 - \$15.50) \times 20{,}000{,}000 = \$17{,}000{,}000 \]

If the bank sells the shares at $16.35, the issuer still receives $310 million, the bank earns $17 million, and the issuer's stock price is $16.35. However, if the bank can only sell at $14.75, the issuer still receives $310 million but the bank suffers a loss

\[ \text{Bank loss} = (\$14.75 - \$15.50) \times 20{,}000{,}000 = -\$15{,}000{,}000 \]

On a best-efforts basis, the bank sells 18.4 million shares at $15.50 and charges $0.375 per share sold. The issuer receives

\[ \text{Issuer proceeds} = (\$15.50 - \$0.375) \times 18{,}400{,}000 = \$278{,}300{,}000 \]
\[ \text{Bank profit} = \$0.375 \times 18{,}400{,}000 = \$6{,}900{,}000 \]

On a best-efforts basis, the bank's profit stays at $6.9 million regardless of the selling price, because the price risk is borne by the issuer, not the bank.

3.2 Venture Capital

Venture capital is a professionally managed pool of money used to finance new and often high-risk firms, in return for an equity investment. Unlike commercial banks, which are generally unwilling to lend to companies with no assets and business history, venture capital firms buy an equity interest and are usually active, providing expertise and even helping recruit senior managers. Types include institutional venture capital firms (limited partnerships, subsidiaries of investment or commercial banks, and corporate venture capital subsidiaries), small business investment companies (SBICs) licensed by the Small Business Administration with access to very low-rate Treasury funding, and angel venture capitalists (wealthy individuals), who have invested much more in new and small firms than institutions. Venture capital differs from private equity, which deals more with established companies. In making investment decisions, venture capital firms look for two things, namely high returns (sometimes as high as 700 percent within five to seven years) and an easy exit.

3.3 Market Making

Market making involves creating a secondary market in an asset, either through agency transactions (on behalf of customers for a fee, for example buying IBM stock from one customer at $190 and immediately reselling it at $191 to another, with the $1 difference being the bid-ask spread) or principal transactions (holding inventory positions to profit from price movements). In periods of market stress, market-making profits can disappear, for example NYSE specialists, who have an affirmative obligation to buy stocks even when the market is crashing, suffered actual and near bankruptcies in October 1987. A modern example is Knight Capital's failure on August 1, 2012, when its automated routing system streamed thousands of orders per second over 45 minutes, executing over 4 million trades in 154 stocks totaling more than 397 million shares, causing losses of over $460 million and a 75 percent drop in its stock price in one day, before it was acquired by Getco (forming KCG Holdings) and later by Virtu Financial. Competition from electronic exchanges such as Instinet also cut profits, with NYSE floor specialists handling only 12 percent of volume in 2018 (down from 100 percent in the early 1990s) and floor traders falling to fewer than 500 from about 5,000 at the peak.

3.4 Trading

There are at least six types of trading activities

  1. Position trading, purchasing large blocks of securities expecting a favorable price move, holding positions for weeks or months
  2. Pure arbitrage, buying an asset in one market and immediately selling it in another at a higher price, locking in profits with no equity investment, usually exploiting discrepancies between spot and futures prices
  3. Risk arbitrage, buying securities in anticipation of an information release such as a merger or Federal Reserve decision, with the risk of losses if the event does not occur
  4. Program trading, defined by the NYSE as the simultaneous buying and selling of a portfolio of at least 15 stocks valued at more than $1 million, initiated by computer programs, often seeking arbitrage between cash and futures prices (e.g., the S&P 500 index)
  5. Stock brokerage, trading securities on behalf of individuals through a broker at an exchange
  6. Electronic brokerage, direct Internet access to the trading floor, bypassing traditional brokers at lower cost per share

3.5 Investing, Cash Management, and M&A

Investing involves managing pools of assets such as closed- and open-end mutual funds and pension funds, aiming to beat a return-risk benchmark such as the S&P 500 index, generating a more stable fee income than investment banking or trading. Cash management offers bank-deposit-like cash management accounts (CMAs), which are money market mutual funds with check-writing privileges, blurring the distinction between commercial and investment banks. Mergers and acquisitions involve advice, finding merger partners, underwriting new securities, valuing target firms, and even helping prevent mergers (e.g., writing poison-pill provisions). Domestic M&A value rose from under $200 billion (1990) to $1.83 trillion (2000, 10,864 deals), fell to $458 billion (2002), peaked at about $1.7 trillion in the U.S. and $4.5 trillion worldwide (2007), declined during the crisis ($903 billion, $713 billion, and $687 billion in 2008, 2009, and 2010), and recovered to $2,749 billion in the U.S. and $4,399 billion worldwide in 2021, the strongest M&A period since 1980.

Back-office functions include custody and escrow services, clearance and settlement, and research and advisory services. Part of the research purchased with client commissions is called soft dollars, creating a conflict of interest that the SEC requires firms to disclose. Conflicts between analyst research and underwriting triggered investigations, for example Merrill Lynch paid a $100 million fine for overly optimistic reports, and in spring 2003 the ten largest securities firms agreed to a record $1.4 billion settlement over investor abuse charges.

4. Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

4.1 Trends

Industry performance depends heavily on the state of the stock market. Brokerage commissions declined as a percentage of revenues since the SEC abolished fixed commissions in May 1975, stabilized and recovered after 1992, and returned to 15-20 percent of total revenues in the mid-2010s. FINRA-registered broker-dealers fell from 4,923 (2008) to 3,435 (2020), down 30 percent, while registered investment advisors (RIAs) rose from 24,147 (2008) to 30,888 (2020), up 28 percent, and fee-based revenue at leading retail broker-dealers grew from 57 percent (2015) to 70 percent (2019).

Domestic underwriting grew from $192.7 billion (1990) to $1,623.9 billion (2002) and $4,389.3 billion (2021), driven by the attractiveness of corporate debt at low long-term rates, debt's lower risk than equity, and growth in asset-backed securities. Pretax profits reached a record $31.6 billion (2000), fell 24 percent to $16 billion (2001) due to the slowdown and the September 11 attacks (Bank of New York costs $125 million, Citigroup $100-200 million, Morgan Stanley $150 million), rose to $24.1 billion (2003) and $33.1 billion (2006), collapsed to $0.78 billion (2007) and a record loss of $34.1 billion (2008), surged to a record $61.4 billion (2009), and eventually reached $77.2 billion (2020).

4.2 Balance Sheet

The short-term nature of many assets of securities firms reflects the importance of trading and brokerage. On the 2016 consolidated balance sheet, the largest assets were receivables from other broker-dealers (29.3 percent), securities purchased under agreements to resell, i.e., reverse repurchase agreements (29.1 percent), and long positions in securities and commodities (22.6 percent). Because the balance sheet consists of financial market securities, the industry faces high levels of market risk, interest rate risk, foreign exchange risk, and sovereign risk.

Like other FIs, securities firms use high financial leverage, but their liabilities are extremely short term. The major source of funds is repurchase agreements (34.3 percent of total liabilities and equity), followed by payables to customers and other broker-dealers, and securities sold short. Over 36 percent of total liabilities are payables incurred in the transaction process. Equity capital is only 6.1 percent of total assets, well below depository institutions (commercial banks 11.34 percent, savings institutions 10.74 percent, credit unions 10.80 percent), because securities firms' balance sheets contain mostly tradable, liquid securities. Securities firms must maintain an aggregate indebtedness to net capital of no more than 1,500 percent, meaning debt cannot exceed 15 times equity.

5. Regulation

The primary regulator of the securities industry is the SEC, established in 1934 largely in response to abuses by securities firms. Its main roles include administering securities laws, reviewing registrations of new securities offerings, reviewing annual and semiannual reports of public corporations, and prohibiting market manipulation. The National Securities Markets Improvement Act (NSMIA) of 1996 reaffirmed the SEC as the primary regulator by removing most state regulatory burdens, effectively giving the SEC exclusive jurisdiction over securities firms.

The analyst research scandals produced major enforcement. In spring 2003, the ten largest securities firms paid a record $1.4 billion settlement, including Citigroup $400 million, Credit Suisse First Boston and Merrill Lynch $200 million each, Morgan Stanley $125 million, Goldman Sachs $110 million, Bear Stearns, JPMorgan Chase, Lehman Brothers, and UBS Warburg $80 million each, and Piper Jaffray $32 million. The settlement required separating research and banking structures and tying analyst pay to research quality rather than underwriting business. Further cases followed, for example Morgan Stanley paid $5 million (December 2012, related to the Facebook IPO) and Citigroup, Goldman Sachs, and eight other firms were fined $43.5 million (December 2014, related to the Toys "R" Us IPO).

Besides the SEC, FINRA is an independent nonprofit organization regulating day-to-day trading practices, including oversight of dark pools (about 13 percent of U.S. stock trades in 2018, up from 3 percent in 2007 and reaching 40 percent by 2020). Goldman Sachs closed its dark pool in 2014, and dark pool penalties were imposed on UBS ($14.5 million), Barclays ($35 million to the SEC and $35 million to the NYAG), and Credit Suisse ($84.3 million total). Congress also oversees the industry, for example through the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations hearings in spring 2010 on investment banks' role in the crisis.

The Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 brought many changes, including the Financial Services Oversight Council, an investment advisor registration threshold of $100 million in assets under management (up from $25 million, effective July 21, 2011), regulation of securitization markets and credit rating agencies, a requirement that issuers and originators retain a financial interest in securitized loans, comprehensive regulation of OTC derivatives, and the Volcker Rule prohibiting proprietary trading (implemented April 2014, compliance by July 21, 2015). The USA Patriot Act imposed three requirements effective October 1, 2003, namely verifying the identity of account openers, maintaining verification records, and checking terrorist lists. Finally, the Securities Investor Protection Corporation (SIPC), created under the Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970 as a nonprofit membership corporation, protects investors against losses of up to $500,000 caused by securities firm failures.

6. Global Issues

More than other FI sectors, securities firms and investment banks operate globally. The top five global debt underwriters in 2021 are U.S. banks (JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America Securities, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley), followed by European banks (Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, HSBC, Credit Suisse), while for global equity, 5 of the top 10 are U.S. banks, 3 European, and 2 Chinese. Foreign investors' transactions in U.S. stocks grew from $6,173.7 billion (2003) to $24,031.1 billion (2008, up 289 percent), fell to $13,155.2 billion (2009), then reached an all-time high of $48,552.9 billion (2020). U.S. investors' transactions in foreign-listed stocks grew from $2,697.7 billion (2003) to $10,866.3 billion (2008, up 303 percent) and an all-time high of $14,435.6 billion (2020). Total international security offerings rose from $1,713.0 billion (2003) to $4,094.9 billion (2020), with U.S. issuers offering $915.0 billion internationally.

After the crisis, global investment banks sought strategic alliances or exited foreign markets. Morgan Stanley sold a 21 percent stake to Mitsubishi UFJ (2008) and formed a joint venture in March 2009, while Citigroup sold its Japanese securities and asset management units to Sumitomo Mitsui Financial Group. A major scandal marked the global industry, namely the manipulation of the LIBOR interbank rate, estimated to have earned the banks involved at least $75 billion. Barclays agreed to a $450 million settlement (June 2012), UBS about $1.5 billion (December 2012), and Royal Bank of Scotland $610 million (February 2013), while in 2016 six major banks (Barclays, JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, RBS, UBS, and Bank of America) were fined nearly $6 billion total for rigging the foreign exchange market and LIBOR. Trader Tom Hayes was sentenced to 14 years in prison in August 2015, the first criminal conviction of an individual for LIBOR manipulation.

Nearly $200 trillion of derivatives, loans, securities, and mortgages referenced a benchmark derived from just $500 million in average daily trading volumes (year-end 2016), so regulators developed alternative reference rates, namely SOFR (U.S.), SONIA (U.K.), ESTER (euro area), SARON (Switzerland), and TONA (Japan). SOFR, calculated from the U.S. Treasury repo market with daily volumes of about $1 trillion, is more resilient to manipulation. LIBOR ceased to be provided after December 31, 2021 for sterling, euro, Swiss franc, yen, and the one-week and two-month U.S. dollar settings, and after June 30, 2023 for the remaining U.S. dollar settings.

7. Key Calculations and Formulas

7.1 Firm Commitment Underwriting

Under firm commitment, the investment bank buys the entire issue from the issuer at a fixed price and bears the price risk.

\[ \text{Issuer proceeds} = P_{\text{buy}} \times N \]
\[ \text{Bank profit} = (P_{\text{sell}} - P_{\text{buy}}) \times N \]

where \( P_{\text{buy}} \) is the price the bank pays the issuer, \( P_{\text{sell}} \) the public selling price, and \( N \) the number of shares. For example, 20 million shares at $15.50 with a selling price of $16.35 yield issuer proceeds of $310 million and bank profit of $17 million, while a selling price of $14.75 yields a bank loss of $15 million.

7.2 Best-Efforts Underwriting

Under best efforts, the bank acts as an agent earning a fee per share sold, without bearing price risk.

\[ \text{Issuer proceeds} = (P_{\text{sell}} - \text{fee}) \times N_{\text{sold}} \]
\[ \text{Bank profit} = \text{fee} \times N_{\text{sold}} \]

For example, 18.4 million shares sold at $15.50 with a $0.375 per-share fee yield issuer proceeds of $278.3 million and bank profit of $6.9 million, regardless of the selling price.

7.3 Securities Firm Leverage Limit

\[ \frac{\text{Aggregate indebtedness}}{\text{Net capital}} \leq 1{,}500\% \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Debt} \leq 15 \times \text{Capital} \]

Securities firms must keep aggregate indebtedness at no more than 15 times net capital, serving as a substitute for bank-style minimum capital requirements.

7.4 Growth of Global Securities Transactions

\[ \text{Growth} = \frac{V_{2008} - V_{2003}}{V_{2003}} \times 100\% \]

Foreign investors' transactions in U.S. stocks grew 289 percent between 2003 and 2008, and U.S. investors' transactions in foreign stocks grew 303 percent over the same period.

7.5 Other Key Data

  • Securities industry equity capital, $223 billion (2021)
  • Number of broker-dealers, 3,435 (2020) from 4,923 (2008)
  • Total global underwriting, $11.47 trillion (2021), with global equity of $1.29 trillion
  • BHC investment banking fee income, $15.23 billion (2021)
  • Industry pretax profits, record $61.4 billion (2009) and $77.2 billion (2020) versus a record loss of $34.1 billion (2008)
  • Equity-to-assets ratio, 6.1 percent (2016) versus 11.34 percent for commercial banks
  • SIPC protection, up to $500,000 per customer

8. Summary and Conclusion

This chapter presented an overview of securities firms (which offer largely retail services to investors) and investment banking firms (which offer largely wholesale services to corporate customers). The industry assists in bringing new issues of debt and equity to the markets, facilitates trading and market making of securities after issuance, and supports corporate mergers and restructurings. Industry size is measured by equity capital, key activities include investment banking, venture capital, market making, trading, investing, cash management, and M&A, and balance sheets are dominated by short-term market positions with very high leverage.

The industry is in a period of consolidation and globalization. The financial crisis eliminated the independent investment bank model, with the five largest investment banks gone or converted into bank holding companies, while post-Dodd-Frank regulation including the Volcker Rule restricts proprietary trading. The LIBOR scandal and research conflicts of interest highlight the importance of market integrity, and the transition from LIBOR to SOFR marks a major change in global financial market infrastructure.
