Chapter 3 Summary

Financial Services: Finance Companies

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 3. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

Like commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions, the main financial service provided by finance companies is lending. Their primary function is to make loans to both individuals and corporations, covering consumer lending, business lending, and real estate lending. Some of their loans are similar to depository institutions' loans, such as consumer and auto loans, but others are more specialized.

The fundamental difference from depository institutions is that finance companies do not accept deposits, relying instead on short- and long-term debt as a source of funds. In addition, finance companies often lend to customers that depository institutions find too risky. This difference can lead to losses and even failure if the high risk does not pay off.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

The first major finance company was originated during the Depression, when General Electric Corp. created General Electric Capital Corp. (GECC, now GE Capital Global Holdings or GECGH) to finance appliance sales to cash-constrained customers who could not get installment credit from banks. Installment credit is a loan repaid with periodic payments (installments) consisting of varying amounts of interest and principal, such as auto loans, home mortgages, and student loans.

GE Capital peaked in 2008 with more than $600 billion in assets and contributed nearly 60 percent of GE's profits. The financial crisis hit it hard, GE's market value fell by more than half during 2008, and its stock fell to $14.58 in November 2008, the lowest since January 1997. On November 12, 2008, the FDIC approved GE Capital as an eligible entity under the Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program (TLGP). In April 2015 GE announced the GE Capital Exit Plan to sell most of its assets, completed in March 2017, so GE Capital assets fell from over $600 billion (2008) to $119 billion (2018). In March 2021 GE combined GECAS with AerCap Holdings in a deal worth more than $30 billion, marking GE's return to being essentially an engineering organization.

Another major example is Ally Financial (formerly GMAC), founded in 1919 as the General Motors Acceptance Corporation. In November 2006 General Motors sold a 51 percent interest to a consortium led by Cerberus Capital Management. GMAC lost $8 billion in 2007-2008, and on December 24, 2008 it was approved as a bank holding company, giving it access to up to $6 billion in government bailout money. In May 2009 GMAC Bank was rebranded as Ally Bank, and in May 2010 GMAC became Ally Financial. In November 2012 the U.S. Treasury owned 73.8 percent of GMAC. Ally went public in late 2014 and exited the TARP program in December 2014.

Because of the attractive rates finance companies offer on some loans (such as new car loans), their willingness to lend to riskier borrowers than depository institutions, their often direct affiliation with manufacturing firms, and the relatively limited regulation imposed on them, finance companies have been among the fastest-growing FI groups. In the second quarter of 2021, their assets stood at $1,475.5 billion, compared with $39.6 billion at the start of 1977, growth of almost 3,627 percent over 44 years. However, total assets are down 33 percent from the peak at the beginning of 2008.

2.1 The Three Major Types of Finance Companies

  1. Sales finance institutions, such as Ford Motor Credit and Sears Roebuck Acceptance Corp., specialize in making loans to the customers of a particular retailer or manufacturer. Because they can process loans faster and more conveniently (generally at the point of purchase), this sector competes directly with depository institutions for consumer loans
  2. Personal credit institutions, such as HSBC Finance and AIG American General, specialize in making installment and other loans to consumers, including customers too risky for depository institutions. They compensate for the extra risk by charging higher interest rates and/or accepting collateral (e.g., used cars) that depository institutions do not accept
  3. Business credit institutions, such as CIT Group and U.S. Bancorp Equipment Finance, provide financing to corporations, especially through equipment leasing and factoring, in which the finance company purchases accounts receivable from corporate customers at a discount from face value and assumes the responsibility for collecting them

Many finance companies perform more than one of these services. The industry is quite concentrated, with large firms holding assets of $20 billion or more (less than 0.5 percent of all finance companies) accounting for 71 percent of industry assets. Many of the largest finance companies are wholly owned, or captive, subsidiaries of major manufacturers, such as Ford Motor Credit, which produced as much as 80 percent of Ford Motor Corporation's overall profits in the mid-2000s. A benefit of the captive subsidiary to the parent is diversification of revenue streams. The largest captive finance company before 2014 was GECGH, with up to $285.0 billion in receivables.

According to the Federal Reserve, finance companies are defined as entities that have at least 50 percent of total assets in loans or leases to consumers or businesses, and cannot be a government agency, nonprofit organization, cooperative, bank, bank holding company, credit union, or real estate investment trust.

3. Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

3.1 Assets

Business and consumer loans are the major assets of finance companies, accounting for 64.0 percent of total assets in 2021, while real estate loans are 6.2 percent. From 2007 to 2021, consumer loans declined in absolute dollar terms but increased as a percentage of assets, while real estate loans declined in both absolute and percentage terms because the financial crisis prompted lenders to reduce exposure to riskier credit. The only areas growing consistently since 2007 are consumer vehicle loans and business retail loans.

Consumer Loans

Motor vehicle loans and leases are traditionally the major type of consumer loan, reaching 32.5 percent of total assets in 2021. Finance companies historically charged higher rates on auto loans than commercial banks, but rates sometimes dropped dramatically, for example to as low as 0.9 percent in the late 1990s and even zero percent after the September 2001 attacks and during the 2008-2009 crisis to boost car sales. In 2002 finance companies charged 3.33 percent less than commercial banks on new car loans, and it was not until 2013 that the historical trend returned, with rates 0.27 percent higher than banks.

The higher rates finance companies charge on consumer loans are mostly due to the fact that they attract riskier customers than commercial banks. Even individuals with a bankruptcy on their records can obtain a loan from a subprime lender. An extreme example is Jayhawk Acceptance Corp., which financed cosmetic surgery procedures at 9.9 percent per year if repaid within a year and 13.9 percent if repaid within two years, and eventually declared bankruptcy. There are also loan sharks that prey on desperate consumers, charging exorbitant rates as high as 30 percent per year or more.

Another type of subprime lender is the payday lender, which provides short-term cash advances usually due when borrowers receive their next paycheck. A typical borrower takes a two-week loan and pays $15 for every $100 borrowed, the equivalent of a 390 percent annual interest rate, with typical earnings of $25,000 to $50,000 per year. Payday lenders generate about $50 billion in loans annually, earned about $11 billion in revenue in 2018, and numbered close to 15,400 nationwide. The industry is regulated at the state level, with 16 states and the District of Columbia capping rates at 36 percent or less in 2018, and as of July 2021, 32 states and DC impose a 36 percent limit on a $2,000, 2-year installment loan. The CFPB rule of October 2017 requiring lenders to assess ability to pay was delayed and ended in July 2020, then overturned by Congress on June 24, 2021.

Revolving and other consumer loans include personal cash loans, mobile home loans, and private-label credit card loans (e.g., Discover), and in 2021 they made up 27.5 percent of the consumer loan portfolio of finance companies.

Mortgages and Home Equity Loans

Residential and commercial mortgages have become a major component of finance company portfolios, although they did not generally deal in mortgages in the 1970s. Because they are not subject to regulations as extensive as banks', finance companies are often willing to issue mortgages to riskier borrowers and compensate by charging higher rates and fees. Mortgages can be made directly or as securitized mortgage assets, which involves pooling similar mortgages, removing them from the balance sheet, and selling interests in the pool to secondary market investors, creating mortgage-backed securities. Mortgage loans can be first mortgages or second mortgages in the form of home equity loans, which allow customers to borrow on a line of credit secured by a second mortgage on their home. Home equity loans became very profitable after the Tax Reform Act of 1986 disallowed the tax deductibility of consumer interest other than mortgage interest, and their bad debt and administrative costs are lower. The sharp rise in subprime mortgage delinquencies and defaults in 2007-2008 was the root cause of the 2008-2009 financial crisis.

Business Loans

Business loans represented 19.2 percent of the loan portfolio in 2021. Finance companies have five advantages over commercial banks in serving small business customers

  1. They are not subject to regulations restricting the types of products and services they can offer
  2. Because they do not accept deposits, they have no bank-type regulators looking directly over their shoulders
  3. As subsidiaries of corporate-sector holding companies, they often have substantial industry and product expertise
  4. They are more willing to accept risky customers than commercial banks
  5. They generally have lower overheads, for example no tellers or branches for taking deposits

The major subcategories of business loans are retail and wholesale motor vehicle loans and leases (21.2 percent of all business loans in 2021), equipment loans (57.0 percent), and other business loans (21.9 percent). An example of wholesale lending is Ford Motor Credit's floor plan financing to Ford dealers for inventory, where FMCC pays for dealer inventory, places a lien on each car, and the dealer pays periodic interest and pays for the car only when it is sold. Finance companies often prefer to lease equipment rather than sell and finance it, because repossession is less complicated when title is retained (via leasing), leases generally require no down payment, and the finance company receives a tax deduction through depreciation expense.

3.2 Liabilities and Equity

To finance asset growth, finance companies rely primarily on short-term commercial paper and other debt (longer-term notes and bonds). In December 2021, commercial paper amounted to $37.6 billion (2.2 percent of total assets), other debt securities totaled $732.6 billion (43.6 percent), and bank loans totaled $194.5 billion (11.6 percent). By comparison, in 2007 commercial paper was 5.4 percent of total financing, other debt 59.4 percent, and bank loans 8.0 percent. Much of this change reflects the strong economy and low interest rates in U.S. long-term debt markets since 2007. The capital-to-assets ratio of finance companies in 2021 was 24.9 percent, much higher than the 8.5 percent in 2007.

Because they cannot issue deposits, finance companies rely heavily on commercial paper and are now the largest issuers in the short-term commercial paper market, with maturities typically 30 days or less, although they can be issued with maturities up to 270 days. Debt owed to the parent company (e.g., FMCC's debt to Ford) is less likely to trigger legal proceedings if the subsidiary has a bad year, but the parent is susceptible to large losses of its own if the subsidiary fails.

4. Industry Performance

In the early 2000s the industry outlook was bright, with historically low interest rates, growing mortgage refinancing, and strong loan demand. Industry assets grew at almost 8 percent per year. Successful finance companies became takeover targets, for example Citigroup acquired Associates First Capital, AIG acquired American General (May 2001), and HSBC Holdings acquired Household International for $14.9 billion (2003), one of the largest mergers of that year.

However, in the mid- and late 2000s, problems among lenders specializing in lower-quality customers created large losses. At the end of 2006, the percentage of subprime mortgages at least 60 days late was 14 percent, up from 6 percent in early 2005. Subprime mortgage originations declined 30-35 percent in 2007 from about $600 billion in 2006, about one-fifth of the entire mortgage market. Shares of New Century Financial, the number-two subprime lender, plunged nearly 79 percent in early March 2007 and the NYSE suspended trading on March 13, 2007. Fremont General lost one-third of its value, Countrywide Financial lost over half its market value and survived only thanks to a $2 billion equity investment from Bank of America in 2007 and an acquisition offer in 2008, and HSBC (the number-one subprime lender) took a $10.6 million charge for bad loans in 2006.

By the end of 2009, mortgage delinquencies had increased for the 12th straight quarter, hitting an all-time national average high of 6.89 percent, meaning more than 7.2 million mortgage loans were behind on payments. Small business loan failure rates hit 11.9 percent in 2009 (versus 2.4 percent in 2004), and the national default rate on commercial real estate mortgages rose to 2.25 percent from 1.62 percent in the fourth quarter of 2008. The crisis caused the failure of Countrywide, the forced conversion of GMAC to a bank holding company, and the Chapter 11 bankruptcy of CIT Group in November 2009, which had assets of $71 billion and liabilities of $65 billion, with the bankruptcy eliminating $10 billion of debt including $2.3 billion extended in the 2008 taxpayer bailout.

As the economy improved in the late 2000s and early 2010s, the industry recovered. In the mid-2010s, industry median return on equity (ROE) rose to 9.33 percent, up from 6.61 percent at the height of the crisis, and for business credit institutions ROE rose to 13.73 percent, up from 5.31 percent. Industry assets reached $1.82 trillion (up from $1.59 trillion during the crisis), but receivables fell to $1.43 trillion (from $1.93 trillion) and employment shrank to 562,400 (from 715,900).

5. Regulation

The Federal Reserve defines a finance company as a firm (other than a depository institution) whose primary assets are loans to individuals and businesses. Like depository institutions, finance companies are financial intermediaries that borrow funds for relending and profit from the difference between borrowing and lending rates, and they are subject to state usury ceilings and rules on collecting delinquent loans. However, because they do not accept deposits, they are not subject to extensive federal or state oversight as banks and thrifts are, even though they offer services that compete directly with depository institutions. This lack of oversight allows them to offer a wide scope of bank-like services while avoiding regulatory compliance costs such as the Community Reinvestment Act of 1977.

Because of the impact of nonbank FIs during the financial crisis and the need for the Federal Reserve to rescue several of them (GMAC, GE Capital, CIT Group), the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 gave the government tools to manage financial crises, namely a new regime to resolve nonbank FIs whose failure could have systemic effects, revisions to the Federal Reserve's emergency lending authority, the creation of the Financial Services Oversight Council to identify systemic risks, new authority for the Federal Reserve to supervise firms posing a threat to financial stability, and stronger capital and prudential standards for all financial firms. American Express, Discover Financial, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley also became bank holding companies in 2008.

Because they are heavy borrowers in capital markets and do not enjoy the same regulatory safety net as banks (no deposit insurance, no access to the discount window), finance companies need to signal solvency and safety to investors, usually by holding higher capital-to-asset ratios and therefore lower leverage than banks. In 2021, the capital-to-assets ratio of finance companies was 24.9 percent, compared with 10.0 percent for commercial banks. Larger captive finance companies also use default protection guarantees from their parents and/or letters of credit or lines of credit purchased from high-quality banks as additional protection against insolvency risk, so larger finance companies tend to operate with lower capital-asset ratios than smaller ones.

6. Global Issues

While commercial banks are the most important source of credit in many countries, especially emerging market economies, nonbank financial institutions (finance companies, credit unions, and building societies) account for a substantial part of outstanding credit, and their relative importance has been increasing. From the mid-1990s to the 2010s, the percentage of aggregate credit issued by nonbank institutions increased from 22 to 32 percent in Latin America and from 4 to 17 percent in central Europe. In Thailand, nonbanks specializing in credit card lending gained market share, and in Mexico specialized mortgage institutions dominated low-income mortgage lending. Because regulations in most foreign countries are not as restrictive as in the U.S., foreign finance companies are generally subsidiaries of commercial banks or industrial firms, so their fortunes follow their parents. In New Zealand the crisis triggered consolidation, collapse, and restructuring of many finance companies, while in Russia significant staff reductions occurred.

7. Key Calculations and Formulas

7.1 Industry Asset Growth

Finance company assets grew from $39.6 billion (1977) to $1,475.5 billion (second quarter 2021).

\[ \text{Growth} = \frac{TA_{2021} - TA_{1977}}{TA_{1977}} \times 100\% = \frac{1{,}475.5 - 39.6}{39.6} \times 100\% \approx 3{,}627\% \]

7.2 Effective Annual Rate of a Payday Loan

A borrower pays $15 for every $100 borrowed over two weeks.

\[ APR = \frac{15}{100} \times \frac{365}{14} \approx 3.90 = 390\% \]

where \( APR \) is the effective annual percentage rate. This is the basis of the criticism that payday loans are extremely expensive.

7.3 Rate Difference between Banks and Finance Companies

\[ \text{Difference} = r_{\text{bank}} - r_{\text{finance}} \]

A positive value means banks are more expensive, a negative value means finance companies are cheaper, for example -3.33 percent in 2002 (due to zero percent programs of captive finance companies) and +0.27 percent in 2013.

7.4 Capital-to-Assets Ratio

\[ \frac{C}{TA} = 24.9\% \quad (2021, \text{finance companies}) \quad \text{versus} \quad 10.0\% \quad (\text{commercial banks}) \]

The higher ratio signals solvency to investors because finance companies lack a deposit safety net.

7.5 Return on Equity

\[ ROE = \frac{NI}{E} \]

Industry median ROE rose from 6.61 percent (height of the crisis) to 9.33 percent (mid-2010s), and for business credit institutions from 5.31 percent to 13.73 percent.

7.6 Other Key Data

  • Industry assets, $1,475.5 billion (Q2 2021), down 33 percent from the early 2008 peak
  • Industry concentration, firms with $20 billion+ assets (less than 0.5 percent of all companies) hold 71 percent of assets
  • 2021 asset composition, consumer and business loans 64.0 percent, real estate 6.2 percent
  • 2021 funding, commercial paper 2.2 percent, other debt securities 43.6 percent, bank loans 11.6 percent
  • Subprime mortgage delinquency 60+ days, 14 percent (end 2006) versus 6 percent (early 2005)
  • National mortgage delinquency, record 6.89 percent (end 2009), over 7.2 million loans
  • Small business loan failure rate, 11.9 percent (2009) versus 2.4 percent (2004)

8. Summary and Conclusion

This chapter provided an overview of the finance company industry, which competes directly with depository institutions for prime loan customers by specializing in consumer, real estate, and business loans, while also serving subprime (high-risk) borrowers deemed too risky for most depository institutions. Because they do not accept deposits, finance companies are not regulated to the same extent as depository institutions and rely heavily on short- and long-term debt, especially commercial paper. The industry is generally growing and profitable, although the subprime lending sector has experienced problems as consumer default rates rise.

The key lesson of this chapter is that the no-deposit business model brings a trade-off, namely regulatory freedom and product flexibility as advantages, but dependence on market funding and exposure to risky borrowers as weaknesses, proven during the 2008-2009 crisis through the failures of Countrywide, GMAC, and CIT Group. High capital ratios and parent guarantees are the key mechanisms for maintaining investor confidence.
