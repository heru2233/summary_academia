1. Introduction and Overview of FI Risks

The primary objective of financial institution (FI) management is to increase returns for the FI's owners. This objective, however, is typically pursued at the cost of increased risk exposure. The central business of modern FIs can be understood as the active management of a complex portfolio of interrelated risks. This chapter provides a foundational overview of the major risk categories that FI managers must identify, measure, monitor, and control. These risks include: interest rate risk, credit risk, liquidity risk, foreign exchange risk, country or sovereign risk, market risk, off-balance-sheet risk, technology risk, operational risk, risk of digital disruption and fintech, and insolvency risk.

Over the past decade, U.S. financial institution profitability has generally been robust, but the risks of financial intermediation have increased as economies have become more integrated. Uncertainty from international events such as Brexit and tariff wars has created significant risks for FIs operating in foreign markets. Even domestically focused FIs can be exposed to foreign exchange and sovereign risk if their domestic customers have business dealings with foreign countries. Consequently, FI managers must devote substantial resources to understanding and managing the diverse risks to which their institutions are exposed.

2. Interest Rate Risk

Interest Rate Risk: The risk incurred by an FI when the maturities of its assets and liabilities are mismatched. This risk arises fundamentally from the asset transformation function of FIs, which involves buying primary securities (assets) with certain maturity and liquidity characteristics and issuing secondary securities (liabilities) with different characteristics. When an FI mismatches the maturities of its assets and liabilities as part of this function, it exposes itself to potential losses from changes in interest rates.

Interest rate risk manifests in two primary forms: refinancing risk and reinvestment risk, as well as through market value risk. These are distinct but related channels through which interest rate changes affect an FI's profitability and economic value.

2.1 Refinancing Risk

Refinancing Risk: The risk that the cost of rolling over or reborrowing funds will rise above the returns being earned on asset investments. This risk arises when an FI holds longer-term assets relative to its liabilities (i.e., the FI is "short-funded" because the maturity of its liabilities is less than the maturity of its assets).

Example 7–1 (Source): Impact of an Interest Rate Increase on an FI's Profits When the Maturity of Its Assets Exceeds the Maturity of Its Liabilities

Setup: An FI issues $100 million of liabilities with one-year maturity to finance the purchase of $100 million of assets with a two-year maturity. The cost of funds (liabilities) is 9% per year, and the return on assets is 10% per year.

Year 1: The FI locks in a profit spread of 1% (10% – 9%) × $100 million = $1 million profit.

Year 2 (if rates rise): If interest rates rise and the FI can borrow new one-year liabilities only at 11% in the second year, the profit spread becomes negative: 10% – 11% = –1%, resulting in a loss of $1 million.

Interpretation: The positive spread earned in the first year is offset by a negative spread in the second year. If rates rise by more than 1%, the FI would suffer net losses over the two-year period as a whole.

Practical Lesson: When an FI holds longer-term assets relative to liabilities, it is exposed to refinancing risk—the risk that the cost of rolling over funds will exceed the return on assets. This was exemplified by banks that borrowed short-term deposits with frequently adjusting rates while investing in fixed-rate loans with infrequently adjusting rates.

Profit Spread and Dollar Profit (Refinancing Risk Context)
\[ \text{Profit Spread} = \text{Return on Assets} - \text{Cost of Funds} \]
\[ \text{Dollar Profit} = \text{Profit Spread} \times \text{Amount of Assets} \]

Symbols and Interpretation: Return on Assets is the percentage yield earned on the FI's asset investments (e.g., 10%). Cost of Funds is the percentage cost of the FI's liabilities (e.g., 9%). Profit Spread is the difference (e.g., 1%). Dollar Profit is the spread multiplied by the amount of assets (e.g., 0.01 × $100 million = $1 million).

Practical Use: This simple calculation is used to evaluate the immediate profitability of the asset transformation function. It is a static measure that does not account for future uncertainty.

Assumption: The calculation assumes no change in the cost of funds or return on assets within the period. The risk is that these rates change when the liability is refinanced.

2.2 Reinvestment Risk

Reinvestment Risk: The risk that the return on funds to be reinvested will fall below the cost of funds. This risk arises when an FI holds shorter-term assets relative to its liabilities (i.e., the FI is "long-funded" because the maturity of its liabilities exceeds the maturity of its assets).

Example 7–2 (Source): Impact of an Interest Rate Decrease When the Maturity of an FI's Liabilities Exceeds the Maturity of Its Assets

Setup: An FI borrows $100 million at 9% per year for two years and invests the funds in assets that yield 10% for one year.

Year 1: The FI locks in a profit spread of 1%, or $1 million.

Year 2 (if rates fall): At the end of the first year, the assets mature. The funds borrowed for two years must be reinvested. If interest rates fall to 8%, the FI faces a negative spread of 1% (8% asset return – 9% cost of funds), resulting in a loss of $1 million.

Interpretation: The positive spread in the first year is offset by a negative spread in the second year. The FI is exposed to reinvestment risk—the uncertainty about the interest rate at which it can reinvest funds borrowed for a longer period.

Practical Lesson: This was exemplified by banks that borrowed fixed-rate deposits while investing in floating-rate loans whose interest rates adjusted frequently.

Profit Spread and Dollar Profit (Reinvestment Risk Context)
\[ \text{Profit Spread} = \text{Return on Reinvested Assets} - \text{Cost of Funds} \]
\[ \text{Dollar Profit} = \text{Profit Spread} \times \text{Amount of Assets} \]

Symbols and Interpretation: Return on Reinvested Assets is the yield on new assets purchased with the proceeds of maturing assets. Cost of Funds remains the fixed cost of the longer-term liabilities. The risk is that the reinvestment rate falls below the cost of funds.

Practical Use: This calculation helps FIs assess the sensitivity of their profitability to changes in reinvestment rates. It is particularly relevant for FIs that fund long-term liabilities with short-term assets.

Important Distinction: Refinancing risk is about the cost of new liabilities rising; reinvestment risk is about the return on new assets falling.

2.3 Market Value Risk

Market Value Risk: The risk that changes in interest rates affect the market (fair) values of assets and liabilities differently due to maturity mismatches. The market value of an asset or liability equals the present value of its current and future cash flows. Rising interest rates increase the discount rate on those cash flows, reducing market values; falling rates increase market values. When an FI holds longer-term assets than liabilities, a rise in interest rates causes the market value of assets to fall by a greater amount than the market value of liabilities, potentially threatening solvency.

Key Insight

Market value risk reflects economic reality—the true value of assets and liabilities if the FI's portfolio were liquidated at today's securities prices—rather than at historical purchase prices. It considers the degree of leverage on the balance sheet as well as the timing of cash flows. Unlike refinancing or reinvestment risk, which focus on income flows, market value risk focuses on the economic value of the FI's net worth.

2.4 Maturity Matching as a Hedge

If holding assets and liabilities with mismatched maturities exposes FIs to reinvestment (or refinancing) and market value risks, FIs can seek to hedge interest rate risk by matching the maturity of their assets and liabilities. However, matching maturities is not necessarily consistent with an active asset-transformation function. FIs cannot be asset transformers (e.g., transforming short-term deposits into long-term loans) and direct balance sheet matchers at the same time. While reducing interest rate risk, maturity matching may reduce profitability because returns from acting as specialized risk-bearing asset transformers are reduced.

Furthermore, matching maturities hedges interest rate risk only in an approximate rather than complete fashion. The reasons are technical, relating to the difference between the average life (or duration) and maturity of an asset or liability, and whether the FI partly funds its assets with equity capital in addition to liabilities. When assets and liabilities are not equal, hedging risk (i.e., insulating stockholders' equity values) may be achieved by not exactly matching maturities.

Analogy

Interest rate risk is like walking a tightrope with a long pole that is unevenly balanced. If the pole (assets) is longer on one side than the other (liabilities), a gust of wind (interest rate change) will twist the pole and destabilize you. Matching the lengths creates balance, but it also prevents you from reaching farther (the asset transformation function).

Key Insight

Maturity matching is a trade-off: it reduces interest rate risk but also reduces the profitability potential from asset transformation. Different types of FIs make different choices on this spectrum. Depository institutions traditionally hold longer-term assets than liabilities, while life insurers tend to match the long-term nature of their liabilities with long-term assets.

Key Takeaways

  • Interest rate risk arises from mismatched maturities of assets and liabilities; it is inherent in the asset transformation function of FIs.
  • Refinancing risk occurs when long-term assets are funded by short-term liabilities and rates rise.
  • Reinvestment risk occurs when short-term assets are funded by long-term liabilities and rates fall.
  • Market value risk reflects the impact of rate changes on the present value of assets and liabilities, directly affecting economic net worth.
  • Maturity matching can hedge interest rate risk but limits asset transformation profitability.
  • Perfect hedging requires matching durations (average lives), not just maturities, and accounting for leverage.

3. Credit Risk

Credit Risk: The risk that promised cash flows from loans and securities held by FIs may not be paid in full. This is the risk of default by borrowers on their contractual obligations, including both principal and interest payments. Virtually all types of FIs face this risk, but those that make loans or buy bonds with long maturities are more exposed than those with short maturities. For example, depository institutions and life insurers are more exposed to credit risk than money market mutual funds and property–casualty insurers.

If all borrowers paid principal and interest in full on the promised dates, FIs would face no credit risk. However, when a borrower defaults, both the principal loaned and the expected interest payments are at risk. Financial claims such as corporate bonds and bank loans have a limited or fixed upside return (principal and interest payments) with a high probability and a large downside risk (loss of principal and promised interest) with a much smaller probability.

Key Insight

A key role of FIs involves screening and monitoring loan applicants to ensure that FIs fund the most creditworthy loans. Managerial monitoring efficiency and credit risk management strategies directly affect the return and risk of the loan portfolio.

3.1 Firm-Specific vs. Systematic Credit Risk

Firm-Specific Credit Risk: The risk of default of the borrowing firm associated with the specific types of project risk taken by that firm. This risk is unique to individual borrowers or industries.

Systematic Credit Risk: The risk of default associated with general economywide or macro conditions affecting all borrowers. Factors such as an economic recession increase the default risk of all firms simultaneously.

One of the advantages FIs have over individual investors is the ability to diversify some credit risk by exploiting the law of large numbers in their asset portfolios. Diversification across assets reduces the overall credit risk in the portfolio, increasing the probability of partial or full repayment of principal and interest. The effect of diversification is to truncate the probabilities of bad outcomes. However, diversification reduces only firm-specific credit risk; systematic credit risk remains.

Empirical Evidence: Charge-Off Rates (Figure 7–1 from Source)

Commercial bank charge-off rates for various loan types from 1984 to 2021 show that credit card loans consistently had the highest charge-off rates, with peaks during the early 2000s recession and the 2007–2010 financial crisis. The data illustrate the cyclical nature of credit risk, with charge-offs rising during economic downturns and falling during recoveries.

Interpretation: Credit risk is not static; it varies with economic conditions, borrower behavior, and regulatory changes. FIs must continuously monitor and adjust their credit risk management strategies.

Analogy

Credit risk is like lending an umbrella to a friend. If the weather is good (strong economy), you get the umbrella back. If a storm comes (recession), the friend might lose the umbrella or be unable to return it. Diversifying across many friends in different locations reduces the chance that all umbrellas are lost simultaneously.

Key Insight

Diversification reduces firm-specific credit risk but does not eliminate systematic credit risk. Even a well-diversified loan portfolio will experience losses during an economywide recession. The maximum dollar return occurs when all loans pay off; the mean return is lower due to defaults, and diversification limits the probability of extreme losses.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit risk is the risk of default on promised cash flows from loans and bonds.
  • FIs with longer-maturity assets are more exposed to credit risk.
  • Firm-specific credit risk can be reduced through diversification; systematic credit risk cannot.
  • Screening and monitoring are essential tools for managing credit risk.
  • Credit risk is cyclical, with charge-off rates rising during economic downturns.

4. Liquidity Risk

Liquidity Risk: The risk that a sudden surge in liability withdrawals may require an FI to liquidate assets in a very short period of time and at less than fair market prices. Liquidity risk also arises when holders of off-balance-sheet loan commitments (credit lines) suddenly exercise their right to borrow. The most liquid asset is cash, but FIs limit cash holdings because cash earns no interest. Day-to-day withdrawals are generally predictable and can be met through normal borrowing.

A liquidity crisis occurs when liability holders demand abnormally large withdrawals due to a lack of confidence in the FI or some unexpected need for cash. When many FIs face abnormally large cash demands, the cost of purchased funds rises and the supply of such funds becomes restricted. FIs may then have to sell less liquid assets at fire-sale prices—prices received when an asset must be liquidated immediately at less than its fair market value—which can threaten profitability and solvency.

Example 7–3 (Source): Impact of Liquidity Risk on an FI's Equity Value

Setup: An FI has $10 million in cash assets and $90 million in nonliquid assets (small business loans), funded by $90 million in deposits and $10 million in equity.

Event: Depositors unexpectedly withdraw $15 million in deposits. The FI first uses its $10 million in cash, then must sell nonliquid assets to raise an additional $5 million. Because it cannot borrow more funds and needs cash immediately, the FI must sell nonliquid assets at 50 cents on the dollar. To raise $5 million, it must sell $10 million in face value of nonliquid assets, incurring a $5 million loss.

Result: The loss is written off against equity, reducing equity from $10 million to $5 million.

Interpretation: Liquidity risk can directly impair an FI's capital position when assets must be sold at fire-sale prices to meet withdrawal demands.

Key Insight

Liquidity risk can trigger a solvency crisis even if the FI is otherwise profitable. The forced sale of assets at fire-sale prices destroys capital. The IndyMac bank failure in July 2008 is a real-world example: a bank run persisted even after the FDIC took over, because depositors feared losing access to their funds despite deposit insurance.

Analogy

Liquidity risk is like having most of your wealth tied up in a house and needing to sell it quickly to pay an unexpected large bill. If you have only a few days to sell, you will likely have to accept a much lower price than if you could wait for the right buyer.

Key Takeaways

  • Liquidity risk arises from sudden large withdrawals or drawdowns of loan commitments.
  • Forced sale of illiquid assets at fire-sale prices can destroy capital and threaten solvency.
  • Even insured deposits may not prevent a bank run if depositors fear delays in accessing funds.
  • Liquidity risk is closely related to confidence: loss of confidence can trigger a crisis.

5. Foreign Exchange Risk

Foreign Exchange Risk: The risk that exchange rate changes can adversely affect the value of an FI's assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies. As FIs have become more global, they hold increasing proportions of foreign assets and liabilities. The returns on domestic and foreign investments are not perfectly correlated because (1) the underlying technologies and economic structures of different economies vary, and (2) exchange rate changes are not perfectly correlated across countries.

Foreign exchange risk arises from holding assets and liabilities denominated in a foreign currency. A U.S. FI that makes a loan in British pounds (£) will find that if the pound depreciates against the dollar, the principal and interest payments are devalued in dollar terms. If the pound falls far enough, the overall return could be negative—foreign exchange losses can offset the promised local currency interest payments.

5.1 Net Long and Net Short Foreign Asset Positions

Net Long Position in Foreign Assets: When an FI holds more foreign assets than foreign liabilities in a given currency. For example, a U.S. FI holds £100 million in pound loans and funds £80 million with pound CDs, with the remaining £20 million funded by dollar CDs. The FI is net long £20 million in pound assets. The FI suffers losses if the pound depreciates against the dollar—the value of pound assets falls more than the value of pound liabilities.

Net Short Position in Foreign Assets: When an FI holds more foreign liabilities than foreign assets in a given currency. For example, a U.S. FI has £20 million more pound liabilities than assets. The FI is exposed to foreign exchange risk if the pound appreciates against the dollar—the value of pound liabilities rises faster than the return on pound assets.

Key Insight

To be approximately hedged against foreign exchange risk, an FI must match the size of its assets and liabilities in each foreign currency. However, even if the size is matched, the FI may still be exposed to foreign interest rate risk if the maturities are mismatched. For example, if pound assets have six-month maturity and pound liabilities have three-month maturity, the FI faces the risk that British interest rates will rise when it rolls over the liabilities. Full hedging requires matching both the size and the maturities (or durations) of assets and liabilities in each currency.

Net Foreign Asset Exposure (Example)
\[ \text{Net Exposure (Long)} = \text{Foreign Assets} - \text{Foreign Liabilities} \]

Symbols and Interpretation: Foreign Assets are the total value of assets denominated in a foreign currency (e.g., £100 million). Foreign Liabilities are the total value of liabilities denominated in that currency (e.g., £80 million). A positive net exposure (long position) means the FI gains if the foreign currency appreciates and loses if it depreciates. A negative net exposure (short position) means the opposite.

Practical Use: FIs monitor their net exposure in each foreign currency to assess potential gains or losses from exchange rate movements. Hedging strategies involve matching asset and liability amounts in each currency and, ideally, their maturities.

Analogy

Foreign exchange risk is like having a savings account in a foreign currency. If the exchange rate moves against you, the dollar value of your savings decreases even though the local currency amount stays the same. Mismatching assets and liabilities in different currencies is like having a loan in one currency and savings in another—you are exposed to exchange rate movements between the two.

Key Takeaways

  • Foreign exchange risk arises from holding assets and liabilities in nondomestic currencies.
  • A net long position in a foreign currency means the FI gains if that currency appreciates and loses if it depreciates.
  • A net short position means the FI gains if the currency depreciates and loses if it appreciates.
  • Full hedging requires matching both the size and maturities of foreign currency assets and liabilities.
  • Diversification across currencies can reduce overall foreign exchange risk if exchange rate movements are not perfectly correlated.

6. Country or Sovereign Risk

Country or Sovereign Risk: The risk that repayments from foreign borrowers may be interrupted because of restrictions, intervention, or interference from foreign governments. This is a special type of credit risk faced by FIs that purchase assets such as bonds and loans of foreign corporations or governments. Unlike domestic defaults, where the FI has recourse to domestic bankruptcy courts, foreign borrowers may be unable or unwilling to repay even if they desire to do so, because their government may prohibit or limit debt payments due to foreign currency shortages or adverse political reasons.

Historical Examples (Source)

Argentina (2001–2002): Argentina defaulted on $130 billion in government-issued debt and passed legislation that led to defaults on $30 billion of corporate debt owed to foreign creditors. The country's economic problems continued into the 2000s and 2010s.

Greece (2012): Greek government debtholders lost 53.5% of their $265 billion investment as Greece restructured its sovereign debt, producing the largest-ever sovereign debt default.

Interpretation: In such events, the FI claimholder has little recourse to local bankruptcy courts or international civil claims courts. The major leverage available to an FI is its control over the future supply of loans or funds to the country, but this leverage may be very weak in the face of a collapsing currency and government.

Key Insight

Sovereign risk is distinct from ordinary credit risk because the borrower's government can unilaterally restrict or prohibit debt payments, and the FI has limited legal recourse. This risk is particularly acute when the borrowing country faces foreign currency shortages or political instability.

Key Takeaways

  • Sovereign risk is the risk that a foreign government will restrict or prohibit debt repayments.
  • Legal recourse is limited compared to domestic defaults.
  • An FI's primary leverage is the future supply of credit, which may be insufficient.
  • Sovereign risk can lead to massive losses, as seen in Argentina and Greece.

7. Market Risk

Market Risk: The risk incurred in the trading of assets and liabilities due to changes in interest rates, exchange rates, and other asset prices. Market risk is closely related to interest rate risk, credit risk, and foreign exchange risk, but it adds another dimension arising from active trading activity, especially over short trading horizons such as a day. Market risk is the incremental risk incurred when these risks are combined with an active trading strategy.

7.1 Trading Book vs. Banking Book

Trading Book: Contains assets, liabilities, and derivative contracts that can be quickly bought or sold on organized financial markets. These are held for short-term trading purposes. Examples include long and short positions in bonds, commodities, foreign exchange, equities, and derivatives.

Banking Book (Investment Portfolio): Contains assets and liabilities that are relatively illiquid and held for longer holding periods. For banks, this includes the majority of loans and deposits plus other illiquid assets.

With the increasing securitization of bank loans, more assets have become liquid and tradable. While bank regulators have traditionally viewed tradable assets as those held for horizons of less than one year, private FIs take an even shorter-term view, focusing on daily fluctuations in the value of trading account assets and liabilities. The more volatile the asset prices, the greater the market risk faced by FIs that adopt open trading positions.

Extreme Case: Financial Crisis of 2008–2009

Investment banks and securities firms were major purchasers and traders of mortgages in the early 2000s. Rising interest rates in the mid-2000s led to significant increases in mortgage defaults, freezing the securitized mortgage market. FIs were left holding "toxic" assets at deeply reduced market values. This illustrates that market risk is present whenever an FI takes an open or unhedged long or short position and prices move in the opposite direction.

Key Insight

Market risk is the risk of losses from adverse price movements in actively traded positions. It requires FI management to establish controls to limit trader positions and to develop models to measure market risk exposure on a day-to-day basis.

Key Takeaways

  • Market risk arises from active trading of assets, liabilities, and derivatives over short horizons.
  • The trading book contains liquid, short-term positions; the banking book contains illiquid, long-term holdings.
  • Market risk is magnified by asset price volatility and open (unhedged) positions.
  • The 2008–2009 financial crisis is an extreme example of market risk realization.
  • Risk measurement models and position limits are essential for managing market risk.

8. Off-Balance-Sheet Risk

Off-Balance-Sheet Risk: The risk incurred by an FI due to activities related to contingent assets and liabilities held off the balance sheet. Off-balance-sheet (OBS) activities do not appear on the current balance sheet because they do not involve holding a current primary claim (asset) or issuing a current secondary claim (liability). Instead, they affect the future shape of the balance sheet by creating contingent assets and liabilities that may become actual assets and liabilities in the future.

Contingent Assets and Liabilities: Assets and liabilities off the balance sheet that potentially produce positive or negative cash flows for an FI. These are contractual claims that directly impact the economic value of the equity holders' stake in the FI.

Letter of Credit: A credit guarantee issued by an FI for a fee, on which payment is contingent on some future event occurring. For example, a standby letter of credit backs the issuance of municipal bonds. If the municipality defaults, the FI must pay the bondholders. The fee income appears on the income statement, but the contingent liability is off the balance sheet until triggered.

Example 7–4 (Source): Impact of Off-Balance-Sheet Risk on an FI's Equity Value

Panel A (Traditional Valuation): E = A – L = 100 – 90 = 10. The FI's net worth is 10, and the capital-to-assets ratio is 10%.

Panel B (Valuation with OBS): E = (A – L) + (CA – CL) = (100 – 90) + (50 – 55) = 5. The contingent assets (CA) are 50, and contingent liabilities (CL) are 55. Because CL exceed CA by 5, this difference is an additional claim on the FI's net worth, reducing true economic net worth from 10 to 5.

Interpretation: Economically, contingent assets and liabilities are contractual claims that directly impact the equity holders' stake. Large increases in OBS liabilities can render the FI economically insolvent just as effectively as losses from on-balance-sheet activities.

Net Worth with Off-Balance-Sheet Activities
\[ E = (A - L) + (CA - CL) \]

Symbols and Interpretation: E is the economic net worth (equity) of the FI. A is the market value of on-balance-sheet assets. L is the market value of on-balance-sheet liabilities. CA is the current market value of contingent assets (off-balance-sheet). CL is the current market value of contingent liabilities (off-balance-sheet).

Practical Use: This formula provides a more complete picture of the FI's economic solvency than the traditional balance sheet alone. Regulators and FI managers should consider both on- and off-balance-sheet activities when assessing capital adequacy.

Important Assumption: The market values of contingent assets and liabilities must be estimated, which can be complex and subjective. The example assumes CA = 50 and CL = 55 for illustration.

Key Insight

Off-balance-sheet activities generate fee income without expanding the balance sheet, but they carry real risk. The ability to earn fee income without loading up the balance sheet motivates FIs to pursue OBS business, but mismanagement or speculative use of these instruments (e.g., credit default swaps) can lead to major losses and even failure, as seen during the 2008–2009 financial crisis.

Analogy

Off-balance-sheet risk is like cosigning a loan for a friend. The loan does not appear on your balance sheet, but if the friend defaults, you are liable. The fee you charge for cosigning is income, but the risk is real and can become a large liability if the friend fails to pay.

Key Takeaways

  • Off-balance-sheet activities create contingent assets and liabilities that affect future balance sheets.
  • Economic net worth must consider both on- and off-balance-sheet items: E = (A – L) + (CA – CL).
  • Large off-balance-sheet liabilities can render an FI economically insolvent.
  • OBS activities generate fee income but carry real risk, as demonstrated in the 2008–2009 crisis.
  • Examples of OBS activities include letters of credit, loan commitments, and derivative positions.

9. Technology and Operational Risk

Technology Risk: Risks including the strategic risk of not embracing new technology, not investing in the right technology, cybersecurity risk, technology vendor and third-party risk, and ineffective risk management. The major objectives of technological expansion are to lower operating costs, increase profits, and capture new markets.

Operational Risk: The risk that existing technology, auditing, monitoring, and other support systems may malfunction or break down. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) defines operational risk (inclusive of technological risk) as "the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems or from external events." Some FIs add reputational risk and strategic risk as part of a broader definition.

9.1 Economies of Scale and Scope

Economies of Scale: The degree to which an FI's average unit costs of producing financial services fall as its output of services increases. Technology enables larger-scale operations at lower per-unit cost.

Economies of Scope: The degree to which an FI can generate cost synergies by producing multiple financial service products. For example, an FI could use the same customer information stored in its computers to expand the sale of both loan products and insurance products.

Technology risks include cybersecurity risk (cyber risk), which is an umbrella term for a wide range of risks resulting from the failure or breach of IT systems. As FIs embrace third-party service providers, risks associated with these vendors proliferate. During the Covid-19 pandemic, the shift to remote working increased the risk of cyber attacks and dependencies on third-party providers. The financial sector has been hit relatively more often by cyber attacks than most other sectors since the pandemic began.

Examples of Operational Risk Events (Source)

FBI Alert (August 2018): The FBI warned banks that cybercriminals were preparing to carry out a global "ATM cash-out" fraud scheme, where thieves use cloned cards to withdraw millions of dollars in hours. The FBI urged banks to review security measures such as strong password requirements and two-factor authentication.

Target Stores (2013): Hackers infected the company's payment card readers, stealing 110 million records during the Christmas shopping season.

JPMorgan Chase "London Whale" (2012): Trader Bruno Iksil took large credit default swap positions that resulted in $6.2 billion in losses. The trades were too large for the market, and when the position was discovered, hedge funds traded against it, forcing JPMorgan to close at a loss. This event damaged the bank's reputation and led to losses.

Key Insight

Operational risk is not exclusively the result of technological failure; employee fraud and errors constitute a major type of operational risk that often negatively affects the reputation of an FI. The Federal Reserve defines reputational risk as the potential that negative publicity regarding an institution's business practices, whether true or not, will cause a decline in the customer base, costly litigation, or revenue reductions.

Key Takeaways

  • Technology risk includes strategic risk, cybersecurity risk, vendor risk, and data management risk.
  • Operational risk encompasses failures in internal processes, people, systems, and external events.
  • Economies of scale and scope are key benefits of technological expansion.
  • Cyber risk is growing in importance, especially with remote work and third-party dependencies.
  • Employee fraud and errors, as in the London Whale case, are significant operational risks.

10. Risk of Digital Disruption and Fintech

Risk of Digital Disruption and Fintech: The risk that an FI fails to successfully redefine and innovate how it creates, delivers, and captures value; and to compete against new disruptive players in the sector. This risk arises from the rapid evolution of financial technology (fintech) and the entry of new competitors, including fintech firms and big tech companies, that offer digital financial services.

Fintech (Financial Technology): Technology-enabled innovation in financial services that could result in new business models, applications, processes, or products with an associated material effect on the provision of financial services. Examples include mobile money, peer-to-peer (P2P) or marketplace lending, robo-advice, insurance technology (insurtech), and digital currencies.

Big Tech Firm: A large company whose primary activity is digital services (e.g., search engines, social media platforms, e-commerce platforms, ride-hailing platforms, and mobile network operators).

Emerging technologies such as digital currencies (e.g., bitcoin, ether) and blockchain provide systems that support the exchange of value between parties unknown to each other without the need for financial intermediaries. By reducing transaction costs, fintech supports models of peer-to-peer mass collaboration that could make many existing organizational forms redundant. However, investing in fintech may enable incumbent FIs to do more with less, streamline their businesses, and reduce risk.

Examples of Fintech Companies (Source)

Stripe: Online payment platform, valued at $95 billion. Klarna: Financing for customers at stores like IKEA, H&M, and Etsy, valued at $31 billion. Kraken: Cryptocurrency exchange, valued at $20 billion.

Key Insight

Traditional FIs have large teams of highly skilled employees with years of experience in risk management, while fintech firms tend to have small teams using risk management processes developed over a relatively short time frame that are untested in dealing with major risk events. A risk event at a fintech firm can result in losses that are substantially damaging to firm value. There is less history to create a protocol for a risk event and to provide confidence that the firm can manage through a problem.

Key Takeaways

  • Digital disruption risk is the risk of failing to innovate and compete against fintech and big tech firms.
  • Fintech includes mobile money, P2P lending, robo-advice, insurtech, and digital currencies.
  • Big tech firms leverage economies of scale and network effects in digital platforms.
  • Traditional FIs have more experienced risk management teams; fintech firms have less tested processes.
  • Adoption of new technologies requires substantial expenditures and adaptation to evolving standards.

11. Insolvency Risk

Insolvency Risk: The risk that an FI may not have enough capital to offset a sudden decline in the value of its assets relative to its liabilities. Insolvency occurs when the capital or equity resources of an FI's owners are driven to, or near to, zero because of losses incurred as a result of one or more of the other risks described in this chapter. Insolvency risk is a consequence or outcome of the interaction of interest rate, credit, liquidity, foreign exchange, sovereign, market, off-balance-sheet, technology, operational, and digital disruption risks.

Examples of Insolvency Risk (Source)

Washington Mutual (WaMu): Incurred heavy losses from on- and off-balance-sheet holdings during the financial crisis. By early September 2008, WaMu's market capital was worth only $3.5 billion, down from $43 billion at the end of 2006. In September 2008, the bank was taken over by the FDIC and sold to JPMorgan Chase.

Citigroup (2009): Citigroup's stock price fell below $1 per share, and the once-largest bank in the United States was near failure. It received a substantial government guarantee against losses (up to $306 billion) and a $20 billion injection of cash to prevent failure, illustrating the "too big to fail" phenomenon.

Key Insight

The more equity capital relative to borrowed funds an FI has (i.e., the lower its leverage), the better able it is to withstand losses from any source. Both management and regulators focus on an FI's capital adequacy as a key measure of its ability to remain solvent and grow in the face of multiple risk exposures.

Key Takeaways

  • Insolvency risk is the ultimate risk that arises when capital is insufficient to absorb losses from other risks.
  • It is a consequence of the interaction of all other risk types.
  • Higher capital (lower leverage) provides greater resilience against insolvency.
  • Regulators monitor capital adequacy as a key measure of FI solvency.
  • Examples like WaMu and Citigroup show how risk accumulation can lead to near-insolvency.

12. Other Risks, Interaction of Risks, and Climate Risk

12.1 Interaction of Risks

Although the 11 major risks are described independently, in reality they are often interdependent. For example:

  • When interest rates rise, corporations and consumers find it more difficult to maintain promised payments on debt, so credit risk, interest rate risk, and off-balance-sheet risk are positively correlated over some range.
  • Funds from promised loan payments are often used for liquidity management, so liquidity risk is correlated with interest rate and credit risks.
  • The inability of a customer to make promised payments affects the FI's income and profits, and consequently its equity or capital position, so each risk and its interaction ultimately affect insolvency risk.
  • Foreign exchange rate changes and interest rate changes are highly correlated—when the Federal Reserve changes a key interest rate, exchange rates are also likely to change.

Key Insight

The interaction of risks means that FI managers face making trade-offs among them. As they take actions to affect one type of risk, they must consider the possible impact on other risks. The effective management of these risks determines a modern FI's success or failure.

12.2 Event Risk and Macroeconomic Risk

Event Risk: Discrete or unexpected events that impact an FI's profitability and risk exposure. Examples include sudden changes in regulatory policy (e.g., the 1994 regulatory change allowing interstate branching, the 1999 Financial Services Modernization Act, the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010), sudden market collapses (1929, 2008), terrorist attacks (September 2001), fraud, theft, earthquakes, and storms.

Macroeconomic or Systemic Risk: General economic conditions such as inflation, inflation volatility, and unemployment directly and indirectly impact an FI's level of interest rate, credit, and liquidity risk exposure. For example, the U.S. unemployment rate exceeded 10% in the fall of 2009, and the economy lost 8 million jobs during the Great Recession. With so many people out of work, credit risk exposure increased dramatically. During the Covid-19 pandemic, more than twice as many jobs were lost between March and April 2020 as during the entire 2007–2009 period, leading to sharp increases in mortgage forbearance and auto loan modification requests.

12.3 Climate Risk

There are increasing concerns that climate risk may adversely affect the balance sheet of FIs and thus be relevant for financial stability. In his 2015 speech at Lloyd's of London, Mark Carney, then Governor of the Bank of England and Chairman of the Financial Stability Board (FSB), discussed three broad channels through which climate change affects the financial sector:

  1. Physical Risks: Related to extreme weather events or changing climate conditions. Floods and storms can cause severe damage to physical assets. If losses are insured, the insurance industry is exposed; if uninsured, banks and other FIs are exposed as households default on loans and mortgages, and collateral values decline.
  2. Liability Risks: Related to the impacts on FIs if parties who have suffered loss or damage from climate change seek compensation from those they hold responsible. Credit risk for banks can rise vis-à-vis corporations facing reduced profitability.
  3. Transition Risks: Related to the shift to a low-carbon economy. FIs are exposed because their portfolios often contain large portions of industries (e.g., fossil fuel and energy) that will be affected by the transition. Lenders face reduced collateral values and borrowers whose business models may become unprofitable.

Climate-related risks may cause abrupt increases in risk premia across a wide range of assets, alter asset price comovement across sectors, amplify credit, liquidity, and counterparty risks, and challenge financial risk management in ways that are hard to predict. On October 21, 2021, the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) released its report on climate-related financial risk, recognizing for the first time that climate change is an emerging and increasing threat to U.S. financial stability. The FSOC report recommended actions on climate change data, disclosure, and scenario analysis, and noted steps being taken by the SEC, the Federal Reserve Board, the CFTC, the FHFA, and the Treasury's Federal Insurance Office.

Key Insight

Climate risk is a newly recognized, systemic risk that interacts with credit, liquidity, market, and operational risks. Physical and liability risks relate directly to climate change, while transition risks relate to society's response to it. These risks may affect the global financial system's ability to respond to shocks and could lead to reductions in bank lending and insurance provision.

Key Takeaways

  • All risks are interdependent: changes in one risk often affect others, creating complex trade-offs.
  • Event risk includes discrete events like regulatory changes, market crashes, and natural disasters.
  • Macroeconomic conditions (unemployment, inflation) significantly affect credit and liquidity risks.
  • Climate risk operates through physical, liability, and transition channels, and is a growing focus of regulators.
  • FSOC (2021) recognized climate change as an emerging threat to U.S. financial stability.

13. Practical Cautions, Common Errors, and Summary

The following practical cautions are derived from the source material and are essential for the correct application of the concepts presented in this chapter. These cautions highlight common mistakes, limitations, and misinterpretations that FI managers, analysts, and students should avoid.

1. Incorrect Interpretation of Profit Spread

The profit spread (return on assets minus cost of funds) is a static, one-period measure. A positive spread in the current period does not guarantee future profitability. The risk is that rates will change when liabilities are refinanced or assets are reinvested. Common mistake: Assuming that the current spread will persist indefinitely.

2. Confusing Association with Causality

Obtaining a positive profit spread in the first year of a mismatched maturity position does not imply that the FI's strategy is sound. The positive spread may be offset by negative spreads in future periods if rates move adversely. Never imply that a single period's result establishes a sustainable advantage.

3. Ignoring Model Assumptions in Maturity Matching

Matching maturities is only an approximate hedge against interest rate risk. Perfect hedging requires matching durations (average lives), not just maturities, and accounting for the FI's leverage (equity capital). Common mistake: Believing that equal maturities eliminate all interest rate risk.

4. Misinterpreting the Intercept in Net Worth Calculations

The traditional net worth calculation E = A – L ignores off-balance-sheet items. The true economic net worth is E = (A – L) + (CA – CL). Ignoring contingent assets and liabilities can lead to a material overstatement of solvency.

5. Ignoring Units of Measurement in Foreign Exchange Risk

Net foreign asset exposure is measured in the foreign currency. A net long position of £20 million means the FI gains $2 million if the pound appreciates by 10 cents, but loses $2 million if the pound depreciates by 10 cents. Always convert to domestic currency to assess actual dollar impact.

6. Confusing Error and Residual in Risk Measurement

In risk measurement models, the error is the true unobservable deviation from the expected value, while the residual is the estimated deviation from the fitted model. These are not the same. The residual is an estimate of the error, and the difference matters for inference.

7. Omitted-Variable Problems in Risk Assessment

When assessing credit risk, focusing only on borrower-specific factors while ignoring macroeconomic conditions (systematic risk) can lead to a severe underestimation of default risk during recessions. Similarly, ignoring the interaction of risks (e.g., interest rate changes affecting credit risk) can lead to incomplete risk assessment.

8. Assuming Diversification Eliminates All Credit Risk

Diversification reduces firm-specific credit risk but does not eliminate systematic credit risk. Even a well-diversified loan portfolio will experience losses during an economywide recession. Common mistake: Believing that a diversified portfolio is immune to credit losses.

9. Overlooking the Trade-Off Between Risk and Profitability

Matching maturities reduces interest rate risk but also reduces the profitability potential from asset transformation. FIs must choose their position on the risk-return spectrum. There is no free lunch: reducing risk typically reduces expected returns.

10. Underestimating the Impact of Off-Balance-Sheet Risk

Off-balance-sheet activities generate fee income but can create large contingent liabilities that, when triggered, can render an FI economically insolvent. The 2008–2009 financial crisis demonstrated that losses from OBS activities (e.g., credit default swaps) can be just as damaging as on-balance-sheet losses.

11. Confusing Refinancing Risk with Reinvestment Risk

Refinancing risk is the risk that the cost of new liabilities rises (affects FIs with long assets and short liabilities). Reinvestment risk is the risk that the return on new assets falls (affects FIs with short assets and long liabilities). These are opposite sides of the same coin and must be distinguished.
