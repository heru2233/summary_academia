Chapter 7 Summary

Risks of Financial Institutions

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 7. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

A major objective of FI management is to increase the FI's returns for its owners, but this often comes at the cost of increased risk. This chapter overviews the various risks facing FIs, namely interest rate risk, credit risk, liquidity risk, foreign exchange risk, country or sovereign risk, market risk, off-balance-sheet risk, technology and operational risk, risk of digital disruption and fintech, and insolvency risk. The effective management of these risks is central to an FI's performance, and it can be argued that the main business of FIs is to manage these risks. Over the past decade, U.S. financial institution profitability has generally been robust, but the risks of financial intermediation have increased as the U.S. and overseas economies have become more integrated, for example uncertainty associated with international events such as Brexit and tariff wars has presented great risks for those FIs that operate in and lend to foreign markets. Even FIs without foreign customers can be exposed to foreign exchange and sovereign risk if their domestic customers have business dealings with foreign countries.

2. Interest Rate Risk

Chapter 1 discussed asset transformation as a key special function of FIs, which involves an FI's buying primary securities or assets and issuing secondary securities or liabilities to fund asset purchases. The primary securities purchased by FIs often have maturity and liquidity characteristics different from those of the secondary securities FIs sell. In mismatching the maturities of assets and liabilities as part of their asset-transformation function, FIs potentially expose themselves to interest rate risk, the risk incurred by an FI when the maturities of its assets and liabilities are mismatched.

Example 7-1. Impact of an Interest Rate Increase on an FI's Profits When the Maturity of Its Assets Exceeds the Maturity of Its Liabilities

Consider an FI that issues $100 million of liabilities of one-year maturity to finance the purchase of $100 million of assets with a two-year maturity. The FI can be viewed as short-funded, that is, the maturity of its liabilities is less than the maturity of its assets. Suppose the cost of funds (liabilities) for the FI is 9 percent per year and the return on assets is 10 percent per year. Over the first year, the FI can lock in a profit spread of 1 percent (10 percent minus 9 percent) times $100 million by borrowing short term and lending long term, so its profit is $1 million.

\[ \text{Profit spread} = r_{\text{assets}} - r_{\text{liabilities}} = 10\% - 9\% = 1\% \]
\[ \text{Profit} = 0.01 \times \$100 \text{ million} = \$1 \text{ million} \]

However, its profits for the second year are uncertain. If interest rates were to rise and the FI can borrow new one-year liabilities only at 11 percent in the second year, its profit spread would be negative, that is, 10 percent minus 11 percent equals minus 1 percent, or the FI suffers a loss of $1 million. By holding longer-term assets relative to liabilities, the FI potentially exposes itself to refinancing risk, the risk that the cost of rolling over or reborrowing funds could be more than the return earned on asset investments. As interest rates rose in the mid-2010s, good examples were provided by banks that borrowed short-term deposits while investing in fixed-rate loans.

Example 7-2. Impact of an Interest Rate Decrease When the Maturity of an FI's Liabilities Exceeds the Maturity of Its Assets

An alternative balance sheet structure would have the FI borrowing $100 million for a longer term than the $100 million of assets in which it invests, so the FI is long-funded, with the maturity of its liabilities longer than the maturity of its assets. Suppose the FI borrows funds at 9 percent per year for two years and invests the funds in assets that yield 10 percent for one year. The FI locks in a one-year profit spread of 1 percent, or $1 million, but at the end of the first year the assets mature and the funds that have been borrowed for two years have to be reinvested. If interest rates fall so that the return on new one-year assets is 8 percent in the second year, the FI faces a loss, or negative spread, of 1 percent (8 percent asset return minus 9 percent cost of funds), or loses $1 million. By holding shorter-term assets relative to liabilities, the FI is exposed to reinvestment risk, the risk that the return on funds to be reinvested will fall below the cost of funds. As interest rates fell in the 2000s, good examples were provided by banks that borrowed fixed-rate deposits while investing in floating-rate loans.

In addition to refinancing or reinvestment risk, an FI faces market value risk. The market (or fair) value of an asset or liability is conceptually equal to the present value of current and future cash flows from that asset or liability, so rising interest rates increase the discount rate on those cash flows and reduce the market value, while falling interest rates increase market values. Moreover, mismatching maturities by holding longer-term assets than liabilities means that when interest rates rise, the market value of the FI's assets falls by a greater amount than its liabilities, exposing the FI to the risk of economic loss and, potentially, the risk of insolvency. Unlike refinancing or reinvestment risk, market value risk reflects economic reality or the true value of assets or liabilities if the FI's portfolio were to be liquidated at today's securities prices, and it considers the degree of leverage on an FI's balance sheet as well as the timing of cash flows.

If holding assets and liabilities with mismatched maturities exposes FIs to reinvestment (or refinancing) and market value risks, FIs can seek to hedge by matching the maturity of their assets and liabilities. However, matching maturities is not necessarily consistent with an active asset-transformation function, since FIs cannot be asset transformers (for example transforming short-term deposits into long-term loans) and direct balance sheet matchers or hedgers at the same time. While reducing exposure to interest rate risk, matching maturities may also reduce profitability. For example, depository institutions traditionally hold longer-term assets than liabilities, whereas life insurers tend to match the long-term nature of their liabilities with long-term assets. Finally, matching maturities hedges interest rate risk only in a very approximate rather than complete fashion, relating to the difference between the average life (or duration) and maturity of an asset or liability and whether the FI partly funds its assets with equity capital as well as liabilities. The causes of interest rate risk and methods to measure it are discussed in detail in Chapters 8 and 9, and the methods and instruments used to hedge it in Chapters 23 through 25.

3. Credit Risk

Credit risk arises because of the possibility that promised cash flows on financial claims held by FIs, such as loans or bonds, will not be paid in full. Virtually all types of FIs face this risk, but in general, FIs that make loans or buy bonds with long maturities are more exposed than those with short maturities. For example, depository institutions and life insurers are more exposed to credit risk than are money market mutual funds and property-casualty insurers. If the principal on all financial claims was paid in full on maturity and interest payments were made on the promised dates, FIs would face no credit risk. If a borrower defaults, however, both the principal loaned and the interest payments expected to be received are at risk. As a result, many financial claims issued by corporations promise a limited or fixed upside return (principal and interest payments) with a high probability and a large downside risk (loss of loan principal and promised interest) with a much smaller probability, for example fixed-income coupon bonds and bank loans, where in the event of default the FI earns zero interest and may lose all or part of the principal, depending on its ability to lay claim to some of the borrower's assets through bankruptcy proceedings. A key role of FIs involves screening and monitoring loan applicants (Chapter 10).

Firm-specific credit risk is the risk of default of the borrowing firm associated with the specific types of project risk taken by that firm, while systematic credit risk is the risk of default associated with general economywide or macro conditions affecting all borrowers, such as an economic recession. One of the advantages FIs have over individual household investors is the ability to diversify some credit risk from a single asset away by exploiting the law of large numbers in their asset investment portfolios. Diversification reduces individual firm-specific credit risk while leaving the FI still exposed to systematic credit risk. The effects of credit risk are evident in the high rate of charge-offs experienced on credit card loans by commercial banks throughout 1984 to 2021, surging in the early 2000s and during the recession from 2007 to 2010. Total credit card loans grew from $227.497 billion (December 1999) to $444.692 billion (December 2008), fell to $403.071 billion (March 2009), recovered as the economy improved, reached a peak of $941.557 billion (December 2019), then fell to $791.990 billion (June 2021). Methods to measure the default risk of individual corporate claims are described in Chapter 10, methods of measuring portfolio risk in Chapter 11, and methods to manage and control credit risk exposures, including the role of credit derivative markets, in Chapters 23, 24, 25, 26, and 27.

4. Liquidity Risk

Liquidity risk arises when an FI's liability holders, such as depositors or insurance policyholders, demand immediate cash for the financial claims they hold, or when holders of off-balance-sheet loan commitments (or credit lines) suddenly exercise their right to borrow. When liability holders demand cash immediacy, the FI must either borrow additional funds or sell assets to meet the demand for withdrawals. The most liquid asset of all is cash, but FIs limit their cash holdings because cash earns no interest. Day-to-day withdrawals are generally predictable, and FIs can normally expect to borrow additional funds to meet any sudden shortfalls of cash on the money and financial markets.

However, there are times when an FI can face a liquidity crisis, when because of a lack of confidence or some unexpected need for cash, liability holders demand larger withdrawals than normal. When all, or many, FIs face abnormally large cash demands, the cost of additional purchased funds rises and the supply becomes restricted, so FIs may have to sell some of their less liquid assets to meet withdrawal demands. This results in a more serious liquidity risk, especially as some assets with thin markets generate lower prices when the asset sale is immediate than when the FI has more time to negotiate. The liquidation of some assets at low or fire-sale prices (the price an FI receives if an asset must be liquidated immediately at less than its fair market value) could threaten an FI's profitability and solvency. For example, in the summer of 2008, IndyMac bank failed, in part due to a bank run that continued for several days even after being taken over by the FDIC, after the bank announced its mortgage operations would stop, and depositors rushed to withdraw money even though their deposits were insured up to $100,000 by the FDIC.

Example 7-3. Impact of Liquidity Risk on an FI's Equity Value

Consider the simple FI balance sheet, before deposit withdrawals the FI has $10 million in cash assets and $90 million in nonliquid assets (such as small business loans), funded with $90 million in deposits and $10 million in owner's equity. Suppose depositors unexpectedly withdraw $15 million in deposits and the FI receives no new deposits to replace them. To meet these withdrawals, the FI first uses the $10 million it has in cash assets and then seeks to sell some of its nonliquid assets to raise an additional $5 million in cash. Because the FI cannot borrow any more funds and cannot wait to get better prices, it has to sell nonliquid assets at 50 cents on the dollar. To cover the remaining $5 million in withdrawals, the FI must sell $10 million in nonliquid assets, incurring a loss of $5 million from the face value of those assets. Because its capital was only $10 million before the withdrawal, the loss on the fire-sale of assets of $5 million leaves the FI with only $5 million in equity.

\[ \text{Fire-sale loss} = \text{Assets sold} \times (1 - \text{fire-sale price}) = \$10 \text{ million} \times (1 - 0.5) = \$5 \text{ million} \]
\[ \text{Final equity} = \text{Initial equity} - \text{Loss} = \$10 \text{ million} - \$5 \text{ million} = \$5 \text{ million} \]

5. Foreign Exchange Risk

Increasingly, FIs have recognized that both direct foreign investment and foreign portfolio investments can extend the operational and financial benefits available from purely domestic investments. U.S. pension funds that held approximately 5 percent of their assets in foreign securities in the early 1990s now hold over 24 percent, and many large U.S. banks, investment banks, and mutual funds have become more global. To the extent that the returns on domestic and foreign investments are imperfectly correlated, there are potential gains for an FI that expands its asset holdings and liability funding beyond the domestic borders. The returns are not perfectly correlated for two reasons, the first being that the underlying technologies of various economies differ (one economy may be based on agriculture while another is industry based, so one economy could be expanding while another is contracting), and the second being that exchange rate changes are not perfectly correlated across countries (the dollar-euro exchange rate may be appreciating while the dollar-yen exchange rate may be falling).

Foreign exchange risk is the risk that exchange rate changes can adversely affect the value of an FI's assets and liabilities denominated in foreign currencies. For example, if a U.S. FI makes a loan to a British company in pounds, and the pound depreciates in value relative to the U.S. dollar, the principal and interest payments received by U.S. investors would be devalued in dollar terms, and if the pound fell far enough, the overall return could be negative when cash flows are converted back into dollars. In general, an FI can hold assets denominated in a foreign currency and/or issue foreign liabilities. A U.S. FI that holds 100 million pounds in pound loans as assets and funds 80 million pounds of them with pound certificates of deposit is net long 20 million pounds in pound assets, and suffers losses if the exchange rate for pounds falls or depreciates against the dollar. Instead, if the FI had 20 million pounds more pound liabilities than assets, it would be holding a net short position in pound assets, exposed to foreign exchange risk if the pound appreciates against the dollar. To be approximately hedged, the FI must match its assets and liabilities in each foreign currency.

Note that the FI is fully hedged only if we assume it holds foreign assets and liabilities of exactly the same maturity. If the FI matches the size of its foreign currency book (pound assets equals pound liabilities, for example 100 million pounds) but mismatches the maturities, for example pound assets of six-month maturity and liabilities of three-month maturity, the FI would be exposed to foreign interest rate risk, the risk that British interest rates would rise when it has to roll over its pound CD liabilities at the end of the third month. An FI that matches both the size and maturities of its exposure in assets and liabilities of a given currency is hedged, or immunized, against foreign currency and foreign interest rate risk. Because foreign exchange rate and interest rate changes are not perfectly correlated across countries, an FI can diversify away part, if not all, of its foreign currency risk. The measurement and evaluation of an FI's foreign currency risk exposure is discussed in depth in Chapter 13.

6. Country or Sovereign Risk

Country or sovereign risk is a different type of credit risk faced by an FI that purchases assets such as the bonds and loans of foreign corporations, namely the risk that repayments from foreign borrowers may be interrupted because of restrictions, intervention, or interference from foreign governments. When a domestic corporation is unable or unwilling to repay a loan, an FI usually has recourse to the domestic bankruptcy courts and eventually may recoup at least a portion of its original investment. By comparison, a foreign corporation may be unable to repay even if it would like to, most commonly because the government of the country in which the corporation is headquartered may prohibit or limit debt payments because of foreign currency shortages and adverse political reasons.

An extreme example is Argentina, which had pegged its peso to the dollar on a one-to-one basis since the early 1990s, but in December 2001 ended up defaulting on $130 billion in government-issued debt, largely because of an overvalued peso and its adverse effect on exports and foreign currency earnings, and in 2002 passed legislation that led to defaults on $30 billion of corporate debt owed to foreign creditors. Argentina later defaulted on a $3 billion loan repayment to the IMF in September 2003. Another case of extreme sovereign risk is the European crisis of the 2010s, where in March 2012 Greek government debtholders lost 53.5 percent of their $265 billion investment as Greece restructured much of its sovereign debt, producing the largest-ever sovereign debt default. In the event of such restrictions, reschedulings, or outright prohibitions on the payment of debt obligations by sovereign governments, the FI claimholder has little, if any, recourse to the local bankruptcy courts or an international civil claims court. The major leverage available to an FI to ensure or increase repayment probabilities and amounts is its control over the future supply of loans or funds to the country concerned, but such leverage may be very weak in the face of a country's collapsing currency and government. Chapter 14 discusses how country or sovereign risk is measured and considers possible financial market solutions to the country risk exposure problems of a globally oriented FI.

7. Market Risk

Market risk arises when FIs actively trade assets and liabilities (and derivatives) rather than hold them for longer-term investment, funding, or hedging purposes. Market risk is closely related to interest rate risk, credit risk, and foreign exchange risk, but adds another dimension resulting from trading activity, namely the incremental risk incurred by an FI when interest rate, foreign exchange, and credit risks are combined with an active trading strategy, especially one that involves short trading horizons such as a day. Conceptually, an FI's trading portfolio can be differentiated from its investment portfolio on the basis of time horizon and secondary market liquidity. The trading portfolio contains assets, liabilities, and derivative contracts that can be quickly bought or sold on organized financial markets, while the investment portfolio (or in the case of banks, the so-called banking book) contains assets and liabilities that are relatively illiquid and held for longer holding periods.

With the increasing securitization of bank loans (for example mortgages), more and more assets have become liquid and tradable. While bank regulators have normally viewed tradable assets as those being held for horizons of less than one year, private FIs take an even shorter-term view, concerned about the fluctuation in the value of their trading account assets and liabilities for periods as short as one day, especially if such fluctuations pose a threat to solvency. An extreme case of market risk is the financial crisis of 2008 to 2009, when investment banks and securities firms were major purchasers and traders of mortgages in the early 2000s, and rising interest rates in the mid-2000s resulted in significant increases in mortgage defaults, so the securitized mortgage market froze up and FIs were left holding toxic assets at deeply reduced market values. Market risk is present whenever an FI takes an open or unhedged long (buy) or short (sell) position in bonds, equities, foreign exchange, and derivative products, and prices change in a direction opposite to that expected. The more volatile are asset prices in the markets in which these instruments trade, the greater are the market risks faced by FIs that adopt open trading positions, requiring FI management and regulators to establish controls to limit positions taken by traders as well as to develop models to measure the market risk exposure on a day-to-day basis. These market risk measurement models are discussed in Chapter 15.

8. Off-Balance-Sheet Risk

One of the most striking trends for many modern FIs has been the growth in their off-balance-sheet activities and thus their off-balance-sheet risk. An off-balance-sheet activity, by definition, does not appear on an FI's current balance sheet since it does not involve holding a current primary claim (asset) or the issuance of a current secondary claim (liability). Instead, off-balance-sheet activities affect the future, rather than the current, shape of an FI's balance sheet, involving the creation of contingent assets and liabilities that give rise to their potential (future) placement on the balance sheet, with a direct impact on the FI's future profitability and performance. Thus, accountants place them below the bottom line of an FI's asset and liability balance sheet.

A good example of an off-balance-sheet activity is the issuance of standby letter of credit guarantees by insurance companies and banks to back the issuance of municipal bonds. Many state and local governments could not issue such securities without bank or insurance company letter of credit guarantees that promise principal and interest payments to investors should the municipality default on its future obligations. If a municipal government's cash flow is sufficiently strong so as to pay off the principal and interest on the debt it issues, the letter of credit guarantee issued by the FI expires unused, and nothing appears on the FI's balance sheet today or in the future, but the fee earned for issuing the guarantee appears on the FI's income statement. The ability to earn fee income while not loading up or expanding the balance sheet has become an important motivation for FIs to pursue off-balance-sheet business. Unfortunately, this activity is not risk free, because if the municipal government defaults, the contingent liability or guaranty the FI issued becomes an actual liability that appears on the FI's balance sheet, and the FI has to use its own equity to compensate investors in municipal bonds.

Letters of credit are just one example of off-balance-sheet activities. Others include loan commitments by banks, mortgage servicing contracts by depository institutions, and positions in forwards, futures, swaps, and other derivative securities by almost all large FIs. While some of these activities are structured to reduce an FI's exposure to credit, interest rate, or foreign exchange risks, mismanagement or speculative use of these instruments can result in major losses. Indeed, as seen during the financial crisis of 2008 to 2009, significant losses in off-balance-sheet (OBS) activities (for example credit default swaps) can cause an FI to fail, just as major losses due to balance sheet default and interest rate risks can. The specific nature of the risks of off-balance-sheet activities is detailed more fully in Chapter 16, and how some of these instruments (forwards, futures, options, and swaps) can be used to manage risks in Chapters 23, 24, 25, and 27.

Example 7-4. Impact of Off-Balance-Sheet Risk on an FI's Equity Value

In panel A, the value of the FI's net worth (E) is calculated in the traditional way as the difference between the market values of its on-balance-sheet assets (A) and liabilities (L)

\[ E = A - L \]
\[ 10 = 100 - 90 \]

Under this calculation, the market value of the stockholders' equity stake in the FI is 10 and the ratio of the FI's capital to assets is 10 percent. A more accurate picture of the FI's economic solvency should consider the market values of both its on-balance-sheet and OBS activities. In panel B, the current market value of the FI's contingent assets (CA) is 50 and the current market value of its contingent liabilities (CL) is 55. Since CL exceed CA by 5, this difference is an additional obligation, or claim, on the FI's net worth, so stockholders' true net worth (E) is really

\[ E = (A - L) + (CA - CL) \]
\[ E = (100 - 90) + (50 - 55) = 5 \]

rather than 10 when off-balance-sheet activities are ignored. Economically speaking, contingent assets and liabilities are contractual claims that directly impact the economic value of the equity holders' stake in an FI, and from both the stockholders' and regulators' perspectives, large increases in the value of OBS liabilities can render the FI economically insolvent just as effectively as losses due to mismatched interest rate gaps and default or credit losses from on-balance-sheet activities.

9. Technology and Operational Risks

Technology and operational risks are closely related and in recent years have caused great concern to FI managers and regulators alike. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) defines operational risk (inclusive of technological risk) as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems or from external events. A number of FIs add reputational risk and strategic risk (for example due to a failed merger) as part of a broader definition of operational risk. Technological innovation was a major growth area of FIs in the 1990s and 2000s, with banks, insurance companies, and investment companies seeking to improve operational efficiency with major investments in internal and external communications, computers, and an expanded technological infrastructure, for example depositors can check account balances, transfer funds, manage finances, and pay bills from their personal electronic devices, and electronic transfer of funds through automated clearing houses (ACH) and wire transfer payment networks such as the Clearing House Interbank Payments Systems (CHIPS) have been developed.

The major objectives of technological expansion are to lower operating costs, increase profits, and capture new markets, exploiting to the fullest extent possible economies of scale (an FI's ability to lower its average costs of operations by expanding its output of financial services) and economies of scope (an FI's ability to generate cost synergies by producing more than one output with the same inputs, for example using the same information on the quality of customers to expand the sale of both loan products and insurance products). Technology risks in financial services include the strategic risk of not embracing new technology, not investing in the right technology, as well as cybersecurity risk, technology vendor and third-party risk, and ineffective risk management. Many FIs are going through major legacy-to-digital transformations by moving their systems to the cloud and investing in new technologies such as blockchain, virtual reality, artificial intelligence, and machine learning. During the COVID-19 pandemic, FIs temporarily shifted to remote working, increasing the risk of cyber attacks and dependencies on third-party service providers, and the financial sector has been hit relatively more often by cyber attacks than most other sectors since the pandemic started, with payment firms, insurers, and credit unions especially affected by a significant increase in hacks.

Operational risk is partly related to technology risk and can arise whenever existing technology malfunctions or back-office support systems break down. For example, in August 2018 the FBI sent out an alert to banks that cybercriminals were preparing to carry out a global fraud scheme known as an ATM cash-out, where thieves use a bank or payment card processor and cloned cards at cash machines to potentially withdraw millions of dollars in a few hours after removing many fraud controls at the financial institution. Another notable instance is Target Stores in 2013, when hackers infected the company's payment card readers, making off with 110 million records at the height of the Christmas shopping season. Operational risk is not exclusively the result of technological failure, since employee fraud and errors constitute a type of operational risk that often negatively affects the reputation of an FI. A good example involves $6.2 billion in losses incurred in 2012 by JPMorgan Chase's trader Bruno Iksil, also known as the London Whale, who had taken large credit default swap (CDS) positions in expectation that the financial crisis in Europe would cause anxiety in financial markets, but bailouts, austerity measures, and interventions prevented any major events in Europe, and the CDS position eventually had to be closed at a loss when hedge fund traders took positions designed to gain from the trades Iksil had to make to keep the position going. These activities result in an overall loss of reputation and, in turn, business for the FI. The Federal Reserve defines reputational risk as the potential that negative publicity regarding an institution's business practices, whether true or not, will cause a decline in the customer base, costly litigation, or revenue reductions. Technology risks are described in more detail in Chapter 17.

10. Risk of Digital Disruption and Fintech

Digital innovation is transforming the financial services sector. Fintech refers to the use of technology to deliver financial solutions in a manner that competes with traditional financial methods, and the Financial Stability Board defines fintech as technology-enabled innovation in financial services that could result in new business models, applications, processes or products with an associated material effect on the provision of financial services. Examples include mobile money, peer-to-peer (P2P) or marketplace lending, robo-advice, insurance technology (insurtech), and digital currencies. A fintech firm specializes in offering digital financial services to consumers, or enables other providers to offer digital financial services, while a big tech firm is a large company whose primary activity is digital services (search engines, social media platforms, e-commerce platforms, ride-hailing platforms, and mobile network operators).

Emerging technologies such as digital currencies (for example bitcoin, ether) and blockchain provide a system that supports the exchange of value between two parties unknown to each other in a swift and effective way, without the need for financial intermediaries. By reducing transaction costs among all participants in the economy, fintech supports models of peer-to-peer mass collaboration that could make many existing organizational forms redundant. However, investing in fintech may enable incumbents such as JPMorgan Chase, Citigroup, and Credit Suisse to do more with less, streamline their businesses, and reduce risk in the process. Adoption of new technologies in financial services would require substantial expenditures in order to adapt to evolving industry standards and consumer preferences, and the SEC noted that digital currencies limit the bank's ability to track movement of funds and comply with laws such as antimoney laundering regulation.

The largest fintech companies by valuation include Stripe ($95 billion), a company that provides online payment platform, Klarna ($31 billion), a company that powers financing for customers at stores like IKEA, H&M, and Etsy, and Kraken ($20 billion), a cryptocurrency exchange. Leading banks are rapidly closing gaps in digitization of internal processes and customer offerings to compete with fintechs and the big tech firms, which are able to deepen their inroads to core financial products as economies of scale and network effects are strong in digital platforms and cloud computing. While fintech firms face increased regulatory oversight, financial institutions face an increasing need to update their technological operating models. That said, traditional financial institutions have used risk management in a certain way for many years, with large teams of highly skilled and experienced employees, while fintech firms tend to have small teams using risk management processes developed over a relatively short time frame and untested in dealing with major risk events, and a risk event at a fintech firm can result in losses that are substantially damaging to firm value because there is less history to create a protocol. Chapter 18 summarizes the opportunities and risks of digital disruption and fintech in detail.

11. Insolvency Risk

Insolvency risk is a consequence or outcome of one or more of the risks described above, namely interest rate, credit, liquidity, foreign exchange, sovereign, market, off-balance-sheet, technology risks, and risk of digital disruption and fintech. Technically, insolvency occurs when the capital or equity resources of an FI's owners are driven to, or near to, zero because of losses incurred as the result of one or more of these risks. Consider the case of Washington Mutual (WaMu), which incurred heavy losses from its on- and off-balance-sheet holdings during the financial crisis, with its market capital worth only $3.5 billion by early September 2008, down from $43 billion at the end of 2006, before being taken over by the FDIC and sold to JPMorgan Chase in September 2008. In contrast, in March 2009 Citigroup's stock price fell to below $1 per share and the once largest bank in the United States was near failure, but it received a substantial government guarantee against losses (up to $306 billion) and a $20 billion injection of cash to prevent failure. Between October 2008 and December 2009, more than 700 banks received a total of $205 billion in federal government funds through the Capital Purchase Program in an effort to prop up capital and support lending.

In general, the more equity capital to borrowed funds an FI has, that is, the lower its leverage, the better able it is to withstand losses, whether due to adverse interest rate changes, unexpected credit losses, or other reasons. Thus, both management and regulators of FIs focus on an FI's capital (and adequacy) as a key measure of its ability to remain solvent and grow in the face of a multitude of risk exposures. The issue of what is an adequate level of capital to manage an FI's overall risk exposure is discussed in Chapter 21.

12. Other Risks and the Interaction of Risks

This chapter concentrates on 11 major risks continuously affecting an FI manager's decision-making process. Even though the discussion generally described each independently, in reality these risks are often interdependent. For example, when interest rates rise, corporations and consumers find maintaining promised payments on their debt more difficult, so over some range of interest rate movements, credit, interest rate, and off-balance-sheet risks are positively correlated. The FI may also have been counting on the funds from promised payments on its loans for liquidity management purposes, so liquidity risk is also correlated with interest rate and credit risks. The inability of a customer to make promised payments affects the FI's income and profits and, consequently, its equity or capital position, so each risk and its interaction with other risks ultimately affect insolvency risk. Foreign exchange rate changes and interest rate changes are also highly correlated, since when the Federal Reserve changes a key interest rate through its monetary policy actions, exchange rates are also likely to change.

Various other risks, often of a more discrete or event type, also impact an FI's profitability and risk exposure, although many view discrete or event risks as part of operational risks. Discrete risks might include events external to the FI, such as a sudden change in regulation policy, including lifting the regulatory barriers to lending or to entry or on products offered (for example the 1994 regulatory change allowing interstate branching after 1997, the 1999 Financial Services Modernization Act, and the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010), as well as sudden and unexpected changes in financial market conditions due to war, revolution, or sudden market collapse, such as the 1929 and 2008 stock market crashes or the September 2001 terrorist attacks. Other event risks include fraud, theft, earthquakes, storms, malfeasance, and breach of fiduciary trust, all of which can ultimately cause an FI to fail or be severely harmed, yet each is difficult to model and predict.

More general macroeconomic or systemic risks, such as increased inflation, inflation volatility, and unemployment, can directly and indirectly impact an FI's level of interest rate, credit, and liquidity risk exposure. During the Great Recession (December 2007 to June 2009), the U.S. economy lost some 8 million jobs, most of which were lost in the period November 2008 through June 2009, and with so many people out of work, credit risk exposure of FIs increased dramatically as borrowers had trouble keeping up with their loan payments after losing their jobs. It took more than five years for the labor market to recover after the end of the Great Recession, and the 2007 unemployment rate of less than 5 percent was not seen again until 2016. However, the magnitude of job loss in the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic had no precedent since the end of World War II, with more than twice as many jobs lost between March and April 2020 as were lost during the entire 2007 to 2009 period. As a result, mortgage forbearance increased sharply, showing nearly a 3,000 percent increase from March 2020 to May 2020, auto loan modification requests increased nearly 10 times since the pandemic began, and U.S. banks increased their loss provisions by three to four times for the first quarter of 2020 compared to the fourth quarter of 2019.

In addition to the above-mentioned risks, there are increasing concerns that climate risk may adversely affect the balance sheet of FIs and therefore may be relevant for financial stability. In his 2015 speech at Lloyd's of London, the governor of the Bank of England and then chairman of the Financial Stability Board (FSB) Mark Carney discussed three broad channels through which climate change affects the financial sector, namely (1) physical risks, related to extreme weather events or changing climate conditions, where floods and heavy storms can cause severe damage to physical assets even for the financial sector, (2) liability risks, related to the impacts on financial institutions if parties who have suffered loss or damage from the effects of climate change seek compensation from those they hold responsible, where if losses are insured the insurance industry is exposed, and if uninsured, banks and other financial institutions are exposed because households are more likely to default on their loans and mortgages while the collateral they provided is no longer available to cover these losses, and (3) transition risks, related to the two-degree goal under the Paris Agreement, where the exposure of the financial sector becomes apparent when taking into account the tendency of insurers, pension funds, and other investors to align their portfolios with capital market indices which often contain large portions of the industries (for example fossil fuel and energy industries) that will be affected by the transition to a low-carbon economy. Average weather-related losses per year worldwide have roughly tripled over the past 30 years, while the share of uninsured losses has increased. Climate-related risks may give rise to abrupt increases in risk premia across a wide range of assets, alter asset price comovement, amplify credit, liquidity, and counterparty risks, and challenge financial risk management in ways that are hard to predict. FIs are taking various actions to reduce or manage their exposure to climate-related risks, such as negative screening in lending, investing, or underwriting processes and exclusion policies for carbon-intensive industries, although these actions are not being systematically applied by most firms. On October 21, 2021, the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) released its report on climate-related financial risk, and for the first time recognized that climate change is an emerging and increasing threat to U.S. financial stability, recommending new actions on climate change data, disclosure, and scenario analysis, while the SEC has begun to evaluate its disclosure rules, the Federal Reserve Board has established two committees to develop a better understanding of climate-related risks, and the CFTC has engaged on climate-related financial risk issues through its Market Risk Advisory Committee.

13. Key Formulas and Calculations

13.1 Profit Spread and FI Profit

\[ \text{Profit spread} = r_{\text{assets}} - r_{\text{liabilities}} \]
\[ \text{Profit} = \text{Profit spread} \times \text{Amount of funds} \]

Example 7-1, a spread of 10 percent minus 9 percent equals 1 percent, a profit of $1 million in year 1, but if interest rates rise to 11 percent in year 2, the spread becomes minus 1 percent or a loss of $1 million (refinancing risk). Example 7-2, if the asset return falls to 8 percent in year 2 with a 9 percent cost of funds, the FI loses $1 million (reinvestment risk).

13.2 Fire-Sale Loss and Final Equity

\[ \text{Fire-sale loss} = \text{Face value of assets sold} \times (1 - \text{fire-sale price}) \]
\[ \text{Final equity} = \text{Initial equity} - \text{Loss} \]

Example 7-3, selling $10 million of nonliquid assets at 50 cents on the dollar produces a loss of $5 million, so equity falls from $10 million to $5 million.

13.3 Net Worth with Off-Balance-Sheet Activities

\[ E = A - L \]
\[ E = (A - L) + (CA - CL) \]

Example 7-4, E = (100 minus 90) + (50 minus 55) = 5, not 10 when off-balance-sheet activities are ignored.

13.4 Net Foreign Exchange Position

\[ \text{Net position} = \text{Foreign currency assets} - \text{Foreign currency liabilities} \]

A net long position in pound assets (pound assets of 100 million minus pound liabilities of 80 million, net long 20 million pounds) suffers losses if the pound depreciates, while a net short position (pound liabilities of 100 million minus pound assets of 80 million) suffers losses if the pound appreciates.

14. Summary and Conclusions

This chapter provided an introductory view of 11 major risks faced by modern FIs. They face interest rate risk when the maturities of their assets and liabilities are mismatched, credit risk or default risk if their clients default on their loans and other obligations, liquidity risk as a result of excessive withdrawals or problems in refinancing liabilities, and foreign exchange and sovereign risks if they conduct foreign business. They incur market risk on their trading assets and liabilities if adverse movements in interest rates, exchange rates, or other asset prices occur, and engage in significant off-balance-sheet activities that expose them to off-balance-sheet risks, namely contingent asset and liability risks. The advent of sophisticated technology and automation exposes FIs to both technological and operational risks, as well as the risk of digital disruption and fintech, which is a failure to successfully implement a digital transformation in their attempt to compete against new disruptive players. FIs face insolvency risk when their capital is insufficient to withstand the losses they incur as a result of such risks.

The interaction of the various risks means that FI managers face making trade-offs among them, since as they take actions in an attempt to affect one type of risk, they must consider the possible impact on other risks. Discrete or event risks, macroeconomic risks such as inflation and unemployment, and climate risk increasingly recognized as a threat to financial stability all add to the complexity. The effective management of these risks determines a modern FI's success or failure, and the chapters that follow analyze each of these risks in greater detail.
