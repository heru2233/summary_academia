1. Introduction to Insurance Companies

Insurance companies are financial institutions that provide risk protection to individuals and corporations (policyholders) against adverse events. By accepting premiums, insurers promise compensation if specified events occur. These policies represent financial liabilities to the insurer. Premiums collected are invested in financial securities such as corporate bonds and stocks. Insurance services are classified into two major groups: life insurance and property–casualty (PC) insurance. Life insurance protects against untimely death, illnesses, and retirement. Property–casualty insurance protects against personal injury and liability such as accidents, theft, and fire. Many firms, such as MetLife and Allstate, offer both types.

Key Insight

The financial crisis of 2008 demonstrated the systemic risk that insurance companies can pose. AIG, a major writer of credit default swaps (CDS) on subprime mortgage securities, faced catastrophic losses when mortgage-backed securities fell in value. The federal government intervened with a $127 billion bailout because AIG's failure would have triggered substantial losses across banks, investment banks, and other insurers that had purchased CDS contracts from it. This illustrates that insurance company risk extends far beyond the insurance sector itself.

Key Takeaways

  • Insurance companies accept premiums and promise compensation for specified adverse events.
  • Two main categories: life insurance (death, illness, retirement) and property–casualty (injury, liability, theft, fire).
  • Insurers invest premiums in financial securities, creating asset-liability management challenges.
  • Insurance company failures can create systemic risk to the entire financial system.

2. Life Insurance

Life insurance allows individuals and their beneficiaries to protect against income losses from premature death or retirement. By pooling risks, life insurance transfers income-related uncertainties from the insured individual to a group. In 2020, the United States had approximately 750 life insurance companies (down from 2,300 in 1988), with aggregate assets of $8.1 trillion. The 10 largest life insurers represented 45.6 percent of industry assets and wrote 40.6 percent of direct premiums.

2.1 Adverse Selection in Insurance

Adverse selection is a fundamental problem facing insurance companies: customers who apply for insurance policies are more likely to be those most in need of insurance. For example, someone with chronic health problems is more likely to purchase life insurance than someone in perfect health. If insurers use population-wide health statistics to calculate premiums, they will underestimate the probability of having to pay out on policies because their customer pool is more prone to health problems than the general population.

Mitigation strategy: Insurance companies deal with adverse selection by establishing different pools of the population based on health and related characteristics (such as income). By tailoring the pool used to determine the probability of losses to a particular customer's health characteristics, the insurer can more accurately determine the probability of payout and adjust the premium accordingly.

Example

A person with a pre-existing condition will be placed in a higher-risk pool and charged a higher premium than a person in perfect health. This reflects the insurer's more accurate assessment of the probability of a claim.

Analogy

Adverse selection is like a used car market where only sellers of defective cars want to sell. If buyers assume all cars are average quality, they underpay for good cars and overpay for bad ones. Insurers face a similar problem: only those who expect to need insurance are most eager to buy it.

Key Insight

Adverse selection forces insurers to use risk-based pricing rather than population-average pricing. Without proper risk segmentation, insurers would systematically underprice policies for high-risk individuals, leading to financial losses and potential insolvency.

Key Takeaways

  • Adverse selection: those most likely to claim are most likely to purchase insurance.
  • Insurers mitigate adverse selection by segmenting the population into risk pools based on health and related characteristics.
  • Proper risk segmentation allows accurate premium determination.

2.2 Mutual versus Stock Insurance Companies

Insurance companies can be organized as either mutual or stock companies. A mutual company is owned by its policyholders, who receive policy dividends and have ownership rights to accumulated profits. A stock company is owned by stockholders, who receive stock dividends and have ownership rights. As of 2020, 74 percent of all life insurance companies are stock companies. Many large insurers, such as Metropolitan and Prudential, have converted from mutual to stock ownership to gain access to equity markets for capital to fund business expansion and compete with the consolidating banking industry.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual insurers: owned by policyholders; profits distributed as policy dividends.
  • Stock insurers: owned by shareholders; profits distributed as stock dividends.
  • Conversion to stock ownership provides access to equity capital markets.

3. Types of Life Insurance Policies

Life insurance policies are classified by how they are sold: ordinary life (individual policies), group life (single policy covering many people), industrial life (weekly payments collected by agents), and credit life (protects lenders against borrower death). In 2020, ordinary life accounted for 78.7 percent of direct premiums, group life for 20.9 percent, and industrial and credit life together for less than 1 percent.

3.1 Ordinary Life Insurance: Traditional Forms

3.1.1 Term Life Insurance

Term life is the closest to pure life insurance, with no savings element. The individual receives a payout contingent on death during the coverage period. The term can vary from 1 year to 40 years or more. Premiums are periodic and level. If the insured survives the term, no payout occurs.

Key Insight

Term life provides pure mortality protection only. There is no cash value accumulation. This makes it the most cost-effective form of life insurance for a given level of death benefit, but it provides no savings or investment component.

3.1.2 Whole Life Insurance

Whole life protects the individual over an entire lifetime. In return for periodic (level) premiums, the beneficiaries receive the face value of the policy upon death. Unlike term insurance, there is certainty that if premiums are maintained, the insurer will pay out. Whole life has both a pure insurance element and a savings element. The savings component accumulates as cash value that the policyholder can borrow against or withdraw.

3.1.3 Endowment Life Insurance

Endowment life combines a pure (term) insurance element with a savings element. It guarantees a payout to beneficiaries if death occurs during the endowment period (e.g., before retirement age). An insured who lives to the endowment date receives the face amount of the policy. This creates a forced savings mechanism with a guaranteed payout at maturity.

3.2 Ordinary Life Insurance: Newer Forms

3.2.1 Variable Life Insurance

Variable life invests fixed premium payments in mutual funds of stocks, bonds, and money market instruments. Policyholders can choose mutual fund investments to reflect their risk preferences. Unlike traditional policies that pay a fixed face amount, the value of a variable life policy increases or decreases with the asset returns of the underlying mutual fund. This provides an alternative way to build savings compared with traditional whole life policies.

3.2.2 Universal Life and Variable Universal Life

Universal life allows both the premium amounts and the maturity of the contract to be changed by the insured, unlike traditional policies that maintain fixed premiums over a fixed contract period. For some contracts, insurers invest premiums in money market, equity, or bond mutual funds—as in variable life insurance—so that the savings component reflects market returns. In this case, the policy is called variable universal life.

Key Takeaways

  • Term life: pure insurance, no savings element, payout only on death during term.
  • Whole life: lifelong coverage, fixed premiums, includes savings component (cash value).
  • Endowment life: insurance plus savings, pays face value at death or at endowment date.
  • Variable life: premiums invested in mutual funds, policy value fluctuates with market returns.
  • Universal life: flexible premiums and maturity; variable universal life adds market-linked investment.

3.3 Group Life, Industrial Life, and Credit Life

Group life insurance covers a large number of insured persons under a single policy, usually issued to corporate employers. Policies may be contributory (employer and employee share the cost) or noncontributory (employer pays entirely). Cost economies are the principal advantage: mass administration, lower medical screening costs, and reduced selling and commission costs.

Industrial life insurance involves weekly payments directly collected by company representatives. It represents a very small area of coverage today, largely displaced by the growth of group life insurance.

Credit life insurance protects lenders against a borrower's death before repayment of a debt contract such as a mortgage or car loan. The face amount typically reflects the outstanding principal and interest on the loan.

Key Takeaways

  • Group life: single policy covering many individuals; cost-efficient due to economies of scale.
  • Industrial life: weekly premium collections; now negligible in market share.
  • Credit life: protects lenders; face amount equals outstanding loan balance.

4. Other Life Insurer Activities

4.1 Annuities

Annuities represent the reverse of life insurance activities. While life insurance involves building up a fund (accumulation), annuities involve liquidating a fund (distribution). Annuities can be sold to individuals or groups on a fixed or variable basis, linked to the return on an underlying investment portfolio. Individuals can purchase annuities with a single payment or with payments spread over years. The annuity builds up a fund whose returns are tax deferred (not subject to capital gains taxes during accumulation). Payments may be structured to start immediately or be deferred (at which time taxes are paid based on the income tax rate of the receiver). Payments may cease on death or continue to beneficiaries for a number of years after death.

Life insurance and annuity products can be used together: a life insurance policy (whole life or universal life) requires regular premium payments and provides a lump-sum death benefit. Upon receipt of that lump sum, a single annuity could be purchased to generate regular cash payments until the value is depleted. This creates a steady stream of cash disbursements without requiring a single lump-sum payment.

Fair Value of an Annuity Policy
\[ \text{Fair value} = \frac{15,000}{1+r} + \frac{15,000}{(1+r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{15,000}{(1+r)^{25}} = 15,000 \times \text{PVA}_{i=10\%, n=25} = 15,000 \times 14.0939 = \$211,409 \]

Explanation of symbols: r = guaranteed interest rate (5 percent in the example). PVA = present value annuity factor. The fair value is the sum of discounted expected future payments. The equation shows that the cost of purchasing an annuity today equals the present value of all expected future payments, discounted at the guaranteed interest rate the insurer can earn on the invested premiums.

Interpretation: If a person wants to receive $15,000 per year for 25 years (their expected remaining lifespan), and the insurer can earn 5 percent on invested funds, the fair price today is $211,409. This is the single premium the person must pay to receive the stream of payments.

Practical use: This calculation is used to price annuity contracts. The higher the guaranteed interest rate, the lower the premium required to generate a given payment stream. The longer the expected lifespan, the higher the premium because more payments will be made.

Assumptions: The calculation assumes the person lives exactly 25 years. If the person lives longer, the insurer must continue payments beyond the expected period, creating longevity risk. If the person dies earlier, the insurer keeps the remaining funds.

Example

A 65-year-old person wants to purchase an annuity paying $20,000 per year for life. The insurer expects the person to live 15 more years and can earn 6 percent. The fair price is $20,000 × PVA6%,15 = $20,000 × 9.7122 = $194,244. A healthier 65-year-old expected to live 20 years would be charged $20,000 × PVA6%,20 = $20,000 × 11.4699 = $229,398. The healthier person pays more because they are expected to receive more payments.

Key Insight

Annuities have become the primary business of many life insurance companies, surpassing traditional life insurance. In 2020, annuity sales (direct premiums written) topped $369.7 billion, twice the $188.0 billion in traditional life insurance sales. This shift reflects the growing demand for retirement income products.

4.2 Private Pension Plans

Insurance companies offer alternative pension plans to private employers, competing with commercial banks and securities firms. Innovative pension plans are based on guaranteed investment contracts (GICs), where the insurer guarantees both the interest rate credited to the pension plan over a given period (e.g., five years) and the annuity rates on beneficiaries' contracts. Other plans include immediate participation and separate account plans that follow more aggressive investment strategies, such as investing premiums in special-purpose equity mutual funds.

4.3 Accident and Health Insurance

Accident and health insurance protects against morbidity (ill health) risk, as opposed to life insurance which protects against mortality risk. In 2020, more than $209.8 billion in direct premiums were written by life and health companies in this area. The major activity line is group insurance, providing health insurance coverage to corporate employees. Life insurance companies write more than 50 percent of all health insurance premiums.

Key Takeaways

  • Annuities are the reverse of life insurance: they liquidate a fund rather than build one up.
  • Annuity fair value = present value of expected future payments, discounted at the guaranteed interest rate.
  • Tax deferral is a key feature of annuity products.
  • GICs allow insurers to compete for pension fund business by guaranteeing both interest rates and annuity rates.
  • Accident and health insurance protects against morbidity risk; group insurance is the dominant line.

5. Life Insurance Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

5.1 Assets

Because of the long-term nature of their liabilities (life insurance policyholders' claims are long-term) and the need to generate competitive returns on the savings elements of life insurance products, life insurance companies concentrate their asset investments at the longer end of the maturity spectrum. In 2020, 6.1 percent of assets were invested in government securities, 64.9 percent in corporate bonds and stocks, and 7.9 percent in mortgages. Policy loans (loans made to policyholders using their policies as collateral) constituted a smaller portion.

Insurance companies hold mortgages as investment securities—they purchase mortgages in secondary markets rather than originating them. Major trends include a long-term increase in the proportion of bonds and equities and a decline in the proportion of mortgages. Insurance company managers must measure and manage credit risk, interest rate risk, and other risks associated with these securities.

5.2 Liabilities

On the liability side, policy reserves ($3,355.7 billion, or 41.2 percent of total liabilities and capital) represent the expected payment commitment on existing policy contracts. These reserves are based on actuarial assumptions regarding expected future liability commitments, including death benefits, matured endowments, and cash surrender values (the cash value paid to the policyholder if the policy is surrendered before maturity).

Even though actuarial assumptions are normally very conservative, unexpected fluctuations in future required payouts can occur. Underwriting life insurance is risky because mortality rates might unexpectedly increase above historically based mortality tables due to events such as a catastrophic epidemic (e.g., AIDS or widespread influenza). To meet unexpected future losses, the life insurer holds a capital and surplus reserve fund. In 2020, capital and surplus reserves were $450.6 billion, or 5.5 percent of total assets.

Separate accounts (37.8 percent of total liabilities and capital in 2020) are funds established and held separately from the insurance company's other funds. These funds may be invested without regard to usual diversification restrictions (e.g., all stocks or all bonds). The payoff on the life insurance policy depends on the return on the funds in the separate account. Separate account assets also appear on the asset side of the balance sheet.

5.3 Profitability and Recent Trends

Insurance companies earn profits by taking in more premium income than they pay out in policy payments. Firms can increase their spread between premium income and policy payouts in two ways: (1) decrease future required payouts for any given level of premium payments (by reducing the risk of the insured pool, provided policyholders do not demand premium rebates that fully reflect lower expected payouts), and (2) increase the profitability of interest income on net policy reserves. Since insurance liabilities are typically long-term, the insurer has long periods to invest premium payments in interest-earning asset portfolios. The higher the yield on investments, the greater the difference between premium income and policy payouts (except for variable life insurance).

The financial crisis severely impacted life insurers. The value of stocks and bonds in asset portfolios dropped as financial markets deteriorated. Losses were experienced on commercial mortgage-backed securities, commercial loans, and lower-grade corporate debt. Lower equity market values reduced asset-based fees earned on equity-linked products such as variable annuities. Government bond yields fell as investors fled to safety, and historically low short-term interest rates prevented insurers from lowering minimum rates on new policies, encouraging higher surrender rates on existing policies. The result was a net after-tax loss of –$51.8 billion in 2008. The Treasury Department extended bailout funds, most notably $127 billion to AIG. By 2020, net income had recovered to $22.2 billion, though this was down 50.5 percent from 2019.

Key Takeaways

  • Life insurers invest in long-term assets (bonds, stocks, mortgages) to match long-term liabilities.
  • Policy reserves are the largest liability, based on actuarial assumptions about future payouts.
  • Separate accounts link policy payouts to underlying investment performance.
  • Profitability depends on the spread between premium income and policy payouts, plus investment returns.
  • The financial crisis demonstrated that asset value declines can severely threaten insurer solvency.

6. Regulation of Life Insurance

The McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 confirms the primacy of state over federal regulation of insurance companies. Unlike depository institutions, which can be chartered at the federal or state level, chartering of life insurers is done entirely at the state level. State insurance commissions supervise and examine insurance companies using a coordinated examination system developed by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC).

The Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 established the Federal Insurance Office (FIO), which reports to Congress and the president on insurance industry matters. While state regulation remains primary, the FIO has authority to monitor the insurance industry, identify regulatory gaps or systemic risk, deal with international insurance matters, and monitor access to affordable insurance products. The Act also established the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC), which designates financial institutions (including insurance companies) that present systemic risk for greater regulation.

The Federal Reserve became a major supervisor of insurance firms through designation of insurers as "systemically important financial institutions" (SIFIs). AIG, Prudential Financial, and MetLife were designated as SIFIs, though all three were subsequently de-designated (MetLife in 2016, AIG in 2017, Prudential in 2018). As of 2018, no nonbank currently holds the SIFI label. The Fed also supervises insurance companies that own savings institutions.

6.1 State Guarantee Funds

States promote life insurance guarantee funds to protect policyholders in the event of insurer failure. These differ from deposit insurance in several important ways:

  • They are sponsored by state regulators but run and administered by private insurance companies themselves.
  • No permanent guarantee fund exists (except in New York); contributions are paid by surviving firms only after a failure has occurred.
  • Required contributions vary by state; surviving insurers are normally levied a pro rata amount according to the size of their statewide premium income.
  • Because no permanent fund exists and annual pro rata payments are often legally capped, a delay usually occurs before small policyholders receive payment.

Key Takeaways

  • Insurance regulation is primarily at the state level (McCarran-Ferguson Act).
  • The FIO monitors the industry and identifies systemic risk at the federal level.
  • The FSOC can designate insurers as systemically important for enhanced regulation.
  • State guarantee funds differ from deposit insurance: no permanent fund, post-failure assessments, and potential payment delays.

7. Property–Casualty Insurance

Property insurance covers loss of real and personal property. Casualty (liability) insurance protects against legal liability exposures. The distinctions between these two areas are increasingly blurred as PC insurers offer multiple-activity line coverages combining features of both into single policy packages, such as homeowners multiple-peril insurance.

7.1 Size, Structure, and Composition

In 2020, approximately 2,476 companies sold property–casualty insurance, with total assets of $2.0 trillion (about 28 percent of the life insurance industry's assets). The industry is concentrated: the top 10 firms hold a 47.6 percent share of the market by direct premiums written, and the top 200 firms make up 96 percent. The top firm (State Farm) wrote 9.1 percent of all PC premiums in 2020, while the second-ranked (Berkshire Hathaway) wrote 6.4 percent. Industry leaders are increasing their market share through mergers and acquisitions.

Key Takeaways

  • PC insurance covers property loss (real and personal property) and liability (legal liability exposures).
  • The industry is concentrated, with the top 10 firms holding nearly half the market.
  • Multiple-peril policies increasingly combine property and liability features.

8. Types of Property–Casualty Insurance

Important PC lines include the following (with 2020 premium shares):

  • Fire insurance (2.2% of premiums): protects against fire, lightning, and removal of property damaged in a fire.
  • Homeowners multiple-peril (MP) insurance (14.9%): protects against multiple perils to personal dwelling and property, plus liability coverage for legal liability due to injury to others. Combines property and liability features.
  • Commercial multiple-peril insurance (6.3%): similar to homeowners MP but for commercial firms.
  • Automobile liability and physical damage (PD) insurance (38.7%): covers legal liability from vehicle ownership/use (auto liability) and theft/damage to vehicles (auto physical damage).
  • Liability insurance (other than auto) (11.8%): protects individuals or commercial firms against nonautomobile-related legal liability, including business operations liability and product liability hazards.

Key Takeaways

  • Fire insurance: declining share (16.6% in 1960 to 2.2% in 2020).
  • Homeowners MP: growing share (5.2% in 1960 to 14.9% in 2020).
  • Auto insurance (liability + physical damage): largest line at 38.7% of premiums.
  • Liability insurance (other than auto): growing share (6.6% in 1960 to 11.8% in 2020).

9. PC Insurance Balance Sheet and Underwriting Risk

9.1 Balance Sheet Structure

PC insurers invest the majority of their assets in long-term securities, subjecting them to credit and interest rate risks. In 2017, bonds ($1,006.3 billion), preferred stock ($5.1 billion), and common stock ($326.6 billion) constituted 65.7 percent of total assets. PC insurers hold long-term securities for two reasons: (1) to match the maturity of their longer-term contractual liabilities, and (2) because they have more uncertain payouts on insurance contracts (greater liquidity risk), so their asset structure includes many assets with relatively fixed returns that can be liquidated easily and at low cost.

The major liability component is the loss reserve and loss adjustment expenses ($658.8 billion, 32.3 percent of total liabilities and capital), set aside to meet expected losses from underwriting and administrative expenses. Unearned premiums ($258.4 billion, 12.7 percent) are a reserve containing the portion of a premium paid before insurance coverage has been provided.

9.2 Underwriting Risk

PC underwriting risk results when premiums generated on a given insurance line are insufficient to cover (1) claims (losses) incurred, (2) administrative expenses (legal expenses, commissions, taxes), after taking into account (3) investment income generated between the time premiums are received and claims are paid. Thus, underwriting risk may result from unexpected increases in loss rates, unexpected increases in expenses, and/or unexpected decreases in investment yields or returns.

9.2.1 Loss Risk

The predictability of claims loss exposure depends on several characteristics of the perils insured:

  • Property versus liability: Maximum levels of losses are more predictable for property lines than for liability lines. The monetary value of a damaged auto is relatively easy to calculate, while the upper limit of losses in a product liability line (e.g., asbestos damage) may be difficult or impossible to estimate.
  • Severity versus frequency: Loss rates are more predictable on low-severity, high-frequency lines (e.g., fire, auto, homeowners) than on high-severity, low-frequency lines (e.g., earthquake, hurricane, financial guaranty insurance). For high-frequency lines, the law of large numbers allows insurers to estimate expected losses within small probability bounds. For low-frequency lines, probabilities are not always stationary, individual risks are not independent, and severity could be enormous.
  • Long tail versus short tail: Some liability lines suffer from long-tail risk, where the insured event occurs during a coverage period but a claim is not filed until many years later. Losses incurred but not reported (IBNR) have caused significant problems in lines such as medical malpractice and other liability insurance where product damage suits (e.g., Dalkon shield, asbestos) have mushroomed many years after the event occurred.
  • Product inflation versus social inflation: Loss rates on property policies are adversely affected by unexpected increases in inflation. Liability lines may be subject to social inflation, reflected in juries' willingness to award punitive and other liability damages at rates far above the underlying rate of inflation. This has been particularly prevalent in commercial liability and medical malpractice insurance.

9.2.2 Expense Risk

The two major sources of expense risk are (1) loss-adjustment expenses (LAE), which relate to the costs of the loss settlement process (e.g., adjusters who determine liability and settlement size), and (2) commissions and other expenses, including costs paid to insurance brokers and sales agents. In 2020, expenses amounted to 27.3 percent of premiums written. The secular decline in the expense ratio has been driven by a switch from relying on independent brokers (American agency method) to selling insurance directly through the insurer's own brokers (direct writer method).

9.2.3 Investment Yield/Return Risk

When the combined ratio exceeds 100, overall profitability can be ensured only by sufficient investment return on premiums earned. PC firms invest premiums in assets between the time they are received and the time they are paid out to meet claims. The effect of interest rates and default rates on PC insurers' investments is crucial to overall profitability. Measuring and managing credit and interest rate risk are key concerns of PC managers.

Key Takeaways

  • PC insurers hold long-term assets (bonds, stocks) to match long-term liabilities, but face greater liquidity risk than life insurers.
  • Loss reserve and loss adjustment expenses are the largest liability.
  • Underwriting risk has three components: loss risk, expense risk, and investment yield risk.
  • Loss risk depends on property vs. liability, severity vs. frequency, long tail vs. short tail, and inflation type.
  • Investment yield risk is critical when combined ratios exceed 100.

10. Measuring Underwriting Performance: Key Ratios

10.1 Loss Ratio

Loss ratio measures the ratio of losses incurred (including loss-adjustment expenses) to premiums earned. A loss ratio less than 100 means premiums earned were sufficient to cover losses incurred on that line. The aggregate loss ratio for the PC industry has increased from the 60 percent range in the 1950s to the 70–80 percent range in recent decades, with a value of 70.2 percent in 2020.

Loss Ratio
\[ \text{Loss Ratio} = \frac{\text{Losses Incurred}}{\text{Premiums Earned}} \]

Explanation: Losses incurred include both pure losses and loss-adjustment expenses. Premiums earned are premiums received and earned on insurance contracts because time has passed with no claim being filed. The loss ratio measures the proportion of premium income consumed by claims and claim-related expenses. A ratio of 70.2 means that for every $100 of premium earned, $70.20 was paid out in losses and loss-adjustment expenses.

10.2 Expense Ratio

Expense ratio reflects commissions and other (non-LAE) expenses for PC insurers. In contrast to the increasing trend in the loss ratio, the expense ratio has generally decreased over time, reaching 27.3 percent in 2020.

Expense Ratio
\[ \text{Expense Ratio} = \frac{\text{Commissions and Other Expenses}}{\text{Premiums Written}} \]

Explanation: The expense ratio measures the proportion of premium income consumed by acquisition costs (commissions) and other operating expenses. The denominator is premiums written (not earned), reflecting that expenses are incurred when policies are written rather than over the life of the policy.

10.3 Combined Ratio

Combined ratio is a common measure of the overall underwriting profitability of a line. It equals the loss ratio plus the ratios of LAE to premiums earned, commissions and other acquisition costs and general expense costs to premiums written, plus any dividends paid to policyholders as a proportion of premiums earned. If the combined ratio is less than 100, premiums alone are sufficient to cover both losses and expenses. If it exceeds 100, the PC insurer must rely on investment income for overall profitability.

Combined Ratio (after dividends)
\[ \text{Combined Ratio} = \text{Loss Ratio} + \text{Expense Ratio} + \text{Dividend Ratio} \]

Explanation: The loss ratio includes LAE. The expense ratio includes commissions and other (non-LAE) expenses. The dividend ratio is dividends paid to policyholders divided by premiums earned. If the combined ratio is 109.7, expected losses, expenses, and dividends exceed premiums earned by 9.7 percent. The combined ratio before dividends excludes the dividend ratio.

Example

An insurance company has a projected loss ratio of 79.8 percent, an expense ratio of 27.9 percent, and pays 2.0 percent of premiums earned as dividends. The combined ratio after dividends = 79.8 + 27.9 + 2.0 = 109.7. Expected losses, expenses, and dividends exceed premiums earned by 9.7 percent.

10.4 Operating Ratio and Overall Profitability

Operating ratio measures the overall profitability of a PC insurer. It equals the combined ratio after dividends minus the investment yield (net investment income to premiums earned). If the operating ratio is less than 100, the insurer is profitable overall (investment income compensates for underwriting losses).

Operating Ratio and Overall Profitability
\[ \text{Operating Ratio} = \text{Combined Ratio (after dividends)} - \text{Investment Yield} \]
\[ \text{Overall Profitability} = 100 - \text{Operating Ratio} \]

Explanation: Investment yield is net investment income divided by premiums earned. If the combined ratio is 109.7 and the investment yield is 12.0 percent, the operating ratio = 109.7 – 12.0 = 97.7. Overall profitability = 100 – 97.7 = 2.3 percent. This means the insurer is profitable overall, but only because of high investment returns. If the investment yield were lower (e.g., 2.8 percent), the operating ratio would be 109.7 – 2.8 = 106.9, and overall profitability would be –6.9 percent (a loss).

Assumptions: This calculation assumes that underwriting losses and investment income are the only sources of profit. The operating ratio depends critically on the investment yield, which in turn depends on interest rates, credit conditions, and portfolio allocation.

Example

In 2012, PC insurers' combined ratio after dividends was 103.2 and investment yield was 10.5 percent, giving an operating ratio of 92.7 (profitable). In 2017, the combined ratio was 103.9. An investment yield of 10.5 percent would give an operating ratio of 93.4 (profitable), but a yield of 2.8 percent would give 101.1 (unprofitable). This illustrates the critical importance of investment returns to PC insurer profitability.

Key Insight

A combined ratio above 100 does not necessarily mean the insurer is unprofitable. If investment income is sufficiently high, the operating ratio can still be below 100, meaning the insurer is profitable overall. This is why measuring and managing credit and interest rate risk are key concerns for PC managers.

Key Takeaways

  • Loss ratio = losses incurred / premiums earned (measures claim cost relative to premium income).
  • Expense ratio = commissions and other expenses / premiums written (measures operating efficiency).
  • Combined ratio = loss ratio + expense ratio + dividend ratio (measures overall underwriting profitability).
  • Operating ratio = combined ratio − investment yield (measures overall profitability including investment income).
  • If combined ratio < 100, premiums alone cover losses and expenses. If combined ratio > 100, investment income is needed for profitability.
  • Overall profitability = 100 − operating ratio. Positive values indicate profit; negative values indicate loss.

11. Reinsurance and Catastrophe Bonds

11.1 Reinsurance

Reinsurance is essentially insurance for insurance companies. It allows primary insurers to protect against unforeseen or extraordinary losses. Reinsurance can enable the insurer to improve its capital position, expand its business, limit losses, and stabilize cash flows. The reinsurer, drawing information from many primary insurers, typically has a far larger pool of data for assessing risks. Reinsurance may represent a layer of risk (e.g., losses within certain limits, such as $5 million to $10 million) or a sharing of both losses and profits for certain types of business. About 75 percent of reinsurance business from U.S. insurers is written by non-U.S. reinsurers such as Munich Re and Swiss Re.

Key Insight

Reinsurance is an international business, and large catastrophes can generate losses exceeding the reasonable carrying capacity of the reinsurance industry. Primary insurers hedging their tail risk face the possibility that when they need coverage most (after a major catastrophe), the entire insurance industry may be heavily affected and the reinsurer may face payment difficulties.

11.2 Catastrophe Bonds

Catastrophe bonds (cat bonds) are a form of insurance-linked security (ILS) that emerged after Hurricane Andrew and the Northridge Earthquake in the mid-1990s forced several insurers into bankruptcy and dried up reinsurance capacity. Cat bonds function like a reinsurance contract structured over several years or a single year. When the sponsor's property damage losses exceed a specified indemnity trigger (e.g., $2 million), the bond absorbs the financial impact up to a stated limit (e.g., $3 million). Other cat bonds use parametric triggers such as earthquake magnitude or wind speed.

Cat bonds offer value to investors through higher yields than traditional bonds (such as Treasuries, asset-backed securities, and commercial mortgage-backed securities) and portfolio diversification benefits because catastrophic property damage risks do not correlate with the risks of other asset classes. A potential challenge to the cat bond market is rising interest rates, which may encourage institutional investors to focus on rising yields in government and corporate bonds rather than cat bonds.

Key Takeaways

  • Reinsurance is insurance for insurers, providing protection against extraordinary losses.
  • Catastrophe bonds are securities that transfer catastrophe risk to capital market investors.
  • Cat bonds offer diversification benefits because catastrophe risk is uncorrelated with market risk.
  • Rising interest rates may reduce investor demand for cat bonds.

12. Recent Trends in Property–Casualty Insurance

The PC insurance industry experiences underwriting cycles—periods of rising premiums leading to increased profitability, followed by softening premiums as supply increases. The period 1985–2017 was characterized by significant catastrophe losses and generally low profitability. Major catastrophes included Hurricane Hugo (1989), San Francisco earthquake (1991), Hurricane Andrew (1991, $20 billion in losses), Northridge earthquake (1994, $7–10 billion), the 9/11 terrorist attacks (2001, $19 billion), Hurricanes Katrina/Rita/Wilma (2005, $57.7 billion in catastrophe losses), Hurricane Ike (2008, $12.5 billion), Hurricane Sandy (2012, $18.8 billion), and the 2017 hurricanes Harvey/Irma/Maria ($19 billion, $25 billion, and $30 billion respectively).

In 2006 and 2007, low catastrophe losses and strong performance in other lines produced combined ratios of 92.4 (best since 1936) and 95.6. The financial crisis pushed the 2008 combined ratio to 105.1. Profitability improved in 2013–2015 with combined ratios of 96.3, 97.0, and 97.6. The 2017 hurricane season drove the combined ratio to 103.9. In 2018–2020, the combined ratio improved to 99.1, 98.7, and 98.7, respectively, due to sustained premium growth, prior-year reserve releases, and lower loss costs in the auto market that offset catastrophe losses and pandemic-related losses.

Key Takeaways

  • The PC industry follows an underwriting cycle with alternating hard and soft markets.
  • Catastrophe losses have been a major driver of underwriting losses and combined ratio volatility.
  • Excluding catastrophe losses, the underlying combined ratio has been below 100 in many years.
  • Premium growth, reserve releases, and auto market conditions have helped offset catastrophe losses in recent years.

13. Regulation of Property–Casualty Insurance

Like life insurers, PC insurers are chartered by states and regulated by state commissions. State guaranty funds provide some protection to policyholders if an insurer fails. The NAIC provides a standardized examination system and the Insurance Regulatory Information System (IRIS) to identify insurers with loss, combined, and other ratios outside normal ranges.

PC insurers face rate regulation in some activity lines, especially auto insurance and workers' compensation insurance. State commissioners set ceilings on premiums and premium increases based on specific cost of capital and line risk exposure formulas. This has led some insurers to leave states with the most restrictive regulations (e.g., New Jersey, Florida, California).

The industry faced controversy over homeowners' claims handling after Hurricane Katrina. Homeowners' policies excluded flood damage. Insurers classified the storm surge as a flood and denied coverage. Policyholders argued the surge was a direct result of hurricane winds (a covered risk). A 2007 verdict held State Farm responsible for policy limits totaling more than $220,000 on each loss deemed due to storm-surge flooding, plus punitive damages.

Key Takeaways

  • PC insurers are state-regulated, with NAIC providing standardized oversight.
  • Rate regulation in auto and workers' compensation lines caps premium increases.
  • Disputes over policy interpretation (e.g., flood vs. wind damage) can lead to significant litigation and liability.

14. Global Issues in Insurance

The insurance sector is becoming increasingly global. The United States, Japan, and Western Europe dominate the global market, but all regions are engaged in insurance, and many insurers operate internationally. Table 6–8 shows the top 10 countries by total premiums written in 2020: the United States ($2,530.6 billion, 40.3% global share), China ($655.9 billion, 10.4%), Japan ($414.8 billion, 6.6%), the United Kingdom ($338.3 billion, 5.4%), and Germany ($258.6 billion, 4.1%) lead the market.

Global insured losses from natural catastrophes and man-made disasters have been significant. In 2011, the Japan earthquake/tsunami ($40 billion insured losses), New Zealand earthquakes ($13 billion), Thailand floods ($10 billion), and U.S. tornadoes ($14.2 billion) contributed to $350 billion in disaster losses. In 2017, global insured losses reached $144 billion, the highest ever recorded, driven by hurricanes Harvey, Irma, and Maria, California wildfires, and severe weather worldwide. In 2021, natural disasters caused $280 billion in total losses, with roughly $120 billion insured, according to Munich Re.

Key Takeaways

  • The U.S. dominates the global insurance market with 40.3% of premiums written.
  • Global catastrophe losses have been increasing, with 2017 setting a record for insured losses.
  • Central governments in many countries cover a significant portion of catastrophe losses, reducing the burden on insurers.

15. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

The following are important cautions and common errors in the analysis and interpretation of insurance company operations and performance:

  • Confusing the combined ratio with overall profitability: A combined ratio above 100 does not necessarily mean the insurer is unprofitable. Investment income can offset underwriting losses. The operating ratio (combined ratio minus investment yield) is the correct measure of overall profitability.
  • Ignoring the time value of money in loss reserves: Loss reserves on the balance sheet represent expected future payouts, but the actual timing of claims payments can significantly affect the present value of liabilities. Long-tail lines (e.g., medical malpractice, product liability) are particularly sensitive to this.
  • Misinterpreting the loss ratio: The loss ratio includes loss-adjustment expenses, not just pure losses. The combined ratio adds expense and dividend ratios. Using only the loss ratio to assess profitability is incomplete.
  • Ignoring the role of investment income: PC insurers rely heavily on investment income to achieve profitability. Changes in interest rates, credit spreads, and equity market returns can have a dramatic impact on the operating ratio, even if underwriting performance is stable.
  • Confusing adverse selection with moral hazard: Adverse selection is about who buys insurance (those who need it most). Moral hazard (not covered in this chapter) is about how insured behavior changes after obtaining coverage. Both are distinct risks.
  • Assuming claims are independent: For high-severity, low-frequency lines (earthquake, hurricane, financial guaranty), individual risks are not independent. The law of large numbers does not apply, and expected loss estimation is extremely difficult.
  • Ignoring social inflation: Liability losses can be driven by jury awards and litigation trends that far exceed general inflation. Using historical loss data without adjusting for social inflation can lead to significant underestimation of required reserves.
  • Treating premiums earned and premiums written as interchangeable: The loss ratio uses premiums earned (denominator), while the expense ratio uses premiums written. These denominators differ because premiums are earned over time, while expenses are incurred when policies are written.
  • Overlooking the impact of reinsurance: Reinsurance can significantly alter the risk profile of a primary insurer. An insurer's net exposure after reinsurance may be much smaller than its gross exposure. Analysts must distinguish between gross and net positions.
  • Confusing the role of policyholder surplus: Policyholder surplus (capital and surplus) is a buffer against unexpected losses. A low surplus-to-assets ratio indicates higher insolvency risk. PC insurers typically hold more surplus relative to assets than life insurers due to greater uncertainty in loss estimation.

Key Takeaways

  • Combined ratio > 100 does not imply unprofitability; investment income must be considered.
  • Loss ratios and expense ratios use different denominators (premiums earned vs. premiums written).
  • High-severity, low-frequency lines violate the independence assumption of the law of large numbers.
  • Social inflation can cause liability losses to outpace general inflation.
  • Reinsurance and policyholder surplus are critical risk management tools.

16. Summary and Key Takeaways

This chapter has examined the activities, balance sheets, regulation, and risk profile of life insurance and property–casualty insurance companies. The key conceptual distinctions are:

  • Life insurance protects against mortality risk (death) and morbidity risk (ill health), and includes savings components in many policy forms. The industry has shifted toward annuities as the primary business line.
  • Property–casualty insurance protects against property loss and legal liability. The industry faces significant underwriting risk from catastrophe losses, long-tail claims, and social inflation.
  • Both industries are regulated primarily at the state level, with federal oversight through the FIO and FSOC for systemic risk.
  • Profitability depends on both underwriting performance (measured by the combined ratio) and investment returns (measured by the investment yield).
  • Reinsurance and catastrophe bonds provide mechanisms for transferring risk to other parties.

Key Insight

The fundamental economic function of insurance companies is risk pooling and risk transfer. By collecting premiums from many policyholders and investing those premiums in diversified portfolios, insurers can absorb losses that would be catastrophic for any individual policyholder. The success of this model depends on accurate risk assessment, appropriate pricing, adequate reserves, and effective asset-liability management. The financial crisis of 2008 demonstrated that when these mechanisms fail, the consequences can be systemic.

Key Takeaways

  • Life insurance: mortality risk protection, savings components, long-term asset investment.
  • Property–casualty insurance: property loss and liability protection, significant underwriting risk from catastrophes.
  • Combined ratio measures underwriting profitability; operating ratio measures overall profitability including investment income.
  • State-level regulation is primary, with federal oversight for systemic risk.
  • Reinsurance and catastrophe bonds are key risk transfer mechanisms.
  • Adverse selection requires risk-based pricing and population segmentation.
  • The financial crisis demonstrated that insurance company failures can create systemic risk to the entire financial system.
