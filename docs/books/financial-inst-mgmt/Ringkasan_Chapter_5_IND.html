1. Introduction to Mutual Funds and Hedge Funds

Mutual funds and hedge funds are financial institutions that pool the financial resources of individuals and companies and invest in diversified portfolios of assets. The core function of these pooled investment vehicles is to provide access to diversified portfolios that individual investors could not efficiently construct on their own.

Mutual Fund (open-ended): A financial intermediary that continuously stands ready to sell new shares to investors and to redeem outstanding shares on demand at their fair market value. This provides small investors with opportunities to invest in financial securities and diversify risk while generating economies of scale through lower transaction costs and commissions.

Hedge Fund: A type of investment pool that solicits funds from wealthy individuals and other investors (e.g., commercial banks) and invests these funds on their behalf. Hedge funds are similar to mutual funds in that they are pooled investment vehicles, but investments are restricted to wealthier clients, and they are subject to less regulatory oversight.

The mutual fund industry experienced tremendous growth in the 1990s due to rising equity values (e.g., the S&P 500 index returned more than 25% in 1997 and 1998) and the relatively low-cost opportunity mutual funds provide to investors. The 2008–2009 financial crisis caused a $2.4 trillion drop in industry assets (20%). Despite these setbacks, by 2020 more than 7,600 different mutual fund companies held total assets of $23.90 trillion.

Key Insight

Mutual funds are the most important financial intermediary group in the United States as measured by asset size, having surpassed the commercial banking industry. This growth has not gone unnoticed by commercial banks, which have sought to compete by buying existing mutual fund groups or managing mutual fund assets for a fee.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual funds pool resources from individuals and companies to invest in diversified portfolios.
  • Open-ended mutual funds continuously issue and redeem shares at fair market value.
  • Hedge funds are restricted to wealthy investors and face less regulation.
  • The mutual fund industry has grown to surpass commercial banking in asset size.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Mutual Fund Industry

Historical Trends: The first mutual fund was founded in Boston in 1924. By 1970, 361 funds held about $50 billion in assets. Since then, the industry has grown dramatically, driven by the advent of money market mutual funds in 1972, tax-exempt money market funds in 1979, and the explosion of special-purpose equity, bond, emerging market, and derivative funds in the 1990s.

Total assets invested in mutual funds grew from $0.45 billion in 1940 to $11,999.71 billion in 2007, then fell to $9,620.27 billion in 2008 during the financial crisis, and recovered to $23,895.84 billion by 2020. The number of mutual funds increased from 68 in 1940 to 8,040 in 2007, then declined to 7,636 in 2020.

Low barriers to entry in the U.S. mutual fund industry have allowed new entrants to offer funds, keeping the industry from being increasingly concentrated. Despite this, the largest 25 companies that sponsor mutual funds managed 81% of the industry's assets in 2020, compared to 25% in 1990.

Key Insight

Growth has been significantly driven by the rise in retirement funds under management by mutual funds. The retirement fund market increased from $4 trillion in 1990 to $34.9 trillion in 2020, with mutual funds managing approximately 70% of this market. Institutional funds—mutual funds that manage retirement plans for an institution's employees—account for about 80% of all retirement plan investments.

3. Types of Mutual Funds and Risk Profiles

Long-term funds include equity funds (comprised of common and preferred stock securities), bond funds (comprised of fixed-income securities with maturity longer than one year), and hybrid funds (comprised of both bond and stock securities). Short-term funds include taxable money market mutual funds (MMMFs) and tax-exempt money market mutual funds.

Long-term equity funds are typically well diversified, with risk being more systematic or market-based. Bond funds have extensive interest rate risk due to their long-term, fixed-rate nature. Sector or industry-specific funds have both systematic (market) and unsystematic risk. The principal type of risk for short-term funds is interest rate risk, but because of the shortness of maturity of assets (often less than 60 days), this risk is mitigated. Short-term funds generally have virtually no liquidity or default risk due to the types of assets held.

Money market mutual funds provide an alternative investment to interest-bearing deposits at commercial banks. The major difference is that interest-bearing deposits (below $250,000) are fully insured by the FDIC, but due to bank regulatory costs, they generally offer lower returns than noninsured MMMFs. The net gain in switching to MMMFs is higher returns in exchange for the loss of deposit insurance coverage.

Key Insight

The share of long-term vs short-term funds varies considerably over time based on market conditions. In 2000, 73.5% of all mutual fund assets were in long-term funds and 26.5% in money market funds. During the 2008–2009 financial crisis, the share of long-term funds plunged to 60.2% while money market funds increased to 39.8%. By 2020, long-term funds had recovered to 81.9% of all funds.

4. Mutual Fund Objectives and Investment Strategies

Mutual fund objective: General information about the types of securities a mutual fund will hold as assets, specified in a prospectus available to potential investors. Regulations require that mutual fund managers specify these investment objectives, which must include a list of the securities the fund holds.

There are 12 major categories of investment objectives for mutual funds. For example, capital appreciation funds hold securities (mainly equities) of high-growth, high-risk firms. Total return funds focus on a balance of growth and income. World funds invest in securities of issuers located outside the United States.

Index funds: A growing number of long-term mutual funds where fund managers buy securities in proportions similar to those included in a specified major stock index (such as the S&P 500 index). These funds are designed to match the performance of a stock index. In 2020, 490 index funds managed total net assets of $4.8 trillion, compared to 6,806 active funds with assets of $14.8 trillion.

Actively managed funds generally underperform index funds. Only 26% of all active funds had a 10-year success rate above their index fund counterparts. The cheapest funds succeeded about twice as often as the priciest ones (35% success rate versus 18%) over the 10-year period ending December 31, 2021.

Key Insight

Passive investment strategies (index funds and ETFs) have grown significantly, accounting for 40% of combined U.S. mutual fund and ETF assets under management in 2020, up from 4% in 1996. Factors contributing to this shift include the efficient markets hypothesis, lower costs of passive investing, evidence of underperformance of active managers, and greater regulatory focus on fees.

5. Investor Returns and Net Asset Value (NAV)

The return an investor receives from investing in mutual fund shares reflects three aspects of the underlying portfolio:

  1. Income and dividends earned on assets.
  2. Capital gains that occur when assets are sold by a mutual fund at prices higher than the purchase price.
  3. Capital appreciation in the underlying values of the assets held in a fund's portfolio.

Marked-to-market: Mutual fund assets are normally marked-to-market daily, meaning managers calculate the current value of each mutual fund share by computing the daily market value of the fund's total asset portfolio and dividing by the number of shares outstanding.

Net Asset Value (NAV): The price an investor gets when selling shares back to the fund or buying new shares. It is equal to the market value of the assets in the mutual fund portfolio divided by the number of shares outstanding.

Net Asset Value (NAV)
\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Market Value of Total Asset Portfolio}}{\text{Number of Shares Outstanding}} \]

Explanation of symbols:

  • NAV: Net Asset Value, the per-share price of the mutual fund.
  • Market Value of Total Asset Portfolio: The sum of the current market values of all securities held in the fund.
  • Number of Shares Outstanding: The total number of mutual fund shares held by all investors.

Mathematical relationship: The NAV is a simple ratio that converts the total market value of the fund's assets into a per-share price. As the market value of the underlying securities increases, the NAV increases proportionally, assuming no change in the number of shares outstanding.

Practical use: Investors use the NAV to determine the price at which they can buy or sell shares in the mutual fund. For open-end mutual funds, the NAV is the transaction price. For money market mutual funds, the NAV is typically fixed at $1 per share, with fluctuations in asset values reflected by changes in the number of shares owned.

Important assumption: The NAV calculation assumes that all assets can be valued at current market prices. For illiquid securities, fair value pricing may be used to estimate the market value.

Example: Impact of Capital Appreciation on NAV

Suppose a mutual fund contains 4,000 shares of Macy's at $22.00, 1,200 shares of Exxon/Mobil at $77.00, and 1,500 shares of AT&T at $33.75. The fund has 15,000 shares outstanding.

Today's NAV = [(4,000 × $22.00) + (1,200 × $77.00) + (1,500 × $33.75)] ÷ 15,000 = $15.402

If next month Macy's increases to $26.50, Exxon/Mobil to $78, and AT&T to $35, the NAV (assuming the same shares outstanding) increases to:

NAV = [(4,000 × $26.50) + (1,200 × $78) + (1,500 × $35)] ÷ 15,000 = $16.807

Interpretation: The NAV increased from $15.402 to $16.807 due to capital appreciation of the underlying securities. The return to the investor over the month was ($16.807 – $15.402) ÷ $15.402 = 9.12%.

Practical lesson: The NAV reflects the market value of the fund's portfolio on a per-share basis. Investors benefit from capital appreciation through an increase in NAV.

Key Insight

For open-end mutual funds, the NAV is determined solely by the market value of the underlying securities divided by the number of shares outstanding. The number of shares fluctuates daily with redemptions and new purchases, but the NAV is always based on the current market value of the portfolio.

Key Takeaways

  • Investor returns come from income/dividends, capital gains, and capital appreciation.
  • Mutual funds are marked-to-market daily, with NAV calculated as total asset value divided by shares outstanding.
  • NAV is the transaction price for buying and selling shares in open-end mutual funds.
  • Changes in NAV reflect changes in the market value of the underlying portfolio.

6. Open-End vs Closed-End Funds and Exchange-Traded Funds

Open-end mutual fund: The supply of shares in the fund is not fixed but can increase or decrease daily with purchases and redemptions of shares. Investors buy and sell shares from and to the mutual fund company. The NAV is determined solely by the market value of the underlying securities divided by the number of shares outstanding.

Closed-end investment company: A specialized investment company that invests in securities and assets of other firms but has a fixed supply of shares outstanding. Investors must buy and sell shares on a stock exchange. Since the number of shares is fixed, the market price is determined not only by the NAV but also by demand for the shares themselves. Shares can trade at a premium (above NAV) or a discount (below NAV).

Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF): An investment company with shares that trade intraday on stock exchanges at market-determined prices. Like a mutual fund, an ETF offers investors a proportionate share in a pool of stocks, bonds, and other assets. ETFs differ from traditional mutual funds in how their shares are issued and redeemed and how they are traded.

Example: Closed-End Fund Premium and Discount

Premium scenario: A closed-end fund has 50 shares trading at $20 per share, but the market value of the asset portfolio is $800 (NAV = $16 per share). The shares trade at a premium of $4 per share.

Discount scenario: A second closed-end fund has 100 shares trading at $25 per share, but the NAV is $30 per share. The shares trade at a discount of $5 per share.

Interpretation: In a closed-end fund, the market price reflects both the value of the underlying assets and the demand for the fund's shares. A premium indicates high demand; a discount indicates low demand.

Practical lesson: Investors in closed-end funds face an additional source of return (or loss) from changes in the premium or discount, separate from the performance of the underlying assets.

Key Insight

ETFs combine features of open-end and closed-end funds: they have a fixed number of shares like closed-end funds but trade at prices based on NAV like open-end funds. ETFs can be traded intraday, purchased on margin, and sold short. They offer tax efficiency because investors are subject to capital gains taxes only when they sell their shares. Assets invested in ETFs grew from $66 billion in 2000 to $5.45 trillion in 2020.

Key Takeaways

  • Open-end funds have a flexible number of shares; closed-end funds have a fixed number.
  • Closed-end funds can trade at a premium or discount to NAV, creating additional risk/return.
  • ETFs trade intraday on exchanges, offer tax efficiency, and are largely passive investment vehicles.
  • Authorized participants (APs) create and redeem ETF shares at NAV, keeping market prices close to NAV.

7. Mutual Fund Costs: Loads, Fees, and Expense Ratios

Load fund: A mutual fund with an upfront sales or commission charge (sometimes as high as 5.75%) that must be paid by the investor. No-load fund: A mutual fund that does not charge upfront fees or commission charges on the sale of shares. The argument in favor of load funds is that managers provide more personal attention, but the cost may not be worthwhile.

12b-1 fees: Annual fees relating to the distribution and other operating costs of mutual fund shares. These fees cannot exceed 1% of a fund's average net assets per year, with marketing and servicing costs capped at 0.25% per year and management fees capped at 0.75% per year.

Share classes: Funds sold through financial professionals offer different share classes (A, B, C, and institutional shares) with different mixes of sales loads and management and 12b-1 fees. Class A shares carry a front-end load; Class B shares have no front-end load but have a back-end load and higher annual fees; Class C shares have no front-end load but have a back-end load in the first year and continuing annual fees. Institutional shares (I, R, N, X, Y) carry the lowest fees and are available to high-net-worth investors and retirement plans.

Annualized Total Shareholder Cost
\[ \text{Total Shareholder Cost} = \text{Annualized Sales Load} + \text{Annual Fund Expense Ratio} \]

Explanation of terms:

  • Annualized Sales Load: The one-time sales load divided by the expected holding period in years. For example, a 4% front-end load held for 10 years gives an annualized load of 0.4% per year.
  • Annual Fund Expense Ratio: The total annual fund operating expenses (including 12b-1 fees, management fees, and other costs) expressed as a percentage of average net assets.

Practical use: Investors should compare the total annualized cost of different funds, accounting for both one-time loads and ongoing expenses. A no-load fund with a 1% expense ratio may be cheaper than a load fund with a lower expense ratio, depending on the holding period.

Important assumption: The annualized load calculation assumes the investor holds the shares for the entire period. If the holding period is shorter, the annualized cost is higher.

Example: Calculation of Mutual Fund Costs

An investor purchases fund shares with a 4% front-end load and expects to hold the shares for 10 years. The annualized sales load is 4% ÷ 10 = 0.4% per year. The fund has a total expense ratio of 1% per year. The annual total shareholder cost is 0.4% + 1% = 1.4% per year.

Interpretation: Over the 10-year holding period, the investor effectively pays 1.4% of assets per year in total costs. If the holding period were only 2 years, the annualized load would be 4% ÷ 2 = 2% per year, making the total cost 3% per year.

Practical lesson: The longer the investment horizon, the smaller the impact of a one-time load fee. Short-term investors should prefer no-load funds.

Key Insight

Expense ratios have been declining across all fund types. Investors paid 0.71% on average for actively managed equity mutual funds in 2020, down from 1.06% in 2000. Expense ratios for index equity funds and ETFs are even lower, at 0.05% or less for institutional shares. This trend reflects increased competition, regulatory scrutiny, and the shift toward passive investing.

Key Takeaways

  • Load funds charge upfront commissions; no-load funds do not.
  • 12b-1 fees cover distribution and marketing costs, capped at 1% per year.
  • Different share classes offer different fee structures; institutional shares have the lowest fees.
  • Total shareholder cost includes both annualized sales load and annual expense ratio.
  • Expense ratios have been declining steadily, benefiting investors.

8. Balance Sheet and Recent Trends in the Mutual Fund Industry

Money Market Mutual Funds (MMMFs): In 2021, $4,680.50 billion (89.9% of total assets) was invested in short-term financial securities. The major asset categories include security repurchase agreements (47.9% of total), Treasury securities (34.9%), and agency- and GSE-backed securities (7.9%). Most consumer-oriented MMMF shares have their values fixed at $1, with fluctuations adjusted by increasing or reducing the number of shares owned.

Long-Term Funds: In 2021, long-term funds held financial assets totaling $21,208.70 billion, of which 69.5% were corporate equities. The asset composition reflects the popularity of different types of bond or equity funds at any point in time. During the financial crisis, equities fell to 55.5% of long-term fund portfolios, while credit market instruments rose to 41.9%.

Key balance sheet trends: Money market funds moved from corporate bonds to safer U.S. government securities during the financial crisis. In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic caused a "dash for cash," with government money market funds receiving $834 billion in inflows in March alone. The Federal Reserve established the Money Market Fund Liquidity Facility (MMLF) to stabilize the market.

Key Insight

Money market mutual funds experienced two major liquidity crises in the past 15 years: the 2008 financial crisis (Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck") and the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic. In both cases, the federal government intervened to stabilize the industry. The SEC has responded with multiple rounds of reforms, including liquidity requirements, floating NAV for institutional prime funds, and the proposed removal of redemption gates.

9. Regulation of Mutual Funds

Primary regulator: The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is the primary regulator of mutual funds. Key legislation includes:

  • Securities Act of 1933: Requires mutual funds to file a registration statement with the SEC and sets rules regarding the fund's prospectus.
  • Securities Exchange Act of 1934: Makes the purchase and sale of mutual fund shares subject to various antifraud provisions and requires full and accurate information disclosure.
  • Investment Company Act of 1940: Sets out rules to prevent conflicts of interest, fraud, and excessive fees or charges for fund shares.
  • Investment Advisers Act of 1940: Regulates the activities of mutual fund advisors.
  • National Securities Markets Improvement Act (NSMIA) of 1996: Exempts mutual fund sellers from oversight by state securities regulators, reducing regulatory burden.
  • Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002: Requires public companies (including mutual fund companies) to ensure their boards' audit committees have appropriate financial expertise.

Abusive activities identified in the early 2000s: Market timing (short-term trading exploiting stale prices), late trading (buying/selling after the 4 PM price was set), directed brokerage (arrangements influencing broker recommendations), and improper assessment of fees. These led to new rules requiring independent boards, chief compliance officers, and improved fee disclosure.

Money market fund reforms: The SEC adopted amendments to Rule 2a-7 in 2010 and 2014, including liquidity requirements (daily liquid assets minimum of 10%, weekly liquid assets minimum of 30%), floating NAV for institutional prime funds, and liquidity fees and redemption gates. In 2021, the SEC proposed further reforms including increased liquidity minimums (25% daily, 50% weekly), removal of redemption gates, and swing pricing.

Key Insight

Regulation of mutual funds has evolved significantly in response to market abuses and financial crises. The 2004 reforms required chief compliance officers reporting directly to fund directors, "hard closing" of trading orders by 4 PM, and improved disclosure of fees and risks. The 2010 and 2014 money market reforms aimed to address systemic risk, while the 2021 proposed reforms continue to refine the regulatory framework.

Key Takeaways

  • The SEC is the primary regulator of mutual funds, with key legislation from the 1930s and 1940s.
  • Abusive activities (market timing, late trading, directed brokerage, fee issues) led to significant reforms.
  • Money market fund reforms have increased liquidity requirements and changed operational structures.
  • Chief compliance officers are required to monitor compliance with regulations.

10. Global Issues in the Mutual Fund Industry

Worldwide investment in mutual funds has grown significantly. Combined assets invested in non-U.S. mutual funds are approximately 15% greater than those invested in U.S. mutual funds. Non-U.S. mutual fund assets increased from $4.545 trillion in 1999 to $14.130 trillion in 2007, fell to $9.316 trillion in 2008, and recovered to $33.71 trillion by 2020.

The worldwide mutual fund market is most active in countries with the most sophisticated securities markets (e.g., Japan, France, Germany, Australia, and the United Kingdom). The U.S. mutual fund industry has worked to lower barriers to entry overseas, particularly in Europe, Japan, and China.

11. Hedge Funds: Characteristics and Distinctions

Hedge fund: A type of investment pool that solicits funds from wealthy individuals and other investors and invests these funds on their behalf. Hedge funds are similar to mutual funds in that they are pooled investment vehicles, but they are not subject to the numerous regulations that apply to mutual funds for the protection of individuals.

Key differences from mutual funds:

  • Hedge funds are not subject to regulations requiring a certain degree of liquidity.
  • Mutual fund shares must be redeemable at any time; hedge funds have more restrictions.
  • Hedge funds are not subject to regulations protecting against conflicts of interest or ensuring fairness in pricing.
  • Hedge funds do not have to disclose their full activities to third parties, offering a high degree of privacy.
  • Until 2010, hedge funds were not required to register with the SEC.
  • Hedge funds can use aggressive strategies including short selling, leveraging, program trading, arbitrage, and derivatives trading.

Investor requirements: To be an "accredited investor," an individual must have a net worth of more than $1 million or an annual income of at least $200,000 ($300,000 if married). This allows hedge funds to avoid regulation under the theory that wealthy individuals can evaluate risk and return on their investments.

In June 2021, there were more than 8,200 hedge funds in the world with managed assets estimated at $3.96 trillion, compared to $23.90 trillion in mutual fund assets and $5.45 trillion in ETF assets.

Example: Hedge Fund Long-Short Strategy

A hedge fund may buy (take a long position in) a bond expecting its price to rise. At the same time, the fund will borrow (take a short position in) another bond and sell it, promising to return the borrowed bond in the future. If prices go up as expected, the hedge fund gains on the bond it purchased while losing on the bond it borrowed. The profit is the difference between the gain and the loss. If prices fall, the profit comes from the bond it borrowed (since it can be repurchased at a lower price).

Interpretation: The simultaneous long and short positions minimize the risk of overall losses, regardless of the direction of price changes. The hedge fund profits from the relative price movement between the two bonds.

Practical lesson: Hedge funds use sophisticated strategies that are not available to mutual funds, but these strategies carry significant risk, as demonstrated by the near-collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) in 1998.

Key Insight

Despite their name, hedge funds do not always "hedge" their investments to protect against market price declines. The near-collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) in 1998, which required a $3.6 billion bailout brokered by the Federal Reserve, demonstrated that hedge fund strategies can fail catastrophically when market conditions deviate from historical patterns.

12. Types of Hedge Funds by Risk Classification

Hedge fund managers follow a variety of investment strategies, classified by risk level:

More Risky (Market Directional): These funds seek high returns using leverage, typically investing based on anticipated events.

  • Aggressive growth: Invest in equities expected to experience acceleration in earnings per share growth. Hedge by shorting equities where earnings disappointment is expected or by shorting stock indexes.
  • Emerging markets: Invest in equity or debt securities of emerging markets, which tend to have higher inflation and volatile growth.
  • Macro funds: Aim to profit from changes in global economies, typically brought about by shifts in government policy that impact interest rates. Use leverage and derivatives.
  • Market timing: Allocate assets among different asset classes depending on the manager's view of economic or market outlook.
  • Short selling: Sell securities in anticipation of buying them back at a lower price based on assessment of overvaluation.

Moderate Risk (Market Neutral or Value Orientation): These funds have moderate exposure to market risk, typically favoring a longer-term investment strategy.

  • Distressed securities: Buy equity, debt, or trade claims at deep discounts of companies in or facing bankruptcy.
  • Funds of funds: Mix hedge funds and other pooled investment vehicles to provide more stable long-term returns.
  • Opportunistic: Change investment strategy as opportunities arise (IPOs, price changes, takeover bids).
  • Multistrategy: Implement various strategies simultaneously to realize short- and long-term gains.
  • Special situations: Invest in event-driven situations such as mergers, hostile takeovers, reorganizations, or leveraged buyouts.

Risk Avoidance: These funds strive for moderate, consistent returns with low risk.

  • Income funds: Focus on yield or current income rather than capital gains.
  • Market neutral–arbitrage: Hedge market risk by taking offsetting positions, often in different securities of the same issuer.
  • Market neutral–securities hedging: Invest equally in long and short equity portfolios in particular market sectors.
  • Value funds: Invest in securities perceived to be selling at deep discounts relative to their intrinsic values.

Key Insight

Hedge fund performance has been cyclical, with strong returns in the 1990s and early 2000s, poor performance during the 2008 financial crisis, and generally lagging returns in the 2010s. From 2010 to 2018, hedge funds made 3.4% on average per year, compared with 6.4% from 2000 to 2010 and 18.3% from 1989 to 2000. More than 3,000 hedge funds were liquidated between 2015 and 2018.

13. Hedge Fund Fees and Performance

Hedge fund managers generally charge two types of fees:

Management fee: Computed as a percentage of total assets under management, typically between 1.5% and 2.0%. This fee covers operating costs and is similar to mutual fund expense ratios.

Performance fee: Unique to hedge funds, this gives the fund manager a share of any positive returns. The average performance fee has long been around 20% but varies widely. Performance fees are paid to the manager before returns are paid to fund investors.

Hurdle rate: A minimum annualized performance benchmark that must be realized before a performance fee can be assessed. Often tied to a benchmark rate such as the S&P 500.

High-water mark: A provision that the manager does not receive a performance fee unless the value of the fund exceeds the highest net asset value it has previously achieved. This links the manager's incentives more closely to those of investors and reduces the incentive to increase risk.

Standard 2/20 fee model: 2% management fee and 20% performance fee. This model is slowly giving way to new structures due to investor dissatisfaction with low returns. For example, Adage Capital Management changed to a flat 0.5% management fee and 20% performance fee on trading gains or losses in excess of the S&P 500, with refunds if performance falls below the benchmark.

Explanation of terms:

  • Management Fee: A fixed percentage of assets under management, typically 1.5–2.0% per year.
  • Performance Fee: A percentage of positive returns (typically 20%), paid to the manager before returns are distributed to investors.

Practical use: Investors should consider the total fee structure when evaluating hedge fund investments. The combination of high management fees and performance fees can significantly reduce net returns, especially in periods of low market returns.

Important assumption: The high-water mark provision ensures that performance fees are only paid on net new profits, not on recovery from losses. This aligns manager and investor interests.

Key Insight

The standard 2/20 fee model is under significant pressure due to poor hedge fund performance in the 2010s. Investors are increasingly dissatisfied with fees, leading to the withdrawal of funds (e.g., CalPERS liquidated its $4 billion hedge fund portfolio in 2014). New fee structures include lower management fees, hurdle rates, and performance fees tied to benchmark outperformance.

Key Takeaways

  • Hedge funds charge management fees (1.5–2% of assets) and performance fees (typically 20% of profits).
  • Hurdle rates and high-water marks protect investor interests.
  • The 2/20 fee model is under pressure, with new structures offering lower fees and benchmark-based performance fees.
  • Fee compression is a major trend in the hedge fund industry.

14. Regulation of Hedge Funds

Historically, hedge funds were generally unregulated, operating under two exemptions from registration requirements under the Investment Company Act of 1940: (1) funds with fewer than 100 investors, and (2) funds where investors are "accredited." Hedge funds were also sold only via private placements and could not be offered or advertised to the general public.

Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010: This act required hedge fund advisors with private pools of capital exceeding $100 million in assets to register with the SEC as investment advisors by July 2011. Key provisions include:

  • Registration with the SEC subjects hedge funds to periodic inspections.
  • Hedge funds must report information about their trades and portfolios that is "necessary for the purpose of assessing systemic risk posed by a private fund."
  • Data are kept confidential and can be shared only with the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC).
  • If the government determines a hedge fund has grown too large or risky, it is placed under the supervision of the Federal Reserve.
  • Hedge fund managers with less than $100 million in assets are overseen by state regulators.

Offshore hedge funds: Funds located outside the United States and structured under foreign laws. Major centers include the Cayman Islands (estimated 85% of all hedge funds), Bermuda, Dublin, and Luxembourg. Offshore funds are not subject to U.S. income taxes on distributions of profit or to U.S. estate taxes on fund shares. They are regulated by the host country, but rules are generally not burdensome and provide anonymity.

Key Insight

While the 2010 act requires large hedge funds to register with the SEC, the regulations imposed on hedge funds continue to be much less onerous than those imposed on mutual funds. Hedge funds are prohibited from abusive trading practices, but they retain significant flexibility in their investment strategies. The regulatory focus is on systemic risk monitoring rather than investor protection in the traditional sense.

Key Takeaways

  • Hedge funds were historically exempt from SEC registration under the 1940 Act.
  • The 2010 Wall Street Reform Act requires advisors with >$100 million in assets to register with the SEC.
  • Registered hedge funds are subject to periodic inspections and must report portfolio data for systemic risk assessment.
  • Offshore hedge funds offer tax advantages and anonymity, with the Cayman Islands being the primary location.
  • Despite increased regulation, hedge funds face less oversight than mutual funds.

15. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

Incorrect interpretation of coefficients and returns: A mutual fund's return does not necessarily reflect the skill of the fund manager; it may simply reflect overall market movements. Index funds often outperform actively managed funds, so past performance is not a reliable indicator of future performance.

Confusing prediction with causality: The fact that a mutual fund has a high NAV does not mean it will continue to perform well. NAV reflects the current market value of the portfolio, not future returns. Similarly, hedge fund returns are not necessarily risk-adjusted returns.

Ignoring model assumptions: The calculation of NAV assumes that all assets can be valued at current market prices. For illiquid securities, fair value pricing may be used, which can introduce estimation error. The assumption that money market funds maintain a $1 NAV was violated in 2008 when Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck."

Misinterpreting the intercept: In the context of fund performance, the "intercept" (or alpha) is often interpreted as the manager's skill. However, this interpretation depends on the validity of the benchmark model used and the assumption that all risk factors are properly accounted for.

Ignoring units of measurement: Expense ratios and load fees are expressed as percentages of assets. A 1% expense ratio on a $10,000 investment is $100 per year, but this amount compounds over time and can significantly reduce returns. The impact of fees is often underestimated.

Confusing error and residual: In the context of fund performance evaluation, the difference between actual returns and expected returns (based on a benchmark) is often called "alpha." However, this alpha may reflect omitted risk factors rather than true manager skill.

Omitted-variable problems: When evaluating hedge fund performance, the inability to explain returns of individual hedge funds with standard multifactor risk models leaves open the possibility that it is not possible to properly measure the risk associated with at least some hedge fund strategies. Risk-adjusted returns may be overstated.

Market timing and late trading: Market timing exploits short-term discrepancies between the price of a mutual fund's shares and out-of-date values on the securities in the fund's portfolio. Late trading involves buying or selling mutual fund shares after the 4 PM price has been set. Both are illegal practices.

Leverage risk: Hedge funds that use leverage can experience amplified losses. The Bear Stearns hedge funds that collapsed in 2007 used substantial leverage to invest in subprime mortgage securities, and when the market moved against them, the losses were catastrophic. The failure to expect extreme price movements and insufficient hedging led to complete loss of capital.

Key Insight

Never imply that obtaining a coefficient or return statistic automatically establishes causality or skill. The performance of a fund may be due to luck, market conditions, or risk exposure rather than managerial ability. Proper evaluation requires risk-adjusted performance measures and careful consideration of the benchmark.

Key Takeaways

  • Past performance does not guarantee future results; index funds often outperform active funds.
  • NAV calculations assume assets can be valued at current market prices; illiquid securities may require estimation.
  • Expense ratios and load fees can significantly reduce returns over time; always consider total costs.
  • Hedge fund returns may not be properly risk-adjusted; standard multifactor models may not capture all risks.
  • Leverage amplifies both gains and losses; hedge fund failures often involve excessive leverage and insufficient hedging.
  • Market timing and late trading are illegal practices that exploit pricing inefficiencies.
  • Correlation does not imply causation; fund performance requires careful attribution analysis.
