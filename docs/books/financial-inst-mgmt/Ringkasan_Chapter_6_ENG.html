1. Introduction: Insurance Services and Systemic Risk

Insurance services protect individuals and corporations—collectively called policyholders—from adverse events. By accepting premiums (periodic payments made by policyholders), an insurance company promises compensation if specified events occur. Each policy is a contractual promise, and the expected payouts under these policies represent financial liabilities to the insurance company. The premiums collected are not held idle; insurance companies invest them in financial securities such as corporate bonds and stocks, earning investment income that supplements the premium stream.

Insurance services are classified into two major groups. Life insurance protects against the possibility of untimely death, illness, and retirement. Property–casualty (PC) insurance protects against personal injury and liability such as accidents, theft, and fire. Many financial institutions (FIs)—for example, MetLife and Allstate—offer both life and PC services, and insurers often sell investment products similar to mutual funds and banking services.

Interpretation

The essence of insurance is a risk transfer: the policyholder pays a known, relatively small premium to avoid the financial consequence of a large, uncertain loss. The insurer, in turn, bears the risk but does so by combining many independent risks. This is why insurance companies are financial intermediaries: they collect premiums (a liability) and invest them in financial assets, earning a spread while also providing a valuable risk-management service.

Example: AIG and the 2008 Financial Crisis

The financial crisis demonstrated how much risk insurance companies can pose to FIs and the global financial system. As subprime mortgage markets failed in 2008, mortgage-backed securities lost value sharply. AIG had written massive volumes of credit default swaps (CDSs)—contracts offering credit protection (a form of insurance) against default on mortgage securities. As losses rose, CDS buyers demanded payment, and AIG could not cover billions of dollars in CDS losses. The federal government bailed out AIG with roughly $127 billion because AIG was a dominant CDS writer; had AIG defaulted, every FI that had purchased CDS contracts from it would have suffered substantial losses.

Practical lesson: an insurer's liabilities can become systemically important. The risk exposure created by an insurer's promises extends beyond its own policyholders to other financial institutions that rely on the insurer's performance.

Analogy

Buying insurance is like pooling money with a large group of neighbors to repair anyone's roof if it is damaged by a storm. Everyone pays a small fee (premium); the unlucky few receive large payments (claims). The system works only if the pool is large and the risks are spread widely.

Key Insight

An insurance policy is simultaneously a liability to the insurer and a risk-management asset to the policyholder. The insurer must hold sufficient assets and capital to meet those liabilities. The AIG case shows that when an insurer's liabilities exceed its capacity to pay, the failure can transmit losses through the entire financial system. This is a matter of solvency and liquidity risk, not merely underwriting skill.

Key Takeaways

  • Insurance is a risk-transfer mechanism: premiums are paid by policyholders; compensation is promised upon specified events.
  • Insurance promises create financial liabilities; premiums are invested to generate returns.
  • Two major groups: life insurance (death, illness, retirement) and property–casualty insurance (injury, liability, theft, fire).
  • Insurer failures can transmit risk to other financial institutions, as shown by AIG's CDS losses in 2008.

2. Core Concepts: Risk Pooling, Adverse Selection, and the Economics of Insurance

Life insurance allows individuals and their beneficiaries to protect against income losses from premature death or retirement. The method is pooling risks: by combining many insured individuals, the insurer transfers income-related uncertainty from the individual to the group. The insurer uses statistical averages (for example, mortality tables and health statistics) to estimate the probability of paying out on a policy and sets the premium accordingly.

A central problem facing both life and PC insurers is the adverse selection problem: customers who apply for insurance policies are more likely to be those most in need of insurance. For example, someone with chronic health problems is more likely to purchase a life insurance policy than someone in perfect health. If the insurer uses population-wide statistics to price policies, it will underprice because its pool of customers is sicker than the overall population.

Interpretation

The insurer's response is to establish different pools of the population based on health, income, and related characteristics. By altering the pool used to determine the probability of losses to match each customer's characteristics, the insurer can more accurately estimate the expected payout and adjust the premium accordingly. This is the actuarial foundation of insurance pricing: premium = expected claims cost + expenses + a return on capital.

Insurance Pricing Logic (Conceptual)
\[ \text{Premium} \approx \text{Expected Payout} + \text{Administrative Expenses} + \text{Required Return on Capital} \]

Premiums are the payments policyholders make for coverage. Expected payout is the frequency of loss times the severity of loss, estimated for the insured pool. Administrative expenses include commissions, legal costs, and loss-adjustment costs. Required return on capital compensates owners for bearing residual risk. The practical implication is that pool selection determines accuracy: using the wrong pool (general population rather than the actual applicant pool) produces premiums that are too low and exposes the insurer to underwriting losses.

Adverse selection is a technical term for the tendency of high-risk individuals to seek insurance more actively than low-risk individuals. Left uncontrolled, it raises the average claim rate in the insured pool above the population average, making population-based pricing inadequate.

Example

A life insurer pricing a policy for a 60-year-old applicant uses mortality tables. If the insurer simply uses the general population's mortality rate, it may set too low a premium, because the applicant pool is self-selected: healthy people may not buy, while those with known health issues do. The insurer therefore underwrite each applicant into risk classes (e.g., smoker/non-smoker, healthy/pre-existing conditions) and charges each class a premium reflecting its own expected claims.

Analogy

Adverse selection is like a used-car market: the people most eager to sell a car are often those who know it is unreliable. If the buyer ignores this and prices based on the average car, he overpays for lemons. Insurance companies avoid this by screening applicants—like a buyer getting a mechanic's inspection.

Key Insight

Actuarial fairness is pool-specific, not population-wide. An insurer's pricing is only valid if the statistical pool used to estimate losses matches the risk characteristics of the actual insured pool. Adverse selection is managed by segmentation (risk classification), not by ignoring self-selection.

Key Takeaways

  • Insurance works through risk pooling: many insureds share the cost of losses that hit a few.
  • Adverse selection arises because those most likely to claim are also most likely to buy insurance.
  • Insurers mitigate adverse selection by grouping policyholders into pools with similar health and risk characteristics.
  • Accurate pricing requires that the statistical pool match the actual customer pool.

3. Life Insurance: Size, Structure, and Ownership

The U.S. life insurance industry is large but has consolidated dramatically. In 2020, approximately 750 life insurance companies operated in the United States, compared with more than 2,300 in 1988. Aggregate assets were $8.1 trillion in 2020, up from $1.1 trillion in 1988. Concentration is high: the 10 largest life insurers held 45.6 percent of the industry's $8.1 trillion in assets and wrote 40.6 percent of its $1.1 trillion in direct premiums written in 2020. The largest insurer by assets, Prudential of America Group, held $680.2 billion in assets; by premiums, Metropolitan Life Insurance Companies ranked first with $103.3 billion in direct premiums written.

Consolidation has occurred through major mergers (e.g., Anthem and Cigna, Hartford Life and Mass Mutual, MetLife and American Life Insurance) driven by competition within the industry and from other FIs. Many of the largest insurers, such as Metropolitan Life and Prudential, converted from mutual companies to stockholder-controlled companies. A mutual insurer is owned by its policyholders; profits accumulate as reserves owned by policyholders. A stock insurer is owned by stockholders, who receive dividends and own the accumulated profits. Conversion gives insurers access to equity markets to raise capital for expansion and to compete with consolidating banks. As of 2020, 74 percent of life insurance companies were stock companies.

Interpretation

Ownership form matters because it determines who owns the accumulated surplus. In a mutual, the policyholders own the accumulated profits (often distributed as policy dividends); in a stock company, stockholders own the profits. The shift toward stock ownership is driven by capital needs, not by any change in the fundamental insurance function.

Example: Ownership Conversion

When a mutual insurer converts to a stock insurer, the existing capital and reserves (equal to accumulated past profits) must be distributed to the insurer's policyholders. In exchange, the new stock company gains access to equity markets. This is why conversion is a strategic financial decision, not merely an accounting change.

Analogy

A mutual insurer is like a consumer cooperative: the customers (policyholders) own the store and share its profits. A stock insurer is like a regular corporation: outside shareholders own it, and customers are simply customers.

Key Insight

Industry concentration and the shift to stock ownership reflect a need for capital, not a change in the risk-pooling function. Larger scale enables insurers to compete in a consolidating financial services market, and stock ownership provides access to equity capital for expansion.

Key Takeaways

  • The number of U.S. life insurers fell from over 2,300 (1988) to about 750 (2020); assets grew to $8.1 trillion.
  • The top 10 life insurers hold 45.6% of industry assets and write 40.6% of direct premiums.
  • Mutual insurers are owned by policyholders; stock insurers are owned by stockholders.
  • Conversion to stock ownership provides access to equity capital; 74% of life insurers are stock companies.

4. Life Insurance Products: Ordinary, Group, Industrial, and Credit Life

The four basic classes of life insurance are distinguished by the manner in which they are sold or marketed: ordinary life, group life, industrial life, and credit life. In 2020, ordinary life accounted for approximately 78.7 percent of the $188.0 billion in direct premiums written; group life accounted for 20.9 percent; industrial life and credit life together accounted for less than 1 percent.

Ordinary life policies are marketed on an individual basis, usually in units of $1,000, with periodic premium payments. There are five basic contractual types. The first three are traditional forms; the last two are newer contracts originating in the 1970s and 1980s as a result of increased competition for savings from other segments of the financial services industry.

  • Term life: the closest to pure life insurance, with no savings element. The individual receives a payout contingent on death during the coverage period. Coverage can range from 1 year to 40 years or more.
  • Whole life: protects the individual over an entire lifetime. In return for periodic level premiums, beneficiaries receive the face value of the policy on death. Because death is certain, a payment is guaranteed (if premiums continue), giving whole life a savings element as well as a pure insurance element.
  • Endowment life: combines a pure term insurance element with a savings element. It guarantees a payout to beneficiaries if death occurs during an endowment period (e.g., before retirement age). A person who lives to the endowment date receives the face amount.
  • Variable life: fixed premium payments are invested in mutual funds of stocks, bonds, and money market instruments, often chosen by the policyholder to reflect risk preferences. The policy's value increases or decreases with the asset returns of the chosen funds, providing an alternative way to build savings.
  • Universal life and variable universal life: universal life allows the insured to change both premium amounts and the maturity of the contract, unlike traditional level-premium, fixed-period policies. In variable universal life, premiums are also invested in money, equity, or bond mutual funds, so the savings component reflects market returns.

Group life covers a large number of insured persons under a single policy, usually issued to corporate employers. It may be contributory (employer and employee share the cost) or noncontributory (employer pays the full cost). The principal advantage is cost economies from mass administration, lower individual evaluation costs (less medical screening), and reduced selling and commission costs.

Industrial life involves small policies with weekly payments collected directly by company representatives. Its growth has been largely eliminated by the growth of group life insurance.

Credit life is sold to protect lenders against a borrower's death before repayment of a debt contract such as a mortgage or car loan. The face amount usually reflects the outstanding principal and interest on the loan.

Interpretation

The distinction between insurance and savings components is central. Term life is pure protection; whole life, endowment, variable, and universal policies bundle protection with a savings/investment vehicle. Universal life is flexible: the policyholder can adjust premiums and maturity, and in variable versions the savings value depends on market returns rather than a fixed credited rate.

Example

A 35-year-old buys a 20-year term life policy of $500,000. If she dies within 20 years, beneficiaries receive $500,000; if she survives, no payout occurs. A whole life policy of $100,000 with level premiums funds a guaranteed death benefit plus an accumulating cash value. A variable universal life policyholder can raise or lower premiums and direct the savings component into an equity mutual fund; the cash value rises and falls with the fund's performance.

Analogy

Term life is like renting protection: you pay for coverage for a period, and if nothing happens, you get nothing back. Whole life is like renting with an escrow account: part of your payment builds an owned cash value. Universal life is a flexible escrow: you can vary contributions and, in variable form, choose how the escrow is invested.

Key Insight

Traditional level-premium policies guarantee fixed payout promises, while newer variable and universal products transfer investment risk to the policyholder. In variable life, the insurer's liability is linked to the performance of separate account assets, so the policyholder bears the market risk. This distinction shapes the insurer's asset-liability management and capital requirements.

Key Takeaways

  • Four distribution classes: ordinary (78.7% of premiums), group (20.9%), industrial, and credit life (together less than 1%).
  • Term life = pure protection; whole life = lifetime protection with savings; endowment = term plus savings paying face value on survival to a date.
  • Variable life invests premiums in mutual funds; policy value varies with returns.
  • Universal life allows flexible premiums and maturity; variable universal life ties savings to market returns.
  • Group life offers cost economies; credit life protects a lender against borrower death.

5. Annuities, Pension Plans, and Accident & Health Insurance

Three other major life insurer activities are the sale of annuities, private pension plans, and accident and health insurance.

Annuities are the reverse of life insurance. Life insurance builds up a fund; annuities liquidate a fund by paying out its proceeds over time. Annuities can be sold to individuals or groups on a fixed or variable basis (the return linked to an underlying investment portfolio), purchased with a single payment or payments spread over several years, and structured to begin payments immediately or to be deferred. The annuity's fund is tax deferred: returns are not subject to capital gains taxes while funds accumulate. Payments may cease on death or continue to beneficiaries for a number of years. A life insurance policy (whole or universal life) requires regular premiums and pays a lump sum at death; an annuity converts a lump sum into regular cash payments until the fund is depleted.

Annuity business is now the major line for life insurers. Annuity sales (direct premiums written) topped $369.7 billion in 2020 ($206.5 billion of which were ordinary annuities), compared with $26 billion in 1996 and roughly twice the $188.0 billion in traditional life insurance sales.

Private pension plans include guaranteed investment contracts (GICs), in which the insurer guarantees both the rate of interest credited to a pension plan over a given period (e.g., five years) and the annuity rates on beneficiaries' contracts. Other plans include immediate participation and separate account plans that follow more aggressive investment strategies, such as investing premiums in special-purpose equity mutual funds.

Accident and health insurance protects against morbidity, or ill health risk, whereas life insurance protects against mortality. Over $209.8 billion in direct premiums were written in this area in 2020. The major activity line is group insurance providing health coverage to corporate employees; life insurance companies write more than 50 percent of all health insurance premiums.

Interpretation

Annuities allow individuals to convert accumulated wealth into a steady income stream, solving the reverse problem of life insurance (the risk of outliving one's assets). Tax deferral makes annuities attractive savings
