Chapter 6 Summary

Financial Services: Insurance Companies

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 6. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

Insurance services offered by FIs protect individuals and corporations (policyholders) from adverse events. By accepting premiums, FIs that offer insurance services promise policyholders compensation if certain specified events occur, and these policies represent financial liabilities to the insurance company. With the premiums collected, insurance companies invest in financial securities such as corporate bonds and stocks. Insurance services are classified into two major groups, life and property-casualty (PC). Life insurance provides protection against the possibility of untimely death, illnesses, and retirement, while property-casualty insurance protects against personal injury and liability such as accidents, theft, and fire. Many FIs (for example MetLife and Allstate) offer both life and property-casualty services, and many also sell investment products such as mutual funds and banking services.

The financial crisis showed just how much risk insurance companies can present to FIs and the global financial system. As the subprime mortgage market began to fail in the summer of 2008, subprime mortgage pools and the securities written on them fell precipitously in value. Many credit default swaps (CDSs) were written on these subprime mortgage securities, and AIG was a major writer of these CDS securities. As mortgage security losses started to rise, AIG had to make good on billions of dollars of credit default swaps and soon it became clear that AIG was not going to be able to cover its losses, significantly increasing the risk exposure of banks, investment banks, and insurance companies that had purchased AIG CDS insurance contracts. The reason the federal government stepped in and bailed out AIG was that the insurer was a dominant player in the CDS market. Had AIG defaulted, every FI that had bought a CDS contract from the company would have suffered substantial losses.

2. Life Insurance

Life insurance allows individuals and their beneficiaries to protect against losses in income through premature death or retirement. By pooling risks, life insurance transfers income-related uncertainties from the insured individual to a group.

2.1 Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

In 2020, the United States had approximately 750 life insurance companies, compared with more than 2,300 in 1988. The aggregate assets of life insurance companies were $8.1 trillion in 2020, compared with $1.1 trillion in 1988. The 10 largest life insurance companies represented 45.6 percent of the industry's $8.1 trillion assets and wrote 40.6 percent of the industry's $1.1 trillion direct premiums written in 2020. The industry has seen major mergers in recent years (for example Anthem and Signa, Hartford Life and Mass Mutual, and MetLife and American Life Insurance). Many of the largest insurance companies, such as Metropolitan and Prudential, have converted to stockholder-controlled companies to gain access to the equity markets for additional capital to finance future business expansions, and as of 2020, 74 percent of all life insurance companies are stock companies. In contrast, a mutual company is owned by its policyholders, so the existing capital and reserves have to be distributed to the insurer's policyholders.

One problem that naturally faces life insurance companies (as well as PC insurers) is the so-called adverse selection problem, where customers who apply for insurance policies are more likely to be those most in need of insurance, for example someone with chronic health problems is more likely to purchase a life insurance policy than someone in perfect health. Thus, using health statistics representing the overall population may not be appropriate for calculating the probability of having to pay out on a contract. Insurance companies deal with adverse selection by establishing different pools of the population based on health and related characteristics (such as income), allowing more accurate determination of the probability of payouts and adjustment of premiums accordingly.

2.2 Types of Life Insurance

The four basic classes or lines of life insurance are distinguished by the manner in which they are sold or marketed, namely (1) ordinary life, (2) group life, (3) industrial life, and (4) credit life. Of the policies in force in the United States, ordinary life accounted for approximately 78.7 percent, group life for 20.9 percent, and industrial life and credit life together for less than 1 percent of the $188.0 billion direct premiums written in 2020. Ordinary life involves policies marketed on an individual basis, usually in units of $1,000, with periodic premium payments, and has essentially five basic contractual types.

  • Term life, the closest to pure life insurance with no savings element, with a payout contingent on death during the coverage period, which can vary from as little as 1 year to 40 years or more
  • Whole life, protecting the individual over an entire lifetime in return for periodic or level premiums, with beneficiaries receiving the face value on death, so it has a savings element as well as a pure insurance element
  • Endowment life, combining a pure (term) insurance element with a savings element, guaranteeing a payout to beneficiaries if death occurs during some endowment period, with an insured person who lives to the endowment date receiving the face amount
  • Variable life, investing fixed premium payments in mutual funds of stocks, bonds, and money market instruments that policyholders can choose to reflect their risk preferences, so the value of the policy increases or decreases with the asset returns of the mutual fund
  • Universal life and variable universal life, allowing both the premium amounts and the maturity of the contract to be changed by the insured, and for some contracts investing premiums in money, equity, or bond mutual funds so the savings component reflects market returns

Group life insurance covers a large number of insured persons under a single policy, usually issued to corporate employers, and may be either contributory (where both the employer and employee cover a share of the cost) or noncontributory. Cost economies represent the principal advantage of group life, resulting from mass administration, lower costs for evaluating individuals through medical screening and other rating systems, and reduced selling and commission costs. Industrial life insurance, involving weekly payments directly collected by company representatives, now represents a very small area of coverage because the growth of group life has led to its demise as a major activity class. Credit life insurance is sold to protect lenders against a borrower's death prior to the repayment of a debt contract such as a mortgage or car loan, with the face amount usually reflecting the outstanding principal and interest on the loan.

2.3 Other Life Insurer Activities

Three other major activities of life insurance companies involve the sale of annuities, private pension plans, and accident and health insurance. Annuities represent the reverse of life insurance activities, since whereas life insurance involves different contractual methods of building up a fund, annuities involve different methods of liquidating a fund, such as paying out a fund's proceeds. Annuities can be sold to an individual or a group on a fixed or variable basis by being linked to the return on an underlying investment portfolio, purchased with a single payment or payments spread over a number of years, and build up a fund whose returns are tax deferred. Payments may be structured to start immediately or be deferred, and may cease on death or continue to be paid to beneficiaries for a number of years after death. Annuity sales (or direct premiums written) in 2020 topped $369.7 billion ($206.5 billion of which were ordinary annuities), compared with $26 billion in 1996, and this is twice the $188.0 billion in sales for the traditional life insurance lines, showing that annuities have become the major area of business for life insurance companies.

Example 6-1. Calculation of the Fair Value of an Annuity Policy

Suppose that a person wants to purchase an annuity today that would pay $15,000 per year until the end of that person's life. The insurance company expects the person to live for 25 more years and can invest the amount received for the annuity at a guaranteed interest rate of 5 percent. The fair price for the annuity policy today is calculated as the present value of 25 annual payments of $15,000

\[ \text{Fair value} = \frac{15{,}000}{1+r} + \frac{15{,}000}{(1+r)^2} + \dots + \frac{15{,}000}{(1+r)^{25}} \]
\[ \text{Fair value} = 15{,}000 \times \text{PVIFA}_{r=5\%, n=25} = 15{,}000 \times 14.0939 = \$211{,}409 \]

Thus, the cost of purchasing this annuity today would be $211,409.

Private pension plans are offered by life insurance companies to private employers, including innovative plans based on guaranteed investment contracts (GICs), under which the insurer guarantees not only the rate of interest credited to a pension plan over a given period (for example five years) but also the annuity rates on beneficiaries' contracts. Other plans include immediate participation and separate account plans that follow more aggressive investment strategies, such as investing premiums in special-purpose equity mutual funds. Accident and health insurance protects against morbidity, or ill health, risk, with more than $209.8 billion in direct premiums written by life and health companies in the accident-health area in 2020, and life insurance companies write more than 50 percent of all health insurance premiums.

2.4 Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

Because of the long-term nature of their liabilities (as a result of the long-term nature of life insurance policyholders' claims) and the need to generate competitive returns on the savings elements of life insurance products, life insurance companies concentrate their asset investments at the longer end of the maturity spectrum, for example bonds, equities, and government securities. In 2020, 6.1 percent of assets were invested in government securities, 64.9 percent in corporate bonds and stocks, and 7.9 percent in mortgages, with other loans including policy loans (loans made to policyholders using their policies as collateral) making up the balance. The major trends have been a long-term increase in the proportion of bonds and equities and a decline in the proportion of mortgages, so insurance company managers must be able to measure and manage the credit risk, interest rate risk, and other risks associated with these securities.

On the liability side of the 2020 balance sheet, policy reserves (the expected payment commitment on existing policy contracts) were $3,355.7 billion, or 41.2 percent of total liabilities and capital. These reserves are based on actuarial assumptions regarding expected future liability commitments, including death benefits, matured endowments, and the cash surrender values of policies (the cash value paid to the policyholder if the policy is surrendered before it matures). Because unexpected fluctuations in future required payouts can occur, for example mortality rates might unexpectedly increase due to a catastrophic epidemic illness, the life insurer holds a capital and surplus reserve fund with which to meet such losses. The capital and surplus reserves of life insurers in 2020 were $450.6 billion, or 5.5 percent of total assets. Separate account business represented 37.8 percent of total liabilities and capital in 2020, where a separate account is a fund established and held separately from the insurance company's other funds, which may be invested without regard to the usual diversification restrictions, and the payoff on the policy depends on the return on the funds in the separate account.

Insurance companies earn profits by taking in more premium income than they pay out in policy payments. Firms can increase their spread between premium income and policy payouts in two ways, (1) decreasing future required payouts for any given level of premium payments by reducing the risk of the insured pool, and (2) increasing the profitability of interest income on net policy reserves, since the higher the yield on the insurance company's investments, the greater the difference between the premium income stream and the policy payouts (except in the case of variable life insurance).

The life insurance industry was very profitable in the early and middle years of the first decade of the 2000s, with net income topping $34 billion in 2006. However, the financial crisis took a toll, with realized and unrealized capital losses from bonds, preferred stocks, and common stocks topping $35 billion in 2008, representing more than an 875 percent drop from 2007, and net investment income falling 3.5 percent. The result was that net after-tax income for the year was minus $51.8 billion, $83.7 billion less than in 2007. The Treasury Department decided to extend bailout funds to a number of struggling life insurance companies, the most notable being $127 billion to AIG (including $45 billion from TARP, $77 billion to purchase collateralized debt and mortgage-backed securities, and a $44 billion bridge loan). Other life insurers receiving TARP funds included Hartford Financial Services Group, Prudential Financial, Lincoln National, and Allstate. Premium income fell by $120 billion (19 percent) from 2008 levels, but late 2009 saw improvements, with total assets increasing by more than $200 billion and net income returning to a positive $21.1 billion. The 2011 premiums of $634.0 billion surpassed the pre-crisis (2007) level of $608.3 billion, and over 2012 to 2020, industry premium and annuity income stayed flat around $635 billion. However, net income fell 50.5 percent in 2020 to $22.2 billion from $44.7 billion in 2019, contributing to the decline being a 36.2 percent decline in net income before capital gains and a capital loss of $10.7 billion.

2.5 Regulation of Life Insurance

An important legislation affecting the regulation of life insurance companies is the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, which confirms the primacy of state over federal regulation of insurance companies. Unlike the depository institutions, which can be chartered either at the federal or the state level, chartering of life insurers is done entirely at the state level, and state insurance commissions supervise and examine insurance companies using a coordinated examination system developed by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC).

In 2009, the U.S. Congress considered establishing an optional federal insurance charter, a move that picked up steam following the failure of the state by state regulatory system to prevent the problems at AIG from becoming a systemic risk. The final version of the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 established the Federal Insurance Office (FIO), which reports to Congress and the president, with authority to monitor the insurance industry, identify regulatory gaps or systemic risk, deal with international insurance matters, and monitor the extent to which underserved communities have access to affordable insurance products. The act also established the Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC), charged with designating any financial institution (including insurance companies) presenting a systemic risk and subjecting it to greater regulation, and made the Federal Reserve a major supervisor of insurance firms through the designation of three of the largest insurers (AIG, Prudential Financial, and MetLife) as systemically important financial institutions (SIFIs). However, in March 2016 a U.S. District Court removed MetLife's SIFI designation, AIG's designation was removed in October 2017, and Prudential's in October 2018, meaning that no nonbank currently has the SIFI label.

In addition to supervision and examination, states promote life insurance guarantee funds. Unlike banks and thrifts, life insurers have no access to a federal guarantee fund (although during the financial crisis the federal government took the unprecedented step of bailing out several major insurance companies). These state guarantee funds differ from deposit insurance in a number of important ways. First, although these programs are sponsored by state insurance regulators, they are actually run and administered by the private insurance companies themselves. Second, unlike the DIF, in which the FDIC has established a permanent reserve fund, no such permanent guarantee fund exists for the insurance industry, with the sole exception of the PC and life guarantee funds in the state of New York, meaning contributions are paid into the guarantee fund by surviving firms only after an insurance company has actually failed. Third, the size of the required contributions varies widely from state to state, with each surviving insurer normally levied a pro rata amount according to the size of its statewide premium income, and the definition of small policyholders varies among states in the range of holding policies from $100,000 to $500,000. Fourth, because no permanent fund exists and the annual pro rata payments are often legally capped, a delay usually occurs before small policyholders receive the cash surrender values of their policies, contrasting with deposit insurance, which normally provides insured depositors immediate coverage up to $250,000.

3. Property-Casualty (PC) Insurance

Property insurance involves insurance coverages related to the loss of real and personal property, while casualty, or more accurately liability, insurance concerns protection against legal liability exposures. However, the distinctions between the two broad areas are increasingly becoming blurred due to the tendency of PC insurers to offer multiple-activity line coverages combining features of property and liability insurance into single policy packages, for example homeowners multiple-peril insurance.

3.1 Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

Some 2,476 companies sell property-casualty insurance, with approximately half of these firms writing PC business in all or most of the United States. The total assets of the PC industry in 2020 were $2.0 trillion, approximately 28 percent of the life insurance industry's assets. The U.S. PC insurance industry is quite concentrated, with the top 10 firms having a 47.6 percent share of the overall PC market measured by direct premiums written, and the top 200 firms making up 96 percent. In 2020, the top firm (State Farm) wrote 9.1 percent of all PC insurance premiums, and the second-ranked insurer (Berkshire Hathaway) wrote 6.4 percent, a joint total of 15.5 percent, up from 14.5 percent for the top two firms in 1985, much of this consolidation coming through mergers and acquisitions.

3.2 Types of Property-Casualty Insurance

Important PC lines include the following, with the 2020 premium share compared to 1960 in parentheses.

  • Fire insurance, protecting against the perils of fire, lightning, and removal of property damaged in a fire (2.2 percent of all premiums in 2020, 16.6 percent in 1960)
  • Homeowners multiple-peril (MP) insurance, protecting against multiple perils of damage to a personal dwelling and personal property as well as providing liability coverage, combining features of both property and liability insurance (14.9 percent in 2020, 5.2 percent in 1960)
  • Commercial multiple-peril insurance, protecting commercial firms against perils, similar to homeowners multiple-peril (6.3 percent in 2020, 0.4 percent in 1960)
  • Automobile liability and physical damage (PD) insurance, providing protection against legal liability due to the ownership or use of the vehicle and theft of or damage to vehicles (38.7 percent in 2020, 43.0 percent in 1960)
  • Liability insurance (other than auto), providing individuals or commercial firms with protection against nonautomobile-related legal liability, including liabilities relating to business operations and product liability hazards (11.8 percent in 2020, 6.6 percent in 1960)

3.3 The Balance Sheet and Underwriting Risk

Similar to life insurance companies, PC insurers invest the majority of their assets in long-term securities, subjecting them to credit and interest rate risks. In 2017, bonds ($1,006.3 billion), preferred stock ($5.1 billion), and common stock ($326.6 billion) constituted 65.7 percent of total assets. PC insurers hold mainly long-term securities for two reasons, (1) to match the maturity of their longer-term contractual liabilities, and (2) because they have more uncertain payouts on their insurance contracts (greater levels of liquidity risk), their asset structure includes many assets with relatively fixed returns that can be liquidated easily and at low cost. On the liability side, the major component is the loss reserve and loss adjustment expenses ($658.8 billion) set aside to meet expected losses from underwriting and administrative expenses, constituting 32.3 percent of total liabilities and capital. Unearned premiums (a reserve set-aside containing the portion of a premium that has been paid before insurance coverage has been provided) are also a major liability, representing 12.7 percent of total liabilities and capital.

PC underwriting risk results when the premiums generated on a given insurance line are insufficient to cover (1) the claims (losses) incurred insuring against the peril and (2) the administrative expenses of providing that insurance (legal expenses, commissions, taxes, etc.), after taking into account (3) the investment income generated between the time premiums are received and the time claims are paid. Thus, underwriting risk may result from unexpected increases in loss rates, unexpected increases in expenses, and/or unexpected decreases in investment yields or returns.

3.3.1 Loss Risk

The key feature of claims loss exposure is the actuarial predictability of losses relative to premiums earned, which depends on a number of characteristics or features of the perils insured.

  • Property versus liability, maximum levels of losses are more predictable for property lines than for liability lines, for example the monetary value of the loss of an auto is relatively easy to calculate while the upper limit to losses in a product liability line, such as asbestos damage to workers' health, may be difficult if not impossible to estimate
  • Severity versus frequency, loss rates are more predictable on low severity, high frequency lines (for example fire, auto, homeowners) where the law of large numbers applies, than on high severity, low frequency lines (for example earthquake, hurricane, financial guaranty) where probabilities are not always stationary, individual risks are not independent, and the severity of the loss could be enormous. Under the 2002 federal terrorism insurance program, the government is responsible for 90 percent of insurance industry losses that arise from any future terrorist incidents that exceed a minimum amount, with the government's losses capped at $100 billion per year, and in 2017 each insurer had a maximum amount it would pay before federal aid kicked in, namely 18 percent of each company's commercial property-casualty premiums
  • Long tail versus short tail, some liability lines suffer from a long-tail risk exposure where the insured event occurs during a coverage period but a claim is not filed or reported until many years later, for example the Dalkon shield case and asbestos cases, with Halliburton in 2002 agreeing to pay $4 billion in cash and stock and to seek bankruptcy protection for a subsidiary (KBR) to settle more than 300,000 asbestos claims, and insurance companies agreeing to reimburse Halliburton $1.5 billion for the cost of asbestos settlements by the end of 2004
  • Product inflation versus social inflation, loss rates on all PC property policies are adversely affected by unexpected increases in inflation (for example the oil price shocks of 1973, 1978, and 2008), and liability lines may be subject to social inflation, reflected in juries' willingness to award punitive and other liability damages at rates far above the underlying rate of inflation, particularly prevalent in commercial liability and medical malpractice insurance

3.3.2 Reinsurance and Catastrophe Bonds

An alternative to managing risk on a PC insurer's balance sheet is to purchase reinsurance from a reinsurance company, which is essentially insurance for insurance companies, allowing primary insurers to protect against unforeseen or extraordinary losses, improve capital position, expand business, limit losses, and stabilize cash flows. About 75 percent of the reinsurance business that comes from U.S. insurance companies is written by non-U.S. reinsurers such as Munich Re and Swiss Re. Following Hurricane Andrew and the Northridge Earthquake in the mid-1990s, several insurers were forced into bankruptcy and reinsurance capacity dried up, so insurers and reinsurers started issuing catastrophe bonds, and the market has grown robustly since. Catastrophe bonds function just like a reinsurance contract structured over several years or a single year, where when the sponsor's property damage losses exceed a specified indemnity trigger (for example $2 million), the bond kicks in to absorb the financial impact up to a stated limit (say $3 million), and other catastrophe bond losses are pegged to parametric triggers like earthquake magnitude or wind speed. Catastrophe bonds also present value to investors because the yields generally are higher than traditional bonds and they offer portfolio diversification benefits, since catastrophic property damage risks do not correlate with the risks of other asset classes. A likely challenge to the catastrophe bond market is rising interest rates, which may reduce investor interest.

3.3.3 Measuring Loss Risk, Expense Risk, and Investment Yield Risk

The loss ratio measures the ratio of losses incurred to premiums earned (premiums received and earned on insurance contracts because time has passed with no claim being filed). A loss ratio less than 100 means that premiums earned were sufficient to cover losses incurred on that line. Aggregate industry loss ratios increased from the 60 percent range in the 1950s to the 70 and 80 percent range in the 1980s into the early 2010s, with a ratio of 79.4 in 2011, dropping to 67.4 in 2013 and staying below 70 through 2015, then rising above 70 percent over 2016 to 2020, peaking at 76.2 percent in 2017.

The two major sources of expense risk for PC insurers are (1) loss adjustment expenses (LAE), the costs surrounding the loss settlement process, for example adjusters who determine the liability of the insurer and the size of the settlement, and (2) commissions paid to insurance brokers and sales agents and other expenses related to the acquisition of business. In contrast to the increasing trend in the loss ratio, the expense ratio generally decreased over the period, partly because of the switch in distribution from independent brokers (the American agency method) to selling directly through the insurer's own brokers (the direct writer method). In 2020, expenses, namely commissions and other (non-LAE), amounted to 27.3 percent of premiums written.

The combined ratio is a common measure of the overall underwriting profitability of a line, technically equal to the loss ratio plus the ratios of LAE to premiums earned, commissions and other acquisition costs and general expense costs to premiums written, plus any dividends paid to policyholders as a proportion of premiums earned. If the combined ratio is less than 100, premiums alone are sufficient to cover both losses and expenses related to the line, as was the case from 2013 to 2015 (95.7, 96.5, and 97.2). If premiums are insufficient and the combined ratio exceeds 100, the PC insurer must rely on investment income earned on premiums for overall profitability, for example the combined ratio before dividends was 103.9 in 2017, driven by higher catastrophe losses and the underperforming auto market.

Example 6-2. Calculation of the Combined Ratio and Operating Ratio

Suppose an insurance company's projected loss ratio is 79.8 percent, its expense ratio is 27.9 percent, and it pays 2 percent of its premiums earned to policyholders as dividends. The combined ratio (after dividends) is equal to

\[ \text{Combined ratio} = \text{Loss ratio} + \text{Expense ratio} + \text{Dividend ratio} = 79.8 + 27.9 + 2.0 = 109.7 \]

Thus, expected losses on all PC lines, expenses, and dividends exceeded premiums earned by 9.7 percent. If the company's investment portfolio yielded 12 percent, the operating ratio and overall profitability of the PC insurer would be

\[ \text{Operating ratio} = \text{Combined ratio after dividends} - \text{Investment yield} = 109.7 - 12.0 = 97.7\% \]
\[ \text{Overall profitability} = 100 - \text{Operating ratio} = 100 - 97.7 = 2.3\% \]

The high investment returns (12 percent) make the PC insurer profitable overall. As another example, in 2012 PC insurers' investment yield was 10.5 percent, so the operating ratio was 92.7 (the combined ratio after dividends of 103.2 minus the investment yield of 10.5), but lower net returns on investments, for example 2.8 percent rather than 10.5 percent, would have meant underwriting PC insurance was marginally unprofitable with an operating ratio of 100.4. Thus, the effect of interest rates and default rates on PC insurers' investments is crucial to overall profitability. In 2017, bonds constituted 49.4 percent of total assets and 65.6 percent of financial assets of PC insurers, and the ratio of policyholder surplus to assets was 38.6 percent.

3.4 Recent Trends and the Underwriting Cycle

While catastrophes should be random, the period 1980 to 2021 was characterized by a number of catastrophes of historically high severity, and the industry experienced troughs of an underwriting cycle, or hard underwriting conditions. Most of the period 1985 to 2017 was not very profitable for the PC industry, with combined ratios of 116.2 in 1987, 115.7 in 1992, and 116.0 in 2001, due to a succession of catastrophes from Hurricane Hugo (1989), the San Francisco earthquake (1991), the Oakland fires (1991), and almost $20 billion in losses from Hurricane Andrew in Florida. In 1994, the Northridge earthquake caused estimated losses of $7 billion to $10 billion, and losses associated with El Niño drove the combined ratio back up to 105.6 in 1998, 107.9 in 1999 (with $3.4 billion paid out on asbestos claims, the largest payouts ever), and 110.5 in 2000.

The terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001 added a huge burden, with early estimates of the costs as high as $19 billion, and only 10 percent of the losses reported in 2001, yet the losses added an estimated 4 percentage points to the combined ratio after dividends of 116.0. The U.S. government proposed paying the majority of the losses, capping insurers' 2002 liabilities at $12 billion, 2003 liabilities at $23 billion, and 2004 liabilities at $36 billion. After several tumultuous years, 2003 saw profitability improve with a combined ratio after dividends of 100.1, the best results since 1979. In 2004, four major hurricanes (Charley, Frances, Ivan, and Jeanne) caused estimated losses of $25 billion, yet the industry saw its first overall profitable year since the 1960s with a combined ratio of 98.7. In 2005, $57.7 billion in catastrophe losses primarily from the record-breaking hurricane season, including Katrina, Wilma, and Rita, raised the combined ratio to 100.9 and prompted both Allstate and State Farm to stop writing new homeowner policies. In 2006 and 2007, a small level of catastrophic losses combined with strong performance in virtually all other major lines resulted in combined ratios of 92.4 (the best underwriting performance since 1936) and 95.6.

Losses rose significantly in 2008 through 2012 due to jumps in catastrophe losses, including $12.5 billion from Hurricane Ike, $18.8 billion from Hurricane Sandy, and $14.2 billion from the Midwest tornadoes, and losses in the mortgage and financial guarantee segments associated with the financial crisis, which experienced losses of 194.6 and 136.6 percent of premiums written respectively in 2012, down from 214.6 and 416.9 percent in 2008. These losses pushed the 2008 combined ratio to 105.1. In 2011, insured catastrophe losses totaled $25.0 billion, overall net income after taxes fell 46 percent to $19.2 billion, and the industry's combined ratio rose to 107.8, its highest level since 2001. Profitability in the PC industry surged to its highest level in the post crisis era in 2013 to 2015, with net written premiums of $546 billion in 2013 and a record $570 billion in 2014, and the combined ratio falling annually to 96.3, 97.0, and 97.6. However, the industry recorded two consecutive years of underwriting loss in 2016 and 2017, with 2017 insured losses more than triple the amount for 2016, including estimated losses of $19 billion for Hurricane Harvey, $25 billion for Irma, and $30 billion for Maria, resulting in a combined ratio of 103.9 in 2017. Despite the impacts of catastrophe losses and COVID-19 in 2020, the PC industry's combined ratio improved to 99.1, 98.7, and 98.7 in 2018, 2019, and 2020, primarily due to sustained premium growth, prior-year reserve releases, and lower loss costs in the auto market.

3.5 Regulation of PC Insurance

As with life insurance companies, PC insurers are chartered by states and regulated by state commissions, with state guaranty funds providing some protection to policyholders if an insurance company fails. The NAIC provides various services to state regulatory commissions, including a standardized examination system and the Insurance Regulatory Information System (IRIS) to identify insurers with loss, combined, and other ratios outside the normal ranges. An additional burden that PC insurers face in some activity lines, especially auto insurance and workers' compensation insurance, is rate regulation, where state commissioners set ceilings on premiums and premium increases based on specific cost of capital and line risk exposure formulas, leading some insurers to leave states such as New Jersey, Florida, and California, which have the most restrictive regulations. The industry also came under attack for the way it handled homeowners' claims associated with Hurricane Katrina, with insurers insisting the storm surge was classified as a flood and therefore excluded from coverage, but a January 2007 verdict held State Farm responsible for policy limits that totaled more than $220,000 on each loss deemed to be due to storm-surge flooding, as well as liable for punitive damages.

4. Global Issues

Like the other sectors of the financial institutions industry, the insurance sector is becoming increasingly global, with the United States, Japan, and western Europe dominating the global market. In 2020, the top countries by total premiums written were the United States ($2,530.6 billion, 40.3 percent global market share), China ($655.9 billion, 10.4 percent), Japan ($414.8 billion, 6.6 percent), the United Kingdom ($338.3 billion, 5.4 percent), Germany ($258.6 billion, 4.1 percent), and France ($231.3 billion, 3.7 percent). The world's largest insurance companies by 2020 net premiums written were UnitedHealth Group Inc. ($201.5 billion), Ping An Ins Co of China Ltd. ($118.8 billion), China Life Insurance Company ($111.2 billion), Centene Corporation ($107.4 billion), and Anthem Inc. ($105.7 billion).

Worldwide, 2011 was a bad year for life and PC insurers, with catastrophe losses the worst on record, including Japan's earthquake and tsunami (insured losses of $40 billion), earthquakes in New Zealand ($13 billion), floods in Thailand ($10 billion), and a series of severe tornadoes in the United States ($14.2 billion), all contributing to $350 billion in disaster losses. Worldwide insured losses in 2012 were 36 percent higher than the 10-year average ($81 billion versus $53 billion). Global insured losses fell to $45 billion in 2013 and $27 billion in 2015, the lowest catastrophe losses of any year since 2009. However, in 2017, insured losses worldwide were $144 billion, the highest ever recorded according to Swiss Re, up from $56 billion in 2016 and above the inflation-adjusted annual average of the previous 10 years ($58 billion), based on payouts related to three major hurricanes in the United States (Harvey, Irma, and Maria), wildfire outbreaks in California, and many thunderstorms, windstorms, and other severe weather events. According to Munich Re, natural disasters worldwide caused total losses of $280 billion in 2021, a dramatic increase from $210 billion in 2020 and $166 billion in 2019, with roughly $120 billion insured, and the USA accounting for around $145 billion of total losses, of which approximately $85 billion was insured.

5. Key Formulas and Calculations

5.1 Fair Value of an Annuity

\[ \text{Fair value} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \frac{CF}{(1+r)^t} = CF \times \text{PVIFA}_{r,n} \]

where CF is the annual payment, r the guaranteed interest rate, and n the expected number of years lived. For example, $15,000 per year for 25 years at 5 percent gives a fair value of $15,000 times 14.0939, or $211,409.

5.2 Loss Ratio

\[ \text{Loss ratio} = \frac{\text{Losses incurred}}{\text{Premiums earned}} \]

The loss ratio measures the actual losses incurred on a line, and a value less than 100 means premiums earned were sufficient to cover losses. The 2020 loss ratio was 70.2 and the 2011 ratio was 79.4.

5.3 Expense Ratio

\[ \text{Expense ratio} = \frac{\text{Expenses (commissions and other)}}{\text{Premiums written}} \]

In 2020, the expense ratio was 27.3 percent of premiums written, and its long-term trend has been decreasing due to the shift to the direct writer method.

5.4 Combined Ratio

\[ \text{Combined ratio} = \text{Loss ratio} + \text{Expense ratio} + \text{Dividend ratio} \]

If the combined ratio is less than 100, premiums alone are sufficient to cover losses and expenses. For example, 79.8 + 27.9 + 2.0 = 109.7, meaning losses, expenses, and dividends exceeded premiums earned by 9.7 percent.

5.5 Operating Ratio

\[ \text{Operating ratio} = \text{Combined ratio after dividends} - \text{Investment yield} \]
\[ \text{Overall profitability} = 100 - \text{Operating ratio} \]

For example, a combined ratio of 109.7 with an investment yield of 12 percent gives an operating ratio of 97.7 and overall profitability of 2.3 percent. In 2015, an investment yield of 9.5 percent produced an operating ratio of 88.1 (97.6 minus 9.5).

6. Summary and Conclusions

This chapter examined the activities and regulation of insurance companies. The first part described the various classes of life insurance and recent trends, including the five contractual forms of ordinary life, group life, industrial life, and credit life, as well as other activities such as annuities, private pension plans, and accident and health insurance. The second part covered property-casualty companies, with the various lines that make up PC insurance becoming increasingly blurred as multiple-activity line coverages are offered, and the three sources of underwriting risk, namely loss risk, expense risk, and investment yield risk, measured through the loss ratio, expense ratio, combined ratio, and operating ratio.

Both life and property-casualty insurance companies are regulated at the state rather than the federal level, based on the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, with additional oversight from the FIO and the Federal Reserve after the financial crisis, and both are protected by state guarantee funds that differ from federal deposit insurance. The financial crisis showed the systemic risk insurance companies can present, as in the case of AIG, while global trends show industry consolidation, increasing frequency and severity of catastrophes driving innovation such as catastrophe bonds, and both types of insurance companies coming under threat from other financial service firms offering similar or competitive products.
