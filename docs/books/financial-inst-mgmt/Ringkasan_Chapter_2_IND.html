1. Introduction to Depository Institutions

Depository institutions (DIs) are financial intermediaries that accept deposits from customers as a primary source of funding and use those funds to make loans and invest in securities. The three major groups of depository institutions are commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions. The defining characteristic of DIs is that a significant proportion of their funding comes from customer deposits, creating a joint-product structure on their balance sheets: they offer products on both sides—loans (assets) and deposits (liabilities). This joint-product nature creates special management challenges, particularly in dealing with interest rate risk, liquidity risk, and credit risk.

Simple Depository Institution Balance Sheet Identity
\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity} \]

Symbols: Assets include loans and other assets. Liabilities include deposits and other borrowed funds. Equity represents the owners' capital contribution plus retained earnings.

Interpretation: For a depository institution, the balance sheet identity reflects the fundamental intermediation function: the institution transforms deposits (liabilities) into loans (assets). The equity cushion absorbs losses and protects depositors.

Practical Use: This identity is used to assess the financial health, leverage, and risk exposure of a DI. Regulators monitor the ratio of equity to assets as a measure of solvency.

Assumption: The balance sheet is recorded at book value; market values may differ significantly, especially for loans and securities.

Example

In 2021, U.S. commercial banks had total assets of $22,195.2 billion, total liabilities of $19,982.8 billion, and total equity capital of $2,212.4 billion. The equity-to-assets ratio was 9.97%, indicating that 90.03% of assets were funded by debt (deposits and borrowings).

Analogy

A depository institution is like a reservoir: deposits flow in from many sources (rainfall/streams), and loans flow out to borrowers (irrigation channels). The reservoir must maintain enough water (liquidity) to meet withdrawal demands while also generating returns through controlled releases (loans). The equity is like the dam's structural integrity—if too many loans go bad (cracks form), the equity absorbs the damage before the reservoir collapses.

Key Insight

The joint-product nature of depository institutions—offering both deposits (liabilities) and loans (assets)—creates inherent maturity mismatch risk. Deposits are typically short-term and liquid, while loans are long-term and illiquid. This structural mismatch exposes DIs to interest rate risk, liquidity risk, and credit risk simultaneously.

Key Takeaways

  • Depository institutions (commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions) are defined by their reliance on customer deposits as a primary funding source.
  • The balance sheet identity (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) is the fundamental framework for analyzing DI financial health.
  • The joint-product nature of DIs creates unique risk management challenges.
  • Regulatory evolution (e.g., Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999, Volcker Rule) has shaped the structure and activities of DIs.

2. Commercial Banks

2.1 Industry Structure and Composition

Commercial banks are the largest group of depository institutions by asset size. They accept deposits (liabilities) and make loans (assets) with a broader range of loan types than savings institutions or credit unions, including consumer, commercial, and real estate loans. The U.S. banking system is unique in having both very large money center banks and a large number of small community banks, a structure largely resulting from historical legal restrictions on geographic diversification.

Key distinctions among bank types:

  • Community banks (assets under $1 billion): Specialize in retail or consumer banking, providing residential mortgages and consumer loans, accessing local deposit bases. They focus on relationship banking, using personal knowledge of customers' creditworthiness.
  • Regional or superregional banks ($1 billion to several hundred billion in assets): Engage in a complete array of wholesale commercial banking activities, including consumer, residential, commercial, and industrial lending (C&I loans) regionally and nationally.
  • Money center banks (e.g., Bank of New York Mellon, Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase): Characterized by heavy reliance on nondeposit or borrowed sources of funds, few retail branches, major participation in foreign currency markets, and headquarters in New York or Chicago.

The number of U.S. commercial banks declined from 14,416 in 1985 to 4,231 in 2021, driven by mergers, failures, and regulatory changes. The Riegle-Neal Act of 1994 eased interstate branching, and the Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999 allowed banks to enter investment banking and insurance. Despite the decline in number of banks, the number of branches increased from 43,293 in 1985 to 85,005 in 2009 before declining to 74,583 in 2020.

Example: Asset Concentration by Bank Size

In 1984, banks with assets under $1 billion held 36.6% of total industry assets. By 2021, this share had fallen to 4.7%. Conversely, banks with assets over $10 billion increased their share from 34.5% (1984) to 86.9% (2021). The number of banks in the largest category increased from 24 to 142 over the same period. This dramatic consolidation reflects the trend toward larger, more complex banking organizations.

Analogy

The banking industry structure is like a forest ecosystem: community banks are the undergrowth—numerous, locally adapted, and providing essential services to the immediate environment. Regional banks are the mid-sized trees, and money center banks are the dominant canopy trees that influence conditions across the entire forest. While the undergrowth has thinned, the largest trees have grown taller and spread their branches wider.

Key Insight

Asset size does not determine whether a bank is a money center bank. Bank of America ($2,427.5 billion in assets) is not a money center bank, while Bank of New York Mellon ($416.6 billion) is. The defining characteristics are location (New York or Chicago), heavy reliance on nondeposit/borrowed funds, and major participation in foreign currency markets.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial banks are the largest DI group, with a broader range of loan types than other DIs.
  • The U.S. banking system has a unique dual structure: many small community banks and a few very large money center/regional banks.
  • Industry consolidation has dramatically reduced the number of banks from 14,416 (1985) to 4,231 (2021).
  • Small banks focus on relationship banking; large banks rely on centralized decision making and computer models.

2.2 Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

The commercial bank balance sheet reveals the core risks and strategic focus of the institution. As of December 31, 2021, total assets for all U.S. commercial banks were $22,195.2 billion.

Assets:

  • Net loans and leases: $10,396.6 billion (46.84% of total assets)
  • Securities: $5,587.1 billion (25.17%)
  • Cash assets: $3,420.4 billion (15.41%)
  • Other assets: $2,791.1 billion (12.58%)

Loan composition: Real estate loans ($4,813.8 billion, 21.69% of total assets), C&I loans ($2,266.0 billion, 10.21%), loans to individuals ($1,744.7 billion, 7.86%), and other loans ($1,667.5 billion, 7.51%).

Liabilities:

  • Total deposits: $18,410.3 billion (82.95% of total liabilities and equity)
  • Borrowings: $591.5 billion (2.67%)
  • Other liabilities: $981.0 billion (4.42%)
  • Total equity capital: $2,212.4 billion (9.97%)

Deposit composition: Transaction accounts ($5,735.6 billion, 31.2% of deposits) including demand deposits and NOW accounts; nontransaction accounts ($11,162.8 billion, 60.6% of deposits) including money market deposit accounts (MMDAs), savings deposits, and time deposits (certificates of deposit).

Critical inference: The liability structure has a shorter maturity than the asset portfolio. Liquid deposits and interbank borrowings fund illiquid loans, creating maturity mismatch. This exposes banks to interest rate risk and liquidity risk. Additionally, because banks are highly leveraged (equity is only ~10% of assets), even a small number of loan defaults can wipe out equity, leaving the bank insolvent.

Return on Assets (ROA)
\[ \text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Assets}} \]

Symbols: Net Income is the bank's profit after all expenses, provisions, and taxes. Total Assets is the book value of all assets.

Interpretation: ROA measures the earnings generated per dollar of assets. It reflects the efficiency of asset utilization and overall profitability.

Practical Use: ROA is used to compare profitability across banks of different sizes. A higher ROA indicates more efficient use of assets.

Assumption: ROA uses book value of assets, which may differ from market value, especially for loans and securities.

Return on Equity (ROE)
\[ \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Equity}} \]

Symbols: Net Income is the bank's profit. Total Equity is the book value of common equity including retained earnings.

Interpretation: ROE measures the return to common stockholders per dollar of equity invested. It reflects the bank's ability to generate profits from shareholders' capital.

Practical Use: ROE is the key metric for shareholders. It can be decomposed via DuPont analysis as ROE = ROA × (Assets/Equity), where the equity multiplier captures financial leverage.

Assumption: ROE is sensitive to leverage. Banks with lower equity per dollar of assets (higher leverage) will have higher ROE for the same ROA.

Example: ROA and ROE by Bank Size (2021)

For banks with assets under $100 million: ROA = 1.00%, ROE = 7.62%. For banks with assets over $10 billion: ROA = 1.21%, ROE = 12.18%. The equity multiplier (Assets/Equity) is higher for large banks (implied by the ratio ROE/ROA = 12.18/1.21 ≈ 10.07) than for small banks (7.62/1.00 ≈ 7.62). Large banks operate with less equity per dollar of assets, which amplifies ROE but also increases financial risk.

Net Interest Margin (NIM)
\[ \text{NIM} = \frac{\text{Interest Income} - \text{Interest Expense}}{\text{Earning Assets}} \]

Symbols: Interest Income is revenue from loans, securities, and other earning assets. Interest Expense is the cost of deposits and borrowings. Earning Assets are assets that generate interest income (loans, securities, federal funds sold).

Interpretation: NIM measures the spread between what the bank earns on its interest-bearing assets and what it pays on its interest-bearing liabilities, relative to the size of earning assets.

Practical Use: NIM is a key indicator of profitability from core intermediation activities. A declining NIM may indicate increased competition or a flattening yield curve.

Assumption: NIM does not capture noninterest income (fees, trading income) or noninterest expenses (operating costs).

Key Insight

The maturity mismatch between short-term liabilities (deposits) and long-term assets (loans) is both a source of profit and a source of risk. Banks earn a positive spread by paying low rates on deposits and charging higher rates on loans. However, when short-term interest rates rise sharply, funding costs increase faster than loan yields, compressing NIM and potentially causing losses—as happened to savings institutions in 1979-1982.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial bank balance sheets are dominated by loans (46.8% of assets) and deposits (82.9% of liabilities and equity).
  • Credit risk is a major exposure because high leverage means even a small proportion of loan defaults can wipe out equity.
  • Maturity mismatch between short-term deposits and long-term loans creates interest rate risk and liquidity risk.
  • ROA, ROE, and NIM are the primary performance metrics used to evaluate bank profitability.

2.3 Off-Balance-Sheet Activities

Off-balance-sheet (OBS) activities are contingent commitments and guarantees that do not appear on the balance sheet under current accounting standards but generate fee income and expose the bank to risk. An OBS asset moves onto the asset side when a contingent event occurs; an OBS liability moves onto the liability side when a contingent event occurs.

Types of OBS activities:

  • Derivatives: Interest rate swaps, futures, forwards, options, foreign exchange contracts, credit derivatives.
  • Letters of credit: Financial standby letters of credit, performance standby letters of credit, commercial letters of credit.
  • Unused commitments: Commitments to lend in the future.

The notional value of OBS activities at U.S. commercial banks grew from $10,236.7 billion in 1992 to $228,467.4 billion in 2012 before declining to $188,189.6 billion in 2021. The decline after 2012 reflects the Volcker Rule (2014), which prohibits proprietary trading by depository institutions.

Critical caveat: The notional (face) value of OBS activities does not accurately reflect the risk to the bank. The potential gain or loss is based on the possible change in market value over the life of the contract, which is normally less than 3% of the notional value.

Example: OBS Activity Growth

In 1992, the notional value of interest rate swaps was $2,122.4 billion. By 2007, this had grown to $99,376.4 billion, and by 2021 it stood at $91,829.5 billion. The notional value of credit derivatives grew from $0 in 1992 to $15,862.8 billion in 2007 before declining to $3,539.1 billion in 2021 due to post-crisis regulation. The total OBS notional value was 8.5 times total on-balance-sheet assets in 2021.

Key Insight

Off-balance-sheet activities have both risk-reducing and risk-increasing attributes. When used appropriately, derivatives can hedge interest rate, credit, and foreign exchange risks. However, OBS activities can also add to overall insolvency exposure, as demonstrated by the financial crisis of 2007-2009, when losses on OBS mortgage-backed securities reached over $1 trillion worldwide.

Key Takeaways

  • OBS activities include derivatives, letters of credit, and unused loan commitments that generate fee income.
  • Banks have both earnings incentives and regulatory tax-avoidance incentives to move activities off the balance sheet.
  • The notional value of OBS activities far exceeds on-balance-sheet assets, but actual risk exposure is typically much smaller.
  • The Volcker Rule (2014) prohibits proprietary trading and has reduced OBS derivative activity.

2.4 Regulation

U.S. commercial banks may be subject to up to four separate regulators:

  • Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC): Insures deposits up to $250,000 to prevent contagious runs. Levies insurance premiums, manages the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF), and examines banks. In 2021, 4,231 commercial banks were FDIC-insured.
  • Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC): Charters and examines national banks. In 2021, 747 banks were nationally chartered (66% of total commercial bank assets).
  • Federal Reserve System (FRS): Regulates bank holding companies and state-chartered member banks (663 in 2021). Provides access to the discount window and federal funds wire transfer network.
  • State authorities: Regulate state-chartered banks (3,484 in 2021, 34% of assets).

The dual banking system allows banks to choose between national and state charters. Most large banks (e.g., Bank of America) choose national charters, though some (e.g., Morgan Guaranty) choose state charters.

Key regulatory milestones include the McFadden Act (1927, restricted interstate branching), Glass-Steagall Act (1933, separated commercial and investment banking), DIDMCA (1980, phased out deposit rate ceilings), FIRREA (1989, responded to thrift crisis), FDICIA (1991, introduced prompt corrective action and risk-based deposit insurance premiums), Riegle-Neal Act (1994, eased interstate branching), Financial Services Modernization Act (1999, repealed Glass-Steagall barriers), Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act (2010, created Financial Services Oversight Council, Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Volcker Rule), and the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act (2018, provided regulatory relief for community banks).

Deposit insurance and contagious runs: The FDIC's guarantee program deters depositor runs by assuring depositors that their claims are secure even if the DI is in trouble. Without deposit insurance, rumors of a bank's liquidity crisis can become a self-fulfilling prophecy, as happened with Bear Stearns in March 2008. Bear Stearns, an investment bank without federal deposit insurance, experienced a run that forced its sale to JPMorgan Chase. In contrast, during the financial crisis, investors deposited funds in FDIC-insured institutions as a safe haven.

Key Insight

Regulation is a double-edged sword: it protects depositors and prevents runs, but it can also create moral hazard. Deposit insurance reduces depositors' incentive to monitor bank risk-taking. The FDICIA (1991) addressed this by introducing risk-based deposit insurance premiums and prompt corrective action (PCA), requiring mandatory regulatory intervention when a bank's capital falls below specified thresholds.

Key Takeaways

  • U.S. commercial banks are regulated by up to four agencies: FDIC, OCC, Federal Reserve, and state authorities.
  • The dual banking system allows banks to choose between national and state charters.
  • Deposit insurance deters contagious runs but creates moral hazard.
  • Key legislation has evolved from restricting geographic expansion (McFadden Act) to allowing nationwide branching (Riegle-Neal Act) and full-service financial institutions (FSMA 1999).
  • Post-crisis regulation (2010 Act) introduced systemic risk oversight, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and the Volcker Rule.

2.5 Industry Performance

Commercial bank performance has varied significantly over the past three decades, reflecting macroeconomic conditions, regulatory changes, and industry dynamics.

1990s: Strong performance driven by economic expansion, falling interest rates, and improved risk management. ROA rose from 0.49% (1990) to 1.31% (1999). ROE rose from 7.64% to 15.31% over the same period.

Early 2000s: A brief downturn in 2000-2001 due to economic recession was followed by record performance. The Federal Reserve cut interest rates 13 times, making debt cheaper and fueling the housing boom. ROA reached an all-time high of 1.40% in 2003, and ROE reached 15.34%.

2007-2009 financial crisis: Performance deteriorated sharply. Net income fell from $105.5 billion (2007) to $10.2 billion (2008). ROA fell to 0.13% (2008) and -0.10% (2009). ROE fell to 1.33% (2008) and -0.93% (2009). Twenty banks failed in 2008, 120 in 2009. Losses on mortgage-backed securities and OBS activities exceeded $1 trillion worldwide.

2010-2021 recovery: Gradual improvement following the crisis. ROA recovered to 1.24% and ROE to 12.27% by 2021. The COVID-19 pandemic caused a temporary dip in Q1-Q2 2020 (ROA fell to 0.38%, ROE to 3.50%), but recovery was swift due to government assistance programs, record deposit inflows, and declining loan loss provisions.

Example: Provision for Loan Losses During the Crisis

In 2006, provision for loan losses to total assets was 0.26%. By 2008, it had risen to 1.30%, and in 2009 it peaked at 1.94%. Net charge-offs to loans rose from 0.41% (2006) to 2.60% (2009). These figures reflect the dramatic deterioration in credit quality during the recession. By 2021, provision for loan losses was negative (-0.15%) for the first time ever, as charge-offs declined and delinquency rates fell.

Key Insight

The relationship between bank size and profitability has reversed over time. In the 1990s, small banks had higher ROA than large banks due to less competition in localized markets. By 2006, large banks (ROA = 1.35%) outperformed the smallest banks (0.95%). Large banks now generate more noninterest income from OBS activities and benefit from economies of scale in technology investments.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial bank performance is cyclical and closely tied to macroeconomic conditions.
  • The 2007-2009 financial crisis was the most severe since the Great Depression, with ROA and ROE turning negative.
  • Post-crisis recovery has been gradual, with capital levels significantly strengthened through stress tests and regulatory requirements.
  • Technology investment and competition from fintech firms are reshaping the competitive landscape.

3. Savings Institutions

3.1 Industry Structure

Savings institutions (historically called savings and loan associations, or S&Ls) were created in the early 1800s to serve the needs of individuals purchasing homes, filling a gap left by commercial banks that focused on business enterprises. They comprise two groups: savings associations (SAs) and savings banks (SBs). Savings associations concentrate more on residential mortgages; savings banks are more diversified, holding some commercial loans, corporate bonds, and stock.

The industry experienced a severe crisis in the 1980s due to:

  • Interest rate risk: The Federal Reserve's restrictive monetary policy (October 1979-October 1982) drove short-term rates as high as 16%. Savings institutions held long-term fixed-rate mortgages funded by short-term deposits, creating negative net interest margins.
  • Disintermediation: Regulation Q ceilings limited the rates savings associations could pay on deposits, causing depositors to move funds to money market mutual funds.
  • Moral hazard: The DIDMCA (1980) and DIA (1982) expanded savings institutions' asset powers, allowing riskier investments. The FSLIC (the insurer) lacked funds to close insolvent institutions and maintained flat insurance premiums regardless of risk, encouraging risk-taking.
  • Credit risk: Real estate and land prices collapsed in Texas, the Southwest, the Northeast, and Western states in the mid-1980s, causing widespread mortgage defaults.

The number of savings institutions declined from 3,677 (1989) to 608 (2021). The FIRREA (1989) abolished the FSLIC, created the Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC) to close insolvent institutions, strengthened capital requirements, and imposed the Qualified Thrift Lender (QTL) test requiring 65% of assets to be mortgage-related.

Key Insight

The savings institution crisis of the 1980s is a textbook example of how deregulation, moral hazard, and inadequate supervision can combine to create a systemic crisis. The interaction of interest rate spikes, expanded asset powers, flat-rate deposit insurance, and regulatory forbearance led to catastrophic losses. The crisis cost U.S. taxpayers an estimated $200 billion and led to the closure of more than 700 institutions by the RTC.

Key Takeaways

  • Savings institutions were created to serve residential mortgage lending needs.
  • The 1980s crisis was caused by interest rate spikes, deregulation, moral hazard, and real estate price collapses.
  • The number of savings institutions declined from 3,677 (1989) to 608 (2021).
  • The QTL test requires 65% of assets to be mortgage-related, reinforcing the focus on mortgage lending.

3.2 Balance Sheet and Regulation

As of December 31, 2021, savings institutions had total assets of $1,524.1 billion. Key balance sheet features:

Assets:

  • Real estate loans: $444.6 billion (29.17% of total assets), compared to 21.69% for commercial banks.
  • Securities: $658.8 billion (43.23%), reflecting higher liquidity needs.
  • Cash: $138.7 billion (9.10%).
  • Net loans: $672.1 billion (44.10%).

Liabilities:

  • Total deposits: $1,291.3 billion (84.73% of total liabilities and equity).
  • Borrowings: $63.7 billion (4.18%), primarily from Federal Home Loan Banks (FHLBs).
  • Equity capital: $147.2 billion (9.66%).

Regulation: The Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS) was consolidated into the OCC on July 21, 2011, as mandated by the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act. The FDIC manages the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF), which merged the Bank Insurance Fund and the Savings Association Insurance Fund in 2007. State-chartered savings institutions are regulated by state agencies.

Key Insight

Savings institutions hold a higher proportion of real estate loans (29.17%) and securities (43.23%) than commercial banks, reflecting their traditional focus on mortgage lending and their need for liquidity to manage the inherent interest rate risk of long-term mortgages. The higher securities holdings provide a buffer against interest rate shocks but also expose the institutions to reinvestment risk and market risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Savings institutions have a higher proportion of real estate loans and securities than commercial banks.
  • Deposits remain the dominant funding source (84.73% of total liabilities and equity).
  • Regulation of savings institutions was consolidated under the OCC in 2011.
  • Equity capital (9.66%) is slightly lower than for commercial banks (9.97%).

3.3 Industry Performance

Savings institution performance has followed patterns similar to commercial banks but with greater volatility:

  • 1990s: Record profits in the mid- to late-1990s. ROA reached 1.00% in 1999, ROE reached 11.73%.
  • Early 2000s: ROA and ROE increased to record levels (1.28% and 13.66% in 2003).
  • Mid-2000s: A flat yield curve and increased funding costs compressed margins. ROA declined to 0.99% (2006), ROE to 8.68%.
  • 2007-2009 crisis: Net income turned negative for the first time since 1991. ROA was -0.72% in 2008. Washington Mutual, the largest savings institution (over $300 billion in assets), failed.
  • 2010-2021 recovery: Gradual improvement. ROA reached 1.11% and ROE 11.29% by 2021.

Example: Asset Concentration in Savings Institutions

In 1992, 8 savings institutions with over $10 billion in assets held 17.8% of industry assets. By 2021, 18 institutions in this category held 67.3% of assets. The number of institutions with under $100 million declined from 1,109 (46.4% of total) to 105 (17.3% of total), but their share of industry assets fell from 5.4% to 0.4%. This dramatic consolidation reflects the closure of weak institutions and the growth of larger, more diversified savings banks.

Key Takeaways

  • Savings institution performance is more volatile than commercial banks due to greater concentration in mortgage lending.
  • The 2008 crisis was severe for savings institutions, with ROA turning negative (-0.72%).
  • Industry consolidation has been dramatic, with the largest institutions (over $10 billion) now controlling 67.3% of industry assets.

4. Credit Unions

4.1 Industry Structure

Credit unions (CUs) are nonprofit depository institutions mutually organized and owned by their members (depositors). They were first established in the United States in the early 1900s as self-help organizations to alleviate poverty. Members are required to have a common bond of occupation, association, or community. Unlike commercial banks and savings institutions, CUs are prohibited from serving the general public.

Key characteristics:

  • Nonprofit status: Net income is not taxed. Earnings are used to pay higher rates on member deposits, charge lower rates on member loans, or attract new members.
  • Mutual ownership: Members are legally the owners. CUs do not issue common stock.
  • Common bond requirement: Members must share a common occupation, association, or geographic community.
  • Tax-exempt status: CUs are not subject to the Community Reinvestment Act and pay no federal income tax, allowing them to offer more favorable rates than banks.

In 2021, there were 4,942 credit unions with total assets of $2,060.4 billion and 129.6 million members. The average CU size was $416.9 million, compared to $5,245.9 million for commercial banks. The CU system has three tiers: U.S. Central Credit Union (national), corporate credit unions (state/regional), and local credit unions.

Key Insight

The tax-exempt status of credit unions gives them a significant cost advantage over commercial banks and savings institutions. As shown in Figure 2-7, credit unions consistently offer lower rates on new car loans and higher rates on 1-year CDs than banks. This has led to ongoing controversy, with banks arguing that CUs have an unfair competitive advantage.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit unions are nonprofit, member-owned, tax-exempt depository institutions with a common bond requirement.
  • There were 4,942 CUs with 129.6 million members and $2,060.4 billion in assets in 2021.
  • Tax exemption allows CUs to offer more favorable rates than banks.
  • The common bond requirement limits the customer base but also reduces credit risk.

4.2 Balance Sheet and Performance

Assets (December 2021): Total loans: $1,255.3 billion (60.9% of total assets). Home mortgages: $551.1 billion (26.7%), auto loans: $404.5 billion (19.6%), credit cards: $64.2 billion (3.1%), commercial loans: $111.7 billion (5.4%). Investment securities: $452.6 billion (22.0%). Cash: $259.9 billion (12.6%).

Liabilities: Total deposits: $1,848.8 billion (89.7% of total liabilities and equity). Net worth: $211.6 billion (10.27%).

Deposit composition: Regular share accounts (35.5%), money market accounts (22.0%), share draft accounts (19.9%), share certificate accounts/CDs (13.4%).

Performance: ROA was 1.07% in 2021. Larger CUs significantly outperform smaller ones: the largest CUs (over $1 billion) had ROA of 1.20%, while the smallest (under $10 million) had ROA of 0.18%. Smaller CUs have a smaller customer base for quality loans and higher overhead expenses per dollar of assets.

During the 2008 crisis, CUs performed better than banks and savings institutions. While commercial banks' ROA fell to 0.13% and savings institutions' to -0.72%, CUs maintained an ROA of 0.31%. This resilience was due to their traditional focus on small consumer loans and government securities, and their limited exposure to subprime mortgages and mortgage-backed securities.

Example: Corporate Credit Union Crisis

While local credit unions survived the 2008 crisis relatively well, corporate credit unions (which invest excess funds from member CUs) did not. Some corporate CUs invested in higher-risk mortgage-backed securities. By November 2008, they reported $18 billion in unrealized losses. Local CUs withdrew deposits from corporate CUs (deposits fell from $44.7 billion to $22.9 billion between March and September 2008). The NCUA placed U.S. Central and WesCorp into conservatorship in March 2009, ultimately declaring five of the largest corporate CUs insolvent. The NCUA purchased $50 billion in toxic assets.

Key Insight

Because credit unions are mutual organizations, maximizing profit (ROA) is not the primary goal. The primary objective is serving the deposit and lending needs of members. As long as capital levels are sufficient to protect against unexpected losses, a CU is meeting its mission. This contrasts sharply with the profit orientation of stockholder-owned banks and savings institutions.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit union assets are dominated by consumer loans (auto loans, credit cards) and home mortgages.
  • CUs were more resilient during the 2008 crisis than banks or savings institutions.
  • Corporate credit unions, however, suffered severe losses from mortgage-backed securities investments.
  • Larger CUs significantly outperform smaller CUs in terms of ROA.
  • Profit maximization is not the primary goal; member service is paramount.

4.3 Regulation

Credit unions can be federally or state chartered. As of 2021, 62.7% of the 4,942 CUs were federally chartered, accounting for 52.6% of total membership and 50.5% of total assets. The National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) regulates federally chartered CUs and administers the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF), which provides deposit insurance guarantees up to $250,000 (covering 98% of all credit union deposits). State-chartered CUs are regulated by state agencies.

Key Takeaways

  • Credit unions are regulated by the NCUA (federal) or state agencies.
  • Deposit insurance is provided by the NCUSIF up to $250,000.
  • 62.7% of CUs are federally chartered.

5. Global Financial Performance

The financial crisis of 2007-2009 spread rapidly from the United States to the global banking system. Banks worldwide suffered losses on structured finance products and securitized subprime mortgage exposures, magnified by illiquidity in those markets.

European banks: Net income fell sharply in 2008. The largest banks in the Netherlands, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom had net losses. Irish, Spanish, and U.K. banks were especially hard hit due to large investments in mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. French and Italian banks were less affected due to their focus on domestic retail banking. Several European banks averted bankruptcy through direct central bank and government support.

European sovereign debt crisis (2010-2012): Greece's debt crisis spread to Portugal, Spain, and Italy. Greek banks experienced a run, with deposit withdrawals reaching €750 million per day (0.5% of the €170 billion deposit base). The crisis threatened the world's financial system, with U.S. banks having sovereign risk exposure to Greece ($43.1 billion), Ireland ($113.9 billion), Portugal ($47.1 billion), and Spain ($187.5 billion). Worldwide exposure to these four countries totaled $2,512.3 billion.

Brexit (2016-2020): The United Kingdom voted to leave the EU on June 23, 2016. The U.K. formally left on January 31, 2020, with a transition period ending December 31, 2020. Brexit has damaged U.K. economic growth, with growth slowing from 2.4% (2015) to 1.6% (2019). Business investment fell by 11% between 2016 and 2019. International banks shifted operations: Barclay's moved 5,000 clients to its Irish subsidiary; Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase, and Morgan Stanley switched 10% of their clients; Bank of America transferred 100 bankers to Dublin and 400 to Paris.

Key Insight

The 2008 financial crisis demonstrated that banking systems are globally interconnected. Losses from U.S. subprime mortgages cascaded through international financial markets, and the Greek sovereign debt crisis (2010-2012) showed that a default by a small country could threaten the entire global financial system. The crisis led to unprecedented government interventions, including bailouts, deposit guarantees, and stimulus packages across Europe, Asia, and North America.

Key Takeaways

  • The financial crisis spread globally, causing severe losses in European banks.
  • The Greek sovereign debt crisis (2010-2012) threatened the stability of the entire eurozone.
  • Brexit has damaged U.K. economic growth and led to a shift of banking operations from London to other EU financial centers.
  • Global financial systems remain highly interconnected, with systemic risk requiring coordinated international regulatory responses.

6. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

1. Confusing association with causality: A high ROA or ROE does not necessarily indicate good management. It may result from high leverage, risk-taking, or favorable macroeconomic conditions. Similarly, a low ROA may reflect conservative risk management rather than poor performance.

2. Ignoring the difference between book value and market value: Balance sheet values are recorded at book value. For loans and securities, market values may differ significantly, especially during periods of interest rate volatility or credit stress. A bank that appears well-capitalized on a book value basis may be insolvent on a market value basis.

3. Misinterpreting the equity multiplier: ROE = ROA × (Assets/Equity). A high ROE achieved through high leverage (low equity) is risky because it amplifies losses as well as gains. The equity multiplier must be evaluated in the context of capital adequacy requirements.

4. Confusing notional value with risk exposure: The notional value of OBS derivatives vastly overstates actual risk. The potential gain or loss is based on changes in market value (typically less than 3% of notional value), not the notional amount itself.

5. Overlooking the impact of off-balance-sheet activities: OBS activities can create significant risk, as demonstrated by the financial crisis. Credit derivatives, mortgage-backed securities, and loan commitments can all generate losses that are not immediately visible on the balance sheet.

6. Assuming that deposit insurance eliminates all risk: Deposit insurance protects depositors, not the institution itself. Banks can still fail, and shareholders can lose their entire investment. The FDIC's prompt corrective action (PCA) framework requires closure when capital falls below 2% of assets.

7. Ignoring the common bond requirement for credit unions: Credit unions cannot serve the general public. The common bond requirement limits the customer base and affects diversification. This is a fundamental constraint on CU growth and risk management.

8. Confusing the QTL test with safety: The Qualified Thrift Lender test requires 65% of assets to be mortgage-related, which actually increases concentration risk in real estate. A savings institution that passes the QTL test may be more, not less, risky than a diversified commercial bank.

9. Misinterpreting the spread: The spread between lending and deposit rates is not the same as the net interest margin. NIM is calculated as (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Earning Assets, which accounts for the fact that not all assets are earning assets and not all liabilities are interest-bearing.

10. Forgetting that "too big to fail" is not a guarantee: The FDICIA (1991) limited the use of too-big-to-fail bailouts, and the 2010 Wall Street Reform Act gave the government power to break up FIs that pose systemic risk. However, the 2008 crisis showed that large institutions may still receive government support in extreme circumstances.

Key Takeaways

  • Do not confuse association with causality when interpreting financial ratios.
  • Book values and market values can differ significantly for loans and securities.
  • High ROE achieved through high leverage is risky and must be evaluated in the context of capital adequacy.
  • Notional values of OBS derivatives overstate actual risk exposure.
  • Deposit insurance protects depositors, not institutions or shareholders.
  • The QTL test may increase rather than decrease risk by forcing concentration in mortgage lending.
