1. Introduction: The Role and Economic Function of Finance Companies

The primary financial service provided by finance companies is lending. Like commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions, finance companies extend loans to both individuals and corporations. Their lending activities span three broad categories: consumer lending, business lending, and real estate lending. Some of these loans closely resemble those offered by depository institutions—such as consumer and auto loans—while others are substantially more specialized and tailored to particular market niches.

The critical structural distinction between finance companies and depository institutions is that finance companies do not accept deposits. Instead, they rely on short-term and long-term debt as their principal source of funds. This single difference has far-reaching consequences for their funding costs, regulatory oversight, risk profile, and competitive behavior. A second important distinction is that finance companies frequently lend to customers whom depository institutions consider too risky to serve. This willingness to serve higher-risk borrowers enables finance companies to earn higher interest income, but it also exposes them to elevated default risk, which can produce losses and even failure when the high-risk strategy does not pay off.

Finance company: a non-depository financial intermediary whose primary function is to make loans to individuals and businesses using funds obtained primarily from short- and long-term debt issuance rather than from customer deposits.

Depository institution: a financial institution (such as a commercial bank, savings institution, or credit union) that accepts deposits from customers and makes loans. Depository institutions typically have access to deposit insurance and regulatory safety nets.

Installment credit: a loan repaid to the lender through periodic payments (installments) consisting of varying amounts of interest and principal. Common examples include auto loans, home mortgages, and student loans.

The historical origin of the finance company industry is instructive. The first major finance company was created during the Great Depression when General Electric Corp. established General Electric Capital Corp. (GECC, now known as GE Capital Global Holdings or GECGH) as a vehicle to finance appliance sales to cash-constrained customers who could not obtain installment credit from banks. This origin illustrates the fundamental economic function of finance companies: they extend credit to market segments underserved by traditional depository institutions. By the late 1950s, banks became more willing to make installment loans, and finance companies expanded beyond their parent companies' product lines to pursue broader lending opportunities.

Example

GE Capital, at its peak in 2008, contributed nearly 60 percent of General Electric's overall profits and held more than $600 billion in assets ranging from commercial real estate to auto loans in Thailand. Following the financial crisis, GE announced the GE Capital Exit Plan in April 2015 to sell most of its financial services assets. By 2018, GE Capital's assets had fallen to $119 billion, down from over $600 billion in 2008. The eventual disposition of GE Capital demonstrates both the profit potential and the systemic vulnerability of large captive finance companies.

Analogy

A finance company resembles a specialty lender that operates like a department store's in-house financing desk but on a massive scale. Just as a furniture store might finance a customer's purchase directly when a bank would reject the customer's application, finance companies step in to provide credit where traditional banks are unwilling or unable to lend. Unlike banks, which obtain funds from depositors' savings accounts, finance companies borrow money from capital markets—much like a business taking out a corporate loan—and then relend those funds to borrowers at higher rates.

Key Insight

Finance companies occupy a distinct position in the financial system as non-depository lenders that compete directly with banks while serving riskier borrower segments. Their comparative advantage lies in regulatory flexibility and specialized lending expertise; their comparative disadvantage lies in their reliance on debt markets for funding and their lack of access to deposit insurance and the Federal Reserve's discount window. The absence of deposit funding removes the traditional safety net but also removes much of the regulatory burden imposed on depositories.

Key Takeaways

  • Finance companies are non-depository lenders whose primary function is making consumer, business, and real estate loans.
  • They differ from banks by not accepting deposits and by relying on short- and long-term debt for funding.
  • Finance companies frequently lend to borrowers that depository institutions consider too risky, earning higher interest rates in exchange for higher default risk.
  • The industry originated during the Depression with GE Capital, which financed appliance sales to customers who could not obtain bank installment credit.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

The finance company industry has experienced dramatic growth over nearly half a century. In the second quarter of 2021, total industry assets stood at $1,475.5 billion. Comparing this figure to $39.6 billion at the beginning of 1977, the industry experienced growth of almost 3,627 percent over 44 years. This extraordinary expansion was driven by attractive rates on certain loans (such as new car loans), a willingness to lend to riskier borrowers than depository institutions serve, direct affiliation with manufacturing firms through captive subsidiaries, and a relatively limited regulatory burden. However, total assets were still 33 percent below the industry's peak at the beginning of 2008, reflecting the substantial contraction during and after the financial crisis.

Industry Growth Rate (Compounded Over 44 Years)
\[ \text{Growth Rate} = \frac{\text{Assets}_{2021\text{Q}2} - \text{Assets}_{1977}}{\text{Assets}_{1977}} \times 100\% \]

Where $\text{Assets}_{2021\text{Q}2} = \$1{,}475.5$ billion and $\text{Assets}_{1977} = \$39.6$ billion. Substituting:

\[ \frac{1{,}475.5 - 39.6}{39.6} \times 100\% = \frac{1{,}435.9}{39.6} \times 100\% \approx 3{,}627\% \]

This is a measure of the total percentage increase in industry assets over the period. It is used to gauge the industry's long-run expansion. The growth calculation does not imply that growth occurred smoothly each year; the time series in Figure 3–1 shows cyclical fluctuations, including substantial declines after 2008. The industry's pronounced growth should be interpreted with the caveat that it partly reflects the transfer of lending activity from banks to finance companies and the expansion of consumer credit in the U.S. economy.

The industry is highly concentrated. Firms with assets of $20 billion or more—representing less than 0.5 percent of all finance companies—account for 71 percent of industry assets. Many of the largest finance companies are wholly owned or captive subsidiaries of major manufacturing companies. A captive finance company is a finance company wholly owned by a parent corporation, and its major role is to provide financing for the purchase of products manufactured by the parent. For example, Ford Motor Credit Corp. finances purchases of Ford vehicles. The parent company is often a major source of debt finance for the captive subsidiary. A benefit of the captive structure to the parent is diversification of revenue streams: when the auto industry suffered from weak sales in the mid-2000s, Ford Motor Credit produced record profits, contributing as much as 80 percent of Ford Motor Corporation's overall profits.

Analogy

The concentration of the finance company industry is similar to the retail industry, where a handful of large national chains dominate total sales while thousands of small independent stores serve local niches. The captive finance company relationship is comparable to an automobile manufacturer owning its own dealership financing arm—the financing arm exists primarily to help sell the manufacturer's products while also generating independent profits that can cushion the parent company during downturns in its core business.

Key Insight

The finance company industry is dominated by captive subsidiaries of nonfinancial corporations. This ownership structure aligns the finance company's lending objective with the parent's commercial objective of selling manufactured goods, creating a powerful distribution channel for credit that is fundamentally different from the standalone banking model. The diversification benefit to the parent is substantial: captive finance subsidiaries can produce countercyclical profits that offset weakness in the parent's manufacturing operations.

Key Takeaways

  • Finance company assets grew from $39.6 billion (1977) to $1,475.5 billion (2021 Q2), an increase of roughly 3,627 percent.
  • Industry assets remained 33 percent below the pre-crisis 2008 peak, reflecting the lasting impact of the financial crisis.
  • Large finance companies with assets of $20 billion or more represent less than 0.5 percent of firms yet account for 71 percent of industry assets.
  • Most of the largest finance companies are captive subsidiaries of major manufacturers, such as Ford Motor Credit, Toyota Motor Credit, and John Deere Capital.
  • Access to FDIC programs and bank holding company status has reshaped the competitive landscape for former finance companies such as GMAC/Ally and CIT Group.

3. Types of Finance Companies and Their Market Segments

The finance company industry is composed of three major types of institutions, each serving a distinct market segment: sales finance institutions, personal credit institutions, and business credit institutions.

Sales finance institutions specialize in making loans to the customers of a particular retailer or manufacturer. Examples include Ford Motor Credit and Sears Roebuck Acceptance Corp. Because sales finance institutions can frequently process loans faster and more conveniently than depository institutions—generally at the location of purchase—this sector competes directly with depository institutions for consumer loans. The key feature of the sales finance model is the integration of credit extension with the point-of-sale transaction, which reduces transaction costs and increases convenience for the borrower.

Personal credit institutions specialize in making installment and other loans to consumers. Examples include HSBC Finance and AIG American General. These institutions make loans to customers that depository institutions find too risky to serve, either because of low income or a bad credit history. To compensate for the additional risk, personal credit institutions charge higher interest rates than depository institutions and/or accept collateral (such as used cars) that banks find unacceptable. The higher rates are not a reflection of exploitation per se but a risk premium required to cover expected losses and still earn a profit on loans to subprime borrowers.

Business credit institutions provide financing to corporations, especially through equipment leasing and factoring. Examples include CIT Group and U.S. Bancorp Equipment Finance. Factoring is the process of purchasing accounts receivable from corporate customers at a discount from their face value; the finance company specializes in and assumes responsibility for collecting the receivables. The corporate customer receives cash for sales faster than the time it takes customers to pay their bills and no longer bears the risk that receivables may be delayed or defaulted upon. Many finance companies perform more than one of these three services—Ally Financial is an example of a multi-line finance company.

Example

Ford Motor Credit Corp. (FMCC) operates as a sales finance institution that provides wholesale financing to Ford dealers for inventory floor plans. In this arrangement, FMCC pays for Ford dealers' auto inventory received from Ford, places a lien on each car on the showroom floor, and charges the dealer periodic interest on the floor plan loan. The dealer does not pay for the car until it is sold. This example illustrates the operational mechanics of sales finance institutions: they are deeply embedded in the distribution chain of the manufacturer, providing financing not only to consumers (retail) but also to dealers (wholesale).

Analogy

Think of the three types of finance companies as three different kinds of stores. Sales finance institutions are like boutique stores attached to a specific brand—they sell credit only for products made by their parent. Personal credit institutions are like general-purpose pawn shops or second-hand stores—they lend to consumers whom mainstream banks reject, charging more for the privilege. Business credit institutions are like wholesale suppliers—they provide financing to businesses through equipment leasing and by buying their unpaid invoices at a discount (factoring), much as a wholesaler might purchase a retailer's inventory at a discount to assume responsibility for selling it.

Key Insight

The three-way taxonomy of finance companies reflects fundamental differences in borrower segments and collateral types. Sales finance institutions are tied to product distribution, personal credit institutions serve subprime consumers, and business credit institutions serve corporations. Understanding which type of finance company one is analyzing is essential because their risk profiles, funding needs, and regulatory exposure differ substantially.

Key Takeaways

  • Sales finance institutions make loans to customers of a specific retailer or manufacturer, competing directly with banks at the point of sale.
  • Personal credit institutions specialize in installment and other consumer loans, serving high-risk borrowers that banks reject and charging higher rates accordingly.
  • Business credit institutions finance corporations through equipment leasing and factoring; factoring means purchasing accounts receivable at a discount.
  • Factoring transfers the responsibility and risk of collecting accounts receivable to the finance company, providing immediate cash to the corporate customer.
  • Many finance companies, such as Ally Financial, operate across more than one of these categories.

Several structural patterns stand out. Third, total loans and leases as a share of total assets fell from 93.5 percent to 70.3 percent, indicating that finance companies now hold a larger fraction of non-loan assets (including possibly cash and securities) than before the crisis.

Composition Share
\[ \text{Share of Total Assets} = \frac{\text{Asset Category Value}}{\text{Total Assets}} \times 100\% \]

Where the asset category value is the dollar amount of a particular loan category (e.g., consumer motor vehicle loans = $370.6 billion in 2021) and total assets is the industry aggregate ($1,682.2 billion in 2021). The ratio expresses the relative importance of each loan category in the industry's asset portfolio. This measure is used to track portfolio composition over time and to assess the industry's exposure to different credit markets. For example, the sharp decline in real estate loans from 25.9 percent to 6.2 percent of assets between 2007 and 2021 reveals a strategic withdrawal from mortgage lending following the financial crisis.

Example

Between 2007 and 2021, consumer motor vehicle loans and leases grew from $384.4 billion combined (261.5 + 122.9) to $546.9 billion combined (370.6 + 176.3), while their share of total assets rose from 17.4 percent to 32.5 percent. Meanwhile, one-to-four-family real estate loans plummeted from $472.7 billion to $73.6 billion. The strategic implication is that finance companies refocused their business models toward auto finance and away from residential mortgage lending after the crisis.

Analogy

The balance sheet of a finance company is like an investor's portfolio. Just as an investor might shift from real estate holdings into automotive stocks in response to changing market conditions, finance companies reshuffled their loan portfolios away from real estate and toward vehicle financing after the financial crisis exposed the risks of mortgage lending. The percentages reveal not just where money is deployed but also management's assessment of which markets offer acceptable risk-adjusted returns.

Key Insight

The balance sheet data reveal that finance companies are primarily vehicle financiers and equipment lenders, not general-purpose mortgage lenders. The dramatic reduction in real estate lending after 2007, combined with the sustained growth in consumer vehicle loans and business retail loans, demonstrates how crisis-driven losses reshaped the industry's strategic focus. The rising share of non-loan assets on the balance sheet suggests finance companies diversified their asset holdings beyond traditional lending.

Key Takeaways

  • Consumer loans (44.8 percent), business loans (19.2 percent), and real estate loans (6.2 percent) constitute the major asset categories in 2021.
  • Consumer vehicle loans and leases are the largest single loan category, representing about 32.5 percent of total assets.
  • Real estate loans contracted dramatically from 25.9 percent (2007) to 6.2 percent (2021) of total assets following the financial crisis.
  • Total loans and leases fell from 93.5 percent to 70.3 percent of total assets, indicating greater diversification into non-loan assets.
  • The only consistently growing loan categories since 2007 are consumer vehicle loans and business retail loans.

5. Consumer Lending and Subprime Credit Markets

Consumer lending is the largest lending activity of finance companies. Consumer loans comprise motor vehicle loans and leases and other consumer loans, including revolving credit and securitized loans. Motor vehicle loans and leases are traditionally the major type of consumer loan, representing 32.5 percent of total assets in 2021. Other consumer loans (revolving and other) constituted 27.5 percent of the consumer loan portfolio in 2021 and consist of personal cash loans, mobile home loans, and private-label credit card loans (e.g., Discover card) used to purchase consumer goods such as appliances, apparel, general merchandise, and recreational vehicles.

Table 3–3 shows consumer credit interest rates for new car loans at commercial banks versus auto finance companies from 1995 to 2021. In the 1990s, finance companies historically charged higher rates than commercial banks—1.62 percent higher in 1995 and 0.79 percent higher in 1996. This premium reflected the riskier borrower base served by finance companies. However, in the late 1990s and early 2000s, the pattern reversed dramatically. Because new car sales by U.S. firms were lower than normal, auto finance companies owned by major manufacturers slashed rates, sometimes to as low as 0.9 percent. After the September 2001 terrorist attacks, some major auto manufacturers lowered rates on new car loans to zero percent to boost sales. By 2002, finance companies were charging 3.33 percent less than commercial banks. It was not until 2013 that the historical trend returned, with finance companies charging 0.27 percent more than banks. In 2021, finance companies charged 0.22 percent less than commercial banks, again reflecting promotional captive financing.

The higher rates that finance companies generally charge for consumer loans are mostly attributable to the fact that finance companies attract riskier customers than commercial banks. Customers who seek loans from finance companies are often judged too risky to obtain loans from commercial banks or thrifts. Subprime lender: a finance company that lends to high-risk customers, sometimes even those with a bankruptcy on their records. Loan sharks: subprime lenders that charge unfairly exorbitant rates to desperate subprime borrowers, sometimes as high as 30 percent per year or more.

Payday Loan Annual Percentage Rate
\[ \text{APR} = \left( \frac{\text{Fee}}{\text{Principal}} \right) \times \left( \frac{\text{Number of Periods per Year}}{1} \right) \times 100\% \]

Where the fee is the dollar charge for the loan (e.g., $15 per $100 borrowed), the principal is the amount borrowed ($100), and the number of periods per year reflects how many times the loan term fits into a year. For a typical two-week payday loan:

\[ \text{APR} = \left( \frac{15}{100} \right) \times \left( \frac{52}{2} \right) \times 100\% = 0.15 \times 26 \times 100\% = 390\% \]

This formula annualizes the cost of short-term credit so that loans with different maturities can be compared on a common basis. The 390 percent APR for payday loans is dramatically higher than rates on conventional bank loans. Lenders argue that such rates are necessary to cover high default rates, operating costs, and the risk of lending to borrowers with poor credit histories. Critics contend the rates are exorbitant and trap financially strapped borrowers in a cycle of paying additional fees to renew the same principal. The payday lending industry is regulated at the state level; as of 2021, 32 states and the District of Columbia imposed an interest rate limit of 36 percent or less on a $2,000, two-year installment loan.

Example

Jayhawk Acceptance Corp., a subprime lender, began marketing loans for tummy tucks, hair transplants, and other procedures not usually covered by health insurance. Borrowers who paid within one year paid 9.9 percent annually; those who repaid within the maximum of two years paid 13.9 percent per year. Skeptical analysts noted the practical problem of repossessing collateral for a cosmetic surgery loan—"no one could repossess a nose job." Jayhawk eventually declared bankruptcy. This example illustrates the extreme end of subprime lending, where collateral is intangible and default risk is difficult to manage. It also demonstrates that the willingness of finance companies to serve high-risk borrowers can produce losses when risk management fails.

Analogy

Subprime lending by finance companies is analogous to a landlord renting an apartment to a tenant with a history of evictions and damaged properties. The landlord charges a higher security deposit and higher monthly rent than market rates for prime tenants. If the tenant defaults, the landlord loses the unpaid rent and may incur repair costs. Similarly, finance companies charge higher interest rates to subprime borrowers to compensate for the elevated probability of default. When the economy turns sour and defaults spike simultaneously across many borrowers, the finance company can face catastrophic losses—just as a landlord with many risky tenants will suffer when a local economic downturn causes widespread job losses.

Key Insight

The higher interest rates charged by finance companies are primarily risk premiums reflecting a riskier borrower base, not monopolistic pricing. However, the payday lending segment demonstrates that some lenders may charge rates that far exceed what expected default costs would justify, particularly when borrowers are desperate and financially unsophisticated. Regulatory intervention at the state and federal levels has sought to cap these rates, but enforcement is complicated by lender partnerships with nationally chartered banks in states without usury ceilings.

Key Takeaways

  • Motor vehicle loans and leases are the dominant consumer loan product, making up 32.5 percent of finance company assets in 2021.
  • Finance companies historically charged 0.79–1.62 percent more than banks on new car loans due to riskier borrowers.
  • Promotional financing by captive auto finance companies periodically drives rates below bank rates—even to zero percent—particularly after 2001 and during the 2008–2009 crisis.
  • Subprime lenders extend credit to borrowers with poor or no credit histories, including those with bankruptcies.
  • Payday lenders charge fees equivalent to 390 percent APR on two-week loans of $15 per $100 borrowed.
  • As of July 2021, 32 states and the District of Columbia impose interest rate limits of 36 percent or less on $2,000, two-year installment loans.

6. Real Estate Lending and Mortgage Securitization

Residential and commercial mortgages became a major component of finance company portfolios in the 1980s and 1990s, even though finance companies did not generally deal in mortgages in the 1970s. Since finance companies are not subject to as extensive regulations as banks, they are often willing to issue mortgages to riskier borrowers than commercial banks would accept, compensating for this additional risk by charging higher interest rates and fees. Mortgages include all loans secured by liens on any type of real estate and can be made either directly or as securitized mortgage assets.

Securitized mortgage assets: mortgages that are pooled with similar characteristics, removed from the originating institution's balance sheet, and sold as interests in the pool to secondary market investors. This process creates mortgage-backed securities (e.g., government agency securities, collateralized mortgage obligations) that can be traded in secondary mortgage markets. The originating finance company may still service the mortgage portfolio for a fee even after the mortgages are removed from its balance sheet. Mortgage servicing is a fee-related business in which the flow of mortgage repayments (interest and principal) is collected and passed on to investors in whole loans or securitization vehicles, with the servicer charging a fee for this intermediation activity.

The mortgages in finance company portfolios can be first mortgages or second mortgages in the form of home equity loans, which allow customers to borrow on a line of credit secured with a second mortgage on their home. Home equity loans became very profitable for finance companies after the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which disallowed the tax deductibility of consumer interest payments other than those on home mortgages. Specifically, interest on first and second mortgages secured by residential real estate is tax deductible, while interest on other types of individual loans (such as credit card loans) is not. Additionally, the bad debt expense and administrative costs on home equity loans are lower than on other finance company loans.
