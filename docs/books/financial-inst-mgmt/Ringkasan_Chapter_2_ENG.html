Chapter 2 Summary

Financial Services and Depository Institutions

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 2. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

The main theme of the book is that the products sold and the risks faced by modern financial institutions (FIs) are becoming increasingly similar, as are the techniques used to measure and manage those risks. In 1999, the U.S. Congress passed the Financial Services Modernization Act (FSMA), which repealed regulations that set barriers between commercial banking, insurance, and investment banking, and allowed for the creation of financial services holding companies that could engage in banking, insurance, and securities activities at once.

This chapter examines the three major groups of FIs known as depository institutions, namely commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions. For each group, four topics are covered, namely size, structure, and composition of the industry, balance sheets and recent trends, regulation, and industry performance.

2. Commercial Banks

2.1 Size, Structure, and Composition of the Industry

Commercial banks make up the largest group of depository institutions measured by asset size. They accept deposits (liabilities) and make loans (assets), with a much more varied composition of assets and liabilities than savings institutions and credit unions. In December 2021, the United States had 4,231 commercial banks, down sharply from 14,416 banks in 1985 and 12,744 in 1989, largely as a result of mergers and acquisitions.

The number of bank branches increased from 43,293 in 1985 to an all-time high of 85,005 in 2009, then declined to 74,583 as of December 2020 due to consolidation. Some of the largest mergers ever occurred, such as J.P. Morgan's acquisition of Chase Manhattan ($33.6 billion, 2000), Bank of America's acquisition of FleetBoston ($49.3 billion, 2003), JPMorgan Chase's acquisition of Bank One ($60.0 billion, 2004), and Bank of New York's acquisition of Mellon Financial ($18.3 billion, 2007).

Consolidation changed asset concentration. The asset share of small banks (under $1 billion) fell from 36.6 percent in 1984 to just 4.7 percent in 2021, while the share of the largest banks (over $1 billion) rose from 63.4 percent to 95.3 percent. Small banks, or community banks, tend to specialize in retail or consumer banking, such as residential mortgages and consumer loans. Regional and superregional banks serve large corporate customers regionally and nationally, while money center banks such as Bank of New York Mellon, Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase, and HSBC Bank USA rely heavily on nondeposit or borrowed funds and are major participants in foreign currency markets, exposing them to foreign exchange risk.

Loan portfolio structure differs by bank size. Small banks (assets under $1 billion) hold 74.49 percent of their loans in real estate loans and only 14.29 percent in commercial and industrial (C&I) loans, whereas large banks (assets of $1 billion or more) have a more balanced portfolio, with real estate 43.83 percent, C&I 21.88 percent, consumer loans 9.44 percent, and credit cards 7.82 percent.

2.2 Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

Loans and investment securities remain the primary assets of the banking industry. Real estate loans were the major asset on bank balance sheets between 1984 and 2019, but starting in the first quarter of 2020 their proportion declined due to the COVID-19 pandemic. In contrast, the proportion of securities and cash increased because government assistance programs, combined with increased personal savings, contributed to a record inflow of deposits, leading banks to shift toward shorter-term, lower-yielding assets.

On the liability side, deposits amounted to $18,410.3 billion, or 82.9 percent of total liabilities and equity. Transaction accounts constituted 31.2 percent of deposits ($5,735.6 billion), including non-interest-bearing demand deposits and interest-bearing checking accounts (most commonly NOW accounts). Nontransaction accounts constituted 60.6 percent ($11,162.8 billion), including money market deposit accounts (MMDAs) as the largest source of deposit funds, other savings deposits, and time deposits (certificates of deposit, CDs). Borrowings and other liabilities totaled about 7.9 percent of all bank liabilities ($1,572.5 billion), including federal funds purchases and repurchase agreements.

Commercial bank equity capital was about 10.0 percent of total liabilities and equity in 2021, implying that 90.0 percent of assets were funded by debt. Because of this high leverage, even a relatively small number of loan defaults can wipe out a bank's equity and leave it insolvent. Regulators require banks to hold a minimum level of equity as a buffer against losses. Bank liability structures generally have shorter maturities than asset portfolios, making maturity mismatch (interest rate risk) and liquidity risk key concerns for bank managers.

2.3 Capital Assistance Programs and Stress Tests

Part of the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) of 2008-2009 was the Capital Purchase Program (CPP), under which the Treasury purchased more than $200 billion of senior preferred equity. TARP also provided emergency funding to Citigroup ($25 billion) and Bank of America ($20 billion). Through December 2009, $204.9 billion of TARP capital had been allocated, and as of December 31, 2021, $196.62 billion had been paid back plus $30.16 billion in dividends and other proceeds.

Under the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, the largest banks are subject to annual stress tests, with scenarios ranging from mild to calamitous. Extreme scenarios include a 5 percent decline in gross domestic product (GDP), an unemployment rate of 12 percent, and a stock market that loses half its value. In the first stress test (February 2009), 10 of the 19 largest depository institutions needed to raise a total of $74.6 billion in capital, and within a month of the May 7, 2009 release they had raised $149.45 billion. The results of the Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST) 2021 showed that the 23 participating firms had sufficient capital to absorb losses under the severely adverse scenario.

2.4 Off-Balance-Sheet Activities

The balance sheet does not reflect the total scope of bank activities. Banks conduct many fee-related activities off the balance sheet, known as off-balance-sheet (OBS) activities. An item is an OBS asset if, when a contingent event occurs, it moves onto the asset side of the balance sheet or an income item is realized, and an OBS liability if it moves onto the liability side or an expense is realized. OBS activities include issuing guarantees such as letters of credit, making future commitments to lend, and engaging in derivative transactions (futures, forwards, options, and swaps).

Banks undertake OBS activities to earn additional fee income to complement declining margins, while avoiding regulatory costs or taxes, since reserve requirements and deposit insurance premiums are not levied on OBS activities. However, OBS activities also add to insolvency risk. The notional value of bank OBS activities grew from $10,236.7 billion in 1992 to a peak of $228,467.4 billion in 2012, then declined to $188,189.6 billion in 2021, while on-balance-sheet assets grew from $3,506.2 billion to $22,195.2 billion. Note that notional value overstates risk, since the potential gain or loss is based on the change in market value of the contract, normally less than 3 percent of notional value.

The Volcker Rule, part of the 2010 legislation, prohibits U.S. depository institutions from engaging in proprietary trading (trading as a principal for the bank's own account). It was implemented in April 2014, with compliance required by July 21, 2015, contributing to a reduction in derivatives held off the balance sheet. Noninterest income as a percentage of total operating income rose from 22.66 percent in 1979 to 30.14 percent in 2018.

Two other fee-generating activities are trust services, where banks hold and manage assets for individuals (about one-half of all trust assets) and pension funds, and correspondent banking, the provision of services such as check clearing and foreign exchange trading to other banks that lack the staff to perform them.

2.5 Regulation

Unlike banks in countries with one or two regulators, U.S. banks may be supervised by up to four regulators, namely the FDIC, the OCC, the Federal Reserve System (FRS), and state bank regulators. This reflects the dual banking system, in which banks may choose a national or a state charter. In December 2021, there were 747 nationally chartered banks and 3,484 state-chartered banks, holding about 66 percent and 34 percent of total commercial bank assets, respectively.

  • FDIC provides deposit insurance to deter runs and contagious panics, levies insurance premiums, manages the deposit insurance fund (DIF), carries out bank examinations, and acts as receiver and liquidator when an insured bank is closed. A vivid example of the importance of insurance is that Bear Stearns (an investment bank) suffered the first major run on a U.S. FI since the Great Depression in March 2008 because its liabilities had no federal insurance
  • OCC is the oldest bank regulatory agency, established in 1863 as a subagency of the U.S. Treasury, and charters, examines, and closes national banks
  • Federal Reserve, besides conducting monetary policy, regulates member banks and bank holding companies, with membership advantages including access to the federal funds wire transfer network (Fedwire) and the discount window as lender of last resort. Since 1980 all banks face uniform reserve requirements
  • State authorities perform functions similar to those of the OCC for state-chartered banks

2.6 Industry Performance

Commercial bank performance is strongly cyclical. In 1999 bank earnings reached a record $71.6 billion with an average ROA of 1.31 percent. In 2003 net income rose to $106.3 billion with ROA of 1.40 percent and ROE of 15.34 percent, both all-time highs, supported by Federal Reserve rate cuts (13 times), a housing boom, and new financial instruments such as credit derivatives and mortgage-backed securities. In 2006 ROA and ROE were 1.33 percent and 13.02 percent, and no banks failed (compared with 206 failures in 1989).

The 2007-2009 crisis hit hard. 2007 net income fell to $105.5 billion (down 27.4 percent from 2006), and in 2008 net income collapsed to just $10.2 billion (down 89.8 percent) with ROA of 0.13 percent, the lowest since 1987, and 23.6 percent of institutions unprofitable. Losses from OBS securities exceeded $1 trillion worldwide through 2009. As the economy recovered, ROA and ROE returned closer to their pre-crisis levels.

3. Savings Institutions

3.1 History, Structure, and the 1980s Crisis

Savings institutions were first created in the early 1800s to serve individuals needing funds to buy homes, since commercial banks concentrated on serving business enterprises. Savings institutions comprise two groups, savings associations (SAs), which historically concentrated on residential mortgages, and savings banks (SBs), which are more diversified, holding some commercial loans, corporate bonds, and corporate stock, and rely more heavily on deposits. Many savings banks were established as mutual organizations in which depositors are also the legal owners of the bank.

At the end of the 1970s there were slightly fewer than 4,000 savings associations with assets of about $0.6 trillion. Between October 1979 and October 1982, the Federal Reserve's restrictive monetary policy drove T-bill rates as high as 16 percent. Savings associations faced negative interest spreads because they funded fixed-rate long-term residential mortgages with short-term deposits, and were constrained by Regulation Q interest ceilings, causing disintermediation as depositors moved funds to money market mutual funds. To address this, the DIDMCA (1980) and DIA (1982) granted new powers, such as NOW accounts, MMDAs, adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs), and limited commercial and consumer lending.

However, in the mid-1980s real estate prices in Texas and the Southwest collapsed, followed by downturns in the Northeast and Western states. Many borrowers defaulted, and the risk-taking (moral hazard) behavior was exacerbated by the FSLIC, which could not close insolvent savings associations (a policy of regulator forbearance). Savings associations declined from 4,000 (1980) to 2,600 (1989), down 35 percent, even though their assets doubled from $600 billion to $1.2 trillion. The New England real estate crash of 1990-1991 then brought down many savings banks.

The crisis produced two landmark laws. FIRREA (1989) abolished the FSLIC, replaced it with SAIF under the FDIC, created the Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC), which by its dissolution in 1995 had closed more than 700 institutions at an estimated cost of $200 billion, and imposed the Qualified Thrift Lender (QTL) test requiring thrifts to hold at least 65 percent of assets in residential mortgage-related assets. FDICIA (1991) introduced risk-based deposit insurance premiums (from 1993) and prompt corrective action (PCA), under which an institution whose equity-to-assets ratio falls below 2 percent must be closed or recapitalized within three months. As a result, the industry shrank from 3,677 institutions (1989) to 2,262 (1993), down 38 percent, with assets falling from $1.427 trillion to $1.001 trillion (down 30 percent).

3.2 Balance Sheet 2021

In 2021 total assets of savings institutions were about $1.524 trillion. Real estate loans accounted for 29.17 percent of total assets, higher than the 21.69 percent at commercial banks. Cash and U.S. government securities amounted to 47.06 percent of assets (compared with 35.53 percent at commercial banks) for liquidity and reserve requirement purposes. Deposits were the predominant source of funds at 84.73 percent of total liabilities and net worth, followed by borrowings from the Federal Home Loan Banks (FHLBs, 12 banks owned by the savings institutions themselves). Net worth amounted to 9.66 percent of total assets in 2021, compared with 9.97 percent at commercial banks.

3.3 Regulation

The main regulators of savings institutions are the OCC and the FDIC. FIRREA (1989) created the Office of Thrift Supervision (OTS) to charter and examine federal savings institutions. Because its performance was judged weak during the financial crisis, the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act mandated the consolidation of the OTS into the OCC, effective July 21, 2011, with the OTS ceasing to exist on October 19, 2011. On the insurance side, the SAIF and the Bank Insurance Fund (BIF) were merged into the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) in January 2007, so thrifts now operate under the same regulatory structure as commercial banks.

3.4 Industry Performance

Savings institution performance mirrors the commercial bank cycle. In 1999 they reported net income of $10.7 billion with ROA of 1.00 percent. In 2007 net income fell to $6.0 billion with ROA of 0.13 percent, and in 2008 net income was negative $8.6 billion with ROA of -0.72 percent, the first negative earnings year since 1991, while only six institutions failed or were assisted, including Washington Mutual, the largest savings institution with over $300 billion in assets. After recovery, in 2021 ROA and ROE were 1.11 percent and 11.29 percent, respectively, with the number of institutions down to 608.

4. Credit Unions

4.1 Characteristics and Structure

Credit unions (CUs) are nonprofit depository institutions mutually organized and owned by their members (depositors). The first U.S. credit unions were established in the early 1900s in Massachusetts as self-help organizations. Unlike commercial banks and savings institutions, CUs are prohibited from serving the general public and only serve members who share a common bond of occupation (e.g., police CUs), association (e.g., university-affiliated CUs), or a well-defined neighborhood, community, or rural district. The primary objective of CUs is to satisfy the depository and lending needs of their members, and because they are nonprofit, their net income is not taxed and they are not subject to the local investment requirements of the Community Reinvestment Act. This tax-exempt status allows CUs to offer higher deposit rates and lower loan rates than banks and savings institutions.

CUs are the most numerous of the depository institutions, totaling 4,942 in 2021, with more than 129.6 million members. Traditionally, more than 60 percent of CU assets have been in small consumer loans, often under $10,000, and CUs hold large amounts of government securities (14.2 percent of assets in 2021) and relatively few residential mortgages. This made CUs more resilient to crises, for example in 2008 the average CU ROA was 0.31 percent, better than commercial banks (0.13 percent) and savings institutions (-0.72 percent). In 2021 business loans represented only 5.9 percent of the industry's lending.

The national credit union system has three tiers, namely the U.S. Central Credit Union at the national level, corporate credit unions (11 institutions) at the state or regional level that serve member credit unions by investing and lending excess funds, and local credit unions at the bottom tier.

4.2 Balance Sheet 2021

In December 2021, 4,942 credit unions had assets of $2,060.4 billion, compared with $155 billion in 1987. The average CU size was $416.9 million, far smaller than the average bank at $5,245.9 million, and total CU assets are less than half the size of JPMorgan Chase. Cash and equivalents reached 12.6 percent of assets, investment securities 22.0 percent, and total loans 60.9 percent. Member loans consist of home mortgages at 26.7 percent and small consumer loans at 23.1 percent, so together member loans amount to 49.8 percent of total assets. Funding comes mainly from member deposits (89.7 percent), with regular shares at 35.5 percent, money market accounts 22.0 percent, share drafts 19.9 percent, and share certificates (CDs) 13.4 percent. The CU capital-to-assets ratio in December 2021 was 10.27 percent, compared with 9.66 percent for savings institutions and 9.97 percent for commercial banks.

4.3 Regulation and Performance

CUs may be federally or state chartered. In 2021, 62.7 percent of the 4,942 CUs were federally chartered and subject to National Credit Union Administration (NCUA) regulation, accounting for 52.6 percent of membership and 50.5 percent of assets. The NCUA also provides deposit insurance of up to $250,000 through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund (NCUSIF), which now covers 98 percent of all credit union deposits.

CU assets grew more than 6.9 percent annually from 1999 to 2021, and membership increased from 77.5 million (1999) to over 129.6 million (2018). Industry ROA in 2021 was 1.07 percent, with the largest CUs reaching 1.20 percent while the smallest (assets under $10 million) earned only 0.18 percent. Because of mutual ownership, profit growth is not the primary goal of CUs, which instead aim to serve members' deposit and lending needs as long as capital is sufficient to protect against unexpected losses.

However, corporate credit unions nearly collapsed during the crisis. They reported $18 billion in unrealized securities losses as of November 2008, and local credit unions' deposits in corporates fell nearly 49 percent between March 31, 2008 and September 30, 2008, from $44.7 billion to $22.9 billion, while the corporates had accumulated $50 billion in toxic mortgage-backed securities. In March 2009 the NCUA placed U.S. Central and WesCorp into conservatorship, and as of the third quarter of 2010 the NCUA held roughly 70 percent of the assets of the corporate credit union system. Ultimately, five of the largest corporate credit unions were declared insolvent.

5. Global Financial Performance

The U.S. financial crisis quickly spread worldwide. In 2008 the largest banks in the Netherlands, Switzerland, and the United Kingdom had net losses, while banks in Ireland, Spain, and the United Kingdom were especially hard hit due to large investments in mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. Governments responded in various ways, for example Germany guaranteed all consumer deposits and bailed out Hypo Real Estate, the U.K. nationalized Bradford & Bingley and raised deposit guarantees from $62,220 to $88,890 per account, Ireland guaranteed deposits and debt of its six major institutions, Iceland rescued its third largest bank for $860 million, and the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg injected $16.37 billion into Fortis NV. The Bank of England lowered its target rate to a record low of 1 percent.

The Greek debt crisis then threatened the European financial system. In March 2010 a bailout plan for Greece of up to $41 billion began to take shape, but in late April 2010 Greek bond prices dropped dramatically after the 2009 budget deficit proved worse than reported and Moody's downgraded Greek debt. A run on Greek banks followed, with withdrawals reaching as high as 750 million per day, nearly 0.5 percent of the 170 billion deposit base. U.S. bank exposure to Greece, Ireland, Portugal, and Spain totaled $43.1 billion, $113.9 billion, $47.1 billion, and $187.5 billion, respectively, and worldwide bank exposure to these four countries reached $2,512.3 billion. On May 9, 2010, European finance ministers and the IMF approved a $147 billion rescue package with a $1 trillion safety net. The situation stabilized after 2012, and on December 18, 2012 Standard & Poor's raised Greek debt six notches to B minus.

In June 2016 the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union (Brexit) by 17.4 million to 16.1 million. The U.K. formally left on January 31, 2020, with the transition ending December 31, 2020. Brexit slowed U.K. growth from 2.4 percent (2015) to 1.6 percent (2019), with an estimated cost of up to 6.7 percent of growth over 15 years, the pound fell from $1.48 to $1.36 the day after the referendum, and business investment fell 11 percent between 2016 and 2019.

6. Key Calculations and Formulas

This section summarizes the important formulas used to assess the performance of depository institutions.

6.1 Return on Assets (ROA)

ROA measures earnings per dollar of assets, calculated as net income divided by total assets.

\[ ROA = \frac{NI}{TA} \]

where \( NI \) is net income and \( TA \) is total assets.

6.2 Return on Equity (ROE)

ROE measures the return to shareholders, calculated as net income divided by equity, which equals ROA times the ratio of assets to equity (the equity multiplier).

\[ ROE = \frac{NI}{E} = ROA \times \frac{TA}{E} \]

where \( E \) is equity. Because large banks hold less equity per dollar of assets, their net income per dollar of equity (ROE) is generally larger.

6.3 The DuPont Decomposition

ROE can be decomposed into three components through DuPont analysis, namely the net profit margin, asset utilization, and the equity multiplier.

\[ ROE = \frac{NI}{S} \times \frac{S}{TA} \times \frac{TA}{E} \]

where \( S \) is sales (revenue). This decomposition helps trace the underlying sources of bank profitability.

6.4 Leverage and the PCA Rule

The equity-to-assets ratio of commercial banks in 2021 was about 10.0 percent, meaning 90.0 percent of assets were funded by debt. By comparison, the capital-to-assets ratios in December 2021 were 10.27 percent for credit unions, 9.66 percent for savings institutions, and 9.97 percent for commercial banks.

\[ \frac{E}{TA} \lt 2\% \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{close or recapitalize within 3 months} \]

This is the prompt corrective action (PCA) threshold, which requires regulators to close or recapitalize an institution within three months if its equity-to-assets ratio falls below 2 percent.

7. Summary and Conclusion

This chapter provided an overview of the major activities of the three groups of depository institutions, namely commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions, together with their regulators. All of these institutions rely heavily on deposits to fund their activities, although borrowed funds are becoming increasingly important for the largest institutions. Historically, commercial banks concentrated on commercial lending and securities investment, savings institutions on mortgage lending, and credit unions on consumer lending, but these differences are being eroded by competitive forces, regulation, and changing financial and business technology.

Key lessons from this chapter are that industry structure changes dramatically through mergers and consolidation, regulation has evolved from strict separation (Glass-Steagall) toward integration (FSMA) and tighter prudential oversight (Dodd-Frank, stress tests, PCA), and performance is strongly influenced by economic cycles and crises, from the S&L crisis of the 1980s to the global financial crisis of 2008-2009 and the European debt crisis. Performance measures such as ROA, ROE, and the DuPont decomposition are essential tools for assessing the health of depository institutions.
