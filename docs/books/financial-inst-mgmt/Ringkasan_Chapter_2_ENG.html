1. Introduction to Depository Institutions

1.1 Definition and Core Concept

A depository institution (DI) is a financial institution that obtains a significant proportion of its funding from customer deposits. The three major groups of DIs are commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions. These institutions form the backbone of the financial system by providing payment services, savings products, fiduciary services, and lending to both businesses and consumers.

The central theme of modern financial services is that the products sold and the risks faced by financial institutions are becoming increasingly similar, as are the techniques used to measure and manage those risks. This convergence accelerated with the passage of the Financial Services Modernization Act (FSMA) of 1999, which repealed the regulatory barriers that had separated commercial banking, insurance, and investment banking since the Glass-Steagall Act of 1933. The FSMA allowed the creation of financial services holding companies that could engage in banking, insurance, and securities activities simultaneously.

However, the financial crisis of 2007–2009 led to a partial reversal of this trend. The Volcker Rule, a provision of the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, prohibits bank holding companies from engaging in proprietary trading and limits their investments in hedge funds, private equity, and related vehicles. Despite these restrictions, many financial institutions continue to operate across multiple industry segments.

During the financial crisis, several nondepository financial institutions—including investment banks Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, and finance company GMAC—converted to bank holding companies. This conversion reflected the recognition that their business models had become too risky and that they needed the stability of bank deposits and access to Federal Reserve lending facilities. By becoming bank holding companies, these firms agreed to tighter regulations, higher capital reserves, greater disclosure, and closer supervision by government bank examiners.

The product-based balance sheet of depository institutions reveals their distinctive joint-product nature: DIs offer products on both sides of their balance sheets—loans on the asset side and deposits on the liability side. This dual role creates unique management challenges, as DI managers must simultaneously address risks arising from both assets and liabilities, including credit risk, liquidity risk, interest rate risk, and operational risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Depository institutions (commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions) are defined by their reliance on customer deposits as a primary funding source.
  • The FSMA of 1999 eliminated barriers between banking, insurance, and securities activities, enabling full-service financial institutions.
  • The Volcker Rule (2010) partially reversed this trend by prohibiting proprietary trading and limiting hedge fund investments by bank holding companies.
  • DIs have a joint-product nature: they originate loans (assets) funded by deposits (liabilities), creating complex risk management challenges.

1.2 Historical Product Evolution

Tables 2–1A and 2–1B in the source document illustrate the dramatic expansion of product offerings by depository institutions between 1950 and 2021. In 1950, DIs primarily offered payment services, savings products, fiduciary services, business lending, consumer lending, and debt underwriting. By 2021, DIs had expanded into equity underwriting, insurance and risk management products, and a broader range of lending and fiduciary services. This expansion reflects both regulatory changes and market-driven innovation.

Key Takeaways

  • DI product offerings expanded significantly from 1950 to 2021, moving into equity underwriting and insurance.
  • Regulatory changes (FSMA) and market forces drove this product expansion.

2. Commercial Banks: Industry Structure and Composition

2.1 Definition and Scope

Commercial banks are depository institutions that accept deposits (liabilities) and make loans (assets). They are the largest group of depository institutions measured by asset size. Commercial banks differ from savings institutions and credit unions in the composition of their assets and liabilities, which are much more varied. Commercial bank liabilities typically include several types of nondeposit sources of funds, while their loans include consumer, commercial, and real estate loans. Commercial banking activity is regulated separately from savings institutions and credit unions.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial banks are the largest DI group by asset size.
  • Their asset and liability compositions are more diversified than those of savings institutions or credit unions.
  • They are subject to separate regulatory oversight.

2.2 Size, Number, and Consolidation Trends

As of December 2021, the United States had 4,231 commercial banks. This number represents a dramatic decline from 14,416 banks in 1985 and 12,744 in 1989. The reduction in the number of banks is largely attributable to mergers and acquisitions, bank failures, and a relatively small number of new bank charters.

Figure 2–3 in the source illustrates the structural changes in the number of commercial banks from 1980 through 2021. The dominant factor driving consolidation has been mergers, particularly after regulators began allowing interstate mergers in the 1980s and 1990s. The Riegle-Neal Act of 1994 eased branching by banks across state lines, accelerating the trend toward nationwide banking.

The number of bank branches increased from 43,293 in 1985 to an all-time high of 85,005 in 2009, before declining to 74,583 by December 2020. This pattern reflects the consolidation of back-office operations alongside the expansion of branch networks, followed by the recent trend toward digital banking and branch reduction.

Key Takeaways

  • The number of U.S. commercial banks declined from 14,416 in 1985 to 4,231 in 2021.
  • Mergers and acquisitions are the primary driver of consolidation.
  • The Riegle-Neal Act (1994) enabled interstate branching, accelerating consolidation.
  • Branch numbers peaked in 2009 and have since declined due to digital banking trends.

2.3 Bank Categories by Size and Function

Commercial banks are categorized by asset size and functional scope into several types: community banks, regional or superregional banks, and money center banks.

Community banks are banks with assets under $1 billion. They specialize in retail or consumer banking, providing residential mortgages, consumer loans, and accessing local deposit bases. These banks focus on relationship banking, often basing lending decisions on personal knowledge of customers' creditworthiness and local business conditions. The asset share of community banks declined from 36.6 percent in 1984 to 4.7 percent in 2021, reflecting the significant consolidation in the industry.

Regional or superregional banks range in size from several billion to several hundred billion dollars in assets. They are headquartered in larger regional cities with offices and branches throughout large portions of the United States. These banks engage in a complete array of wholesale commercial banking activities, including consumer and residential lending as well as commercial and industrial (C&I) lending. They utilize retail deposit bases for funding but also develop relationships with large corporate customers and access purchased funds markets, such as the interbank or federal funds market.

Money center banks are the very largest banks, but asset size alone does not define them. What distinguishes a money center bank is its heavy reliance on nondeposit or borrowed sources of funds, its location (typically New York or Chicago), and its major participation in foreign currency markets. For example, Bank of America, with $2,427.5 billion in assets in 2021, is not a money center bank, while Bank of New York Mellon, with only $416.6 billion in assets, is a money center bank. Money center banks have few retail branches and rely primarily on wholesale and borrowed funds. They are major participants in foreign currency markets and are therefore subject to foreign exchange risk.

  • Community banks focus on relationship banking and provide essential small business lending services.

2.4 Loan Portfolio Composition by Bank Size

Figure 2–2 in the source shows the breakdown of loan portfolios for small banks (assets under $1 billion) versus large banks (assets $1 billion or more) as of December 2021. Small banks allocate 74.49% of their loan portfolio to real estate loans, 5.43% to farm loans, 14.29% to C&I loans, 0.30% to credit cards, 3.79% to consumer loans, and 1.69% to other loans. Large banks allocate 43.83% to real estate loans, 0.39% to farm loans, 21.88% to C&I loans, 7.82% to credit cards, 9.44% to consumer loans, and 16.63% to other loans.

The key difference is that small banks are far more concentrated in real estate lending and have a significant farm loan presence, while large banks have a more diversified loan portfolio with substantial C&I lending, credit card lending, and other loan categories. This difference reflects the distinct customer bases and business models of small versus large banks.

Key Takeaways

  • Small banks concentrate heavily on real estate loans (74.49% of portfolio).
  • Large banks have more diversified loan portfolios across real estate, C&I, credit cards, and consumer loans.

3. Commercial Banks: Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

3.1 Asset Structure

Loans and investment securities are the primary assets of the banking industry. Commercial loans are relatively more important for larger banks, while consumer loans, small business loans, and residential mortgages are more important for smaller banks. Each type of loan creates credit risk and, to varying extents, liquidity risk for the bank. The security portfolio is a source of liquidity and interest rate risk, especially with the increased use of mortgage-backed securities and structured notes.

Figure 2–4 in the source shows portfolio shifts over the 1984–2021 period for four principal earning asset areas: commercial and industrial loans, real estate loans, loans to individuals, and securities, as well as cash assets. Real estate loans were the major asset on bank balance sheets between 1984 and 2019. Starting in Q1 2020, real estate loans as a percentage of total assets began to decline due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Declines in C&I loans and loans to individuals were also observed for 2020 and 2021. In contrast, the proportion of securities and cash assets increased from Q1 2020, driven by government assistance programs, increased personal savings, and record deposit inflows that created historically high bank liquidity.

Key Takeaways

  • Loans constitute 46.84% of bank assets; real estate loans are the largest loan category.
  • Securities (25.17% of assets) are primarily U.S. government securities.
  • High leverage (equity ~10% of assets) means credit risk is a critical concern.

3.2 Liability Structure

Commercial banks have two major sources of funds other than equity: deposits and borrowed or other liability funds. A major difference between banks and other firms is banks' high leverage. In 2021, the average equity-to-assets ratio was 10.0%, implying that 90.0% of bank assets were funded by debt, either deposits or borrowed funds.

Deposits amounted to $18,410.3 billion, or 82.95% of total liabilities and equity. Transaction accounts (checkable deposits) constituted $5,735.6 billion (31.2% of total deposits). These include non-interest-bearing demand deposits and interest-bearing checking accounts, most commonly called NOW accounts (negotiable order of withdrawal accounts). Since their introduction in 1980, interest-bearing checking accounts—especially NOW accounts—have dominated transaction accounts. However, due to limitations on corporate holdings of such accounts and minimum balance requirements, non-interest-bearing demand deposits are still held.

Nontransaction accounts include money market deposit accounts (MMDAs), other savings deposits, and time deposits. MMDAs are high-yield savings accounts that allow depository institutions to compete with money market mutual funds (MMMFs). They generally earn interest at a higher rate than standard savings accounts but have no fixed holding period, although withdrawal limits may apply. Certificates of deposit (CDs) are time deposits that require customers to deposit funds for a specific length of time and typically earn higher rates than MMDAs. In December 2021, nontransaction accounts constituted 60.6% of total deposits, or $11,162.8 billion.

Nondeposit liabilities comprise borrowings and other liabilities totaling $1,572.5 billion (7.09% of total liabilities). These include purchases of federal funds (bank reserves) on the interbank market, repurchase agreements (temporary swaps of securities for federal funds), and longer-term notes and bonds.

The liability structure of bank balance sheets tends to reflect a shorter maturity structure than the asset portfolio, with relatively more liquid instruments (deposits, interbank borrowings) used to fund less liquid assets (loans). This creates maturity mismatch, which exposes banks to interest rate risk and liquidity risk.

Analogy

A bank's liability structure is like a restaurant that borrows ingredients (deposits) that can be recalled at any time (short-term) while cooking dishes (loans) that take months or years to prepare (long-term). If many customers demand their ingredients back simultaneously, the restaurant may not have enough prepared dishes to sell—this is the essence of liquidity risk and maturity mismatch.

Key Takeaways

  • Deposits fund 82.95% of bank assets; equity funds only 9.97%.
  • Transaction accounts (checkable deposits) are 31.2% of deposits; nontransaction accounts are 60.6%.
  • MMDAs are high-yield savings accounts with no fixed holding period; CDs are time deposits with fixed terms.
  • Maturity mismatch (short-term liabilities funding long-term assets) creates interest rate and liquidity risk.

3.3 Equity Capital

Commercial bank equity capital consists mainly of common and preferred stock (at par value), surplus or additional paid-in capital, and retained earnings. Regulators require banks to hold a minimum level of equity capital as a buffer against losses from on- and off-balance-sheet activities. Because deposit funding is relatively low-cost, banks tend to hold equity close to the minimum levels set by regulators. This impacts banks' risk exposures and their ability to grow—both on and off the balance sheet—over time.

During the 2008–2009 financial crisis, the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) included the Capital Purchase Program (CPP), which encouraged U.S. financial institutions to build capital. The Treasury purchased more than $200 billion of senior preferred equity. Through December 2009, $204.9 billion of TARP capital injections had been allocated to DIs, of which $196.62 billion was paid back plus $30.16 billion in dividends and other proceeds as of December 31, 2021.

The Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 requires the largest banks to undergo annual stress tests designed to ensure adequate capitalization. The original stress test in February 2009 measured the ability of the 19 largest DIs to withstand a protracted economic slump. Results showed that 10 of the 19 DIs needed to raise a total of $74.6 billion in capital; within a month, they had raised $149.45 billion. The Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test (DFAST) 2021 showed that large banks have sufficient capital levels to absorb losses during stressful conditions, due in large part to the substantial buildup of capital since the 2007–2009 financial crisis.

Key Takeaways

  • Equity capital acts as a buffer against losses; regulators set minimum capital requirements.
  • TARP injected $204.9 billion into DIs during the financial crisis; most has been repaid.
  • Annual stress tests ensure the largest banks can withstand severe economic scenarios.

4. Commercial Banks: Off-Balance-Sheet Activities

4.1 Definition and Importance

Off-balance-sheet (OBS) activities are fee-generating activities that do not appear on the current balance sheet under standard accounting rules. They include issuing guarantees (such as letters of credit), making future commitments to lend, and engaging in derivative transactions (futures, forwards, options, and swaps). An off-balance-sheet asset is an item that moves onto the asset side of the balance sheet or generates income when a contingent event occurs. An off-balance-sheet liability is an item that moves onto the liability side or generates an expense when a contingent event occurs.

Banks use OBS activities to earn additional fee income to complement declining margins or spreads on traditional lending. At the same time, they can avoid regulatory costs, since reserve requirements and deposit insurance premiums are not levied on OBS activities. Thus, banks have both earnings and regulatory "tax-avoidance" incentives to undertake OBS activities.

However, OBS activities can involve risks that add to the overall insolvency exposure of a financial institution. At the heart of the financial crisis were losses associated with OBS mortgage-backed securities. Losses from the falling value of OBS securities reached over $1 trillion worldwide through 2009, resulting in the failure, acquisition, or bailout of some of the largest FIs. When used appropriately, however, OBS activities can also reduce or hedge interest rate, credit, and foreign exchange risks.

Key Takeaways

  • OBS activities generate fee income without appearing on the balance sheet.
  • They help banks avoid regulatory costs (reserve requirements, deposit insurance premiums).
  • OBS activities can increase or decrease risk depending on how they are used.
  • OBS mortgage-backed securities were central to the 2007–2009 financial crisis.

4.2 Growth and Distribution of OBS Activities

The notional (face) value of OBS activities at commercial banks was $10,236.7 billion in 1992, compared to $3,506.2 billion of on-balance-sheet activities. By 2012, the notional value of OBS bank activities had increased to $228,467.4 billion (compared to $13,387.5 billion of on-balance-sheet activities) before falling to $188,737.9 billion in 2015 and $188,189.6 billion in 2021.

The decline after 2012 is partly attributable to the Volcker Rule, which prohibits U.S. depository institutions from engaging in proprietary trading. The Volcker Rule was implemented in April 2014, with full compliance required by July 21, 2015. This resulted in a reduction in derivative securities held off-balance-sheet by financial institutions.

The notional value of OBS activities does not accurately reflect the risk to the bank. The potential gain or loss is based on the possible change in market value over the life of the contract, which is normally less than 3% of the notional value of an OBS contract.

  • Interest rate derivatives are the largest OBS category (48.8% of total in 2021).
  • Volcker Rule contributed to the decline in OBS activity after 2012.
  • Actual risk exposure is much smaller than notional value (typically <3%).
  • Noninterest income from OBS activities has grown significantly over time.

4.3 Policy Responses to OBS Risks

The financial crisis revealed the systemic risks of OBS activities. The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) gave the U.S. Treasury funds to buy "toxic" mortgages and other securities from financial institutions. The Public-Private Investment Fund (PPIF) was created to acquire real estate-related OBS assets, initially funded at $500 billion with plans to expand to $1.25 trillion. The purchases of $1.25 trillion in OBS mortgage-backed securities were completed in March 2010.

Regulators responded by imposing capital requirements on OBS activities and explicitly recognizing FIs' solvency risk exposure from such activities. In spring 2009, regulators proposed extending regulatory oversight to unregulated over-the-counter (OTC) derivative securities. The regulation requires that all OTC derivative contracts be subject to regulation, all derivatives dealers be subject to supervision, and regulators be empowered to enforce rules against manipulation and abuse.

Key Takeaways

  • TARP and PPIF were created to address OBS-related toxic assets during the financial crisis.
  • Post-crisis regulation extended oversight to OTC derivatives and imposed capital requirements on OBS activities.

5. Commercial Banks: Other Fee-Generating Activities

5.1 Trust Services

The trust department of a commercial bank holds and manages assets for individuals or corporations. Only the largest banks have sufficient staff to offer trust services. Individual trusts represent about one-half of all trust assets managed by commercial banks, including estate assets and assets delegated by less financially sophisticated investors. Pension fund assets are the second largest group of trust assets managed by commercial banks. Banks manage the pension funds, act as trustees for any bonds held by the funds, and act as transfer and disbursement agents.

Key Takeaways

  • Trust services involve managing assets for individuals and corporations.
  • Only large banks typically offer trust services due to staffing requirements.

5.2 Correspondent Banking

Correspondent banking is the provision of banking services to other banks that do not have the staff resources to perform the services themselves. These services include check clearing and collection, foreign exchange trading, hedging services, and participation in large loan and security issuances. Correspondent banking services are generally sold as a package, and payment is typically in the form of non-interest-bearing deposits held at the bank offering the correspondent services.

Key Takeaways

  • Correspondent banking provides specialized services to smaller banks.
  • Payment is usually in the form of non-interest-bearing deposits.

6. Commercial Banks: Regulation

6.1 The Regulatory Framework

Unlike banks in countries with one or two regulators, U.S. banks may be subject to the supervision and regulations of up to four separate regulators: the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the Federal Reserve System (FRS), and state bank regulators.

The FDIC provides deposit insurance to deter depositor runs and contagious panics. In exchange for insuring deposits, the FDIC levies insurance premiums on member banks, manages the deposit insurance fund, and carries out bank examinations. When an insured bank is closed, the FDIC acts as receiver and liquidator. The FDIC manages the Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF), which combines the former commercial bank and savings association insurance funds since 2007.

The OCC, established in 1863, is the oldest bank regulatory agency and a subagency of the U.S. Treasury. Its primary function is to charter and close national banks. The OCC also examines national banks and has the power to approve or disapprove their merger applications.

The Federal Reserve System has regulatory power over banks and, when relevant, their holding company parents. All nationally chartered banks are automatically members of the Federal Reserve System; state-chartered banks may choose to become members. The primary advantages of FRS membership are direct access to the federal funds wire transfer network and the discount window for lender of last resort borrowing. The Federal Reserve regulates and examines bank holding companies because the holding company's management can influence the risk exposure of bank subsidiaries.

State authorities regulate state-chartered commercial banks, performing functions similar to those the OCC performs for national banks. The choice of national versus state charter lies at the foundation of the dual banking system in the United States. In December 2021, 747 banks were nationally chartered and 3,484 were state chartered, with approximately 66% and 34% of total commercial bank assets, respectively.

Analogy

The U.S. banking regulatory system is like a house with multiple security systems: the FDIC is the fire alarm (deterring runs), the OCC is the building inspector (chartering and examining), the Federal Reserve is the utility company (providing liquidity and overseeing holding companies), and state authorities are local zoning boards (regulating state-chartered banks). Each system serves a distinct purpose, but they must work together to ensure the safety of the entire structure.

Key Takeaways

  • U.S. banks may be regulated by up to four entities: FDIC, OCC, Federal Reserve, and state regulators.
  • FDIC insurance deters contagious runs by guaranteeing deposits.
  • The dual banking system allows banks to choose between national and state charters.
  • The Federal Reserve regulates bank holding companies to control risk at the parent level.

2018 Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act Relaxed mortgage lending rules for some institutions; exempted banks under $10B from Volcker Rule; raised enhanced prudential regulation threshold from $50B to $250B.

The evolution of banking regulation reflects a cycle of restriction and deregulation. The 1927 McFadden Act restricted interstate branching, while the 1933 Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial banking from investment banking. The 1980 DIDMCA and 1982 DIA were deregulatory, eliminating interest ceilings and expanding bank powers. The 1989 FIRREA and 1991 FDICIA responded to the thrift crisis and bank failures by pulling back from deregulation. The 1994 Riegle-Neal Act and 1999 FSMA reversed long-standing restrictions on geographic expansion and product diversification. The 2010 Wall Street Reform Act responded to the financial crisis by re-regulating many aspects of banking, and the 2018 Act provided regulatory relief for smaller institutions.

Key Insight

Banking regulation in the U.S. follows a crisis-response pattern: major financial crises (the Great Depression, the S&L crisis, the 2007–2009 financial crisis) trigger significant regulatory reforms, which are then partially relaxed over time as the memory of the crisis fades and industry lobbying intensifies.

Key Takeaways

  • Glass-Steagall (1933) separated commercial and investment banking; FSMA (1999) repealed this separation.
  • McFadden Act (1927) restricted interstate branching; Riegle-Neal (1994) enabled nationwide branching.
  • FDICIA (1991) introduced prompt corrective action and risk-based deposit insurance premiums.
  • Wall Street Reform Act (2010) created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and systemic risk oversight.
  • Economic Growth Act (2018) provided regulatory relief for community banks (under $10B in assets).

7. Commercial Banks: Industry Performance

7.1 Key Performance Metrics

The two primary measures of bank profitability are return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE).

Return on Assets (ROA)
\[ROA = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Assets}}\]

ROA measures the earnings generated per dollar of assets. It reflects the efficiency with which a bank uses its asset base to generate profits. Y = Net Income, X = Total Assets. The ratio shows how many cents of profit the bank earns for each dollar of assets. A higher ROA indicates more efficient asset utilization. This metric is used to compare profitability across banks of different sizes and over time.

Return on Equity (ROE)
\[ROE = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Total Equity}}\]

ROE measures the return to the bank's common stockholders per dollar of equity invested. Y = Net Income, X = Total Equity. ROE reflects the return on shareholders' investment and is the key metric for equity investors. Because large banks typically operate with less equity per dollar of assets (higher leverage), ROE is generally larger for this group even when ROA is similar or lower.

DuPont Decomposition of ROE
\[ROE = ROA \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}\]

DuPont analysis decomposes ROE into two components: ROA (profitability per dollar of assets) and the equity multiplier (assets/equity, a measure of leverage). Y = ROE, X1 = ROA, X2 = Assets/Equity. This decomposition allows analysts to identify whether a bank's ROE is driven by profitability (ROA) or leverage (equity multiplier). A bank with high ROE but low ROA is relying on high leverage, which amplifies both returns and risk. This is used in practice to assess the sources of bank profitability and to compare banks with different capital structures.

Key Insight

ROE can be decomposed into ROA and leverage. A bank with high ROE but low ROA is using high leverage to boost returns, which also amplifies downside risk. The DuPont decomposition reveals whether profitability comes from operational efficiency (ROA) or financial leverage (equity multiplier).

Spread
\[\text{Spread} = \text{Lending Rate} - \text{Deposit Rate}\]

Spread is the difference between the rate a bank earns on loans (lending rate) and the rate it pays on deposits (deposit rate). Y = Spread, X1 = Lending Rate, X2 = Deposit Rate. The spread reflects the bank's core profitability from intermediation. Larger banks tend to have narrower spreads because they face more competition in national markets, while smaller banks in localized markets often benefit from less competition and wider spreads. However, large banks compensate by generating fee income from OBS activities.

Key Takeaways

  • ROA = Net Income / Total Assets (profitability per dollar of assets).
  • ROE = Net Income / Total Equity (return to shareholders).
  • DuPont decomposition: ROE = ROA × (Assets/Equity).
  • Spread = Lending Rate − Deposit Rate (core intermediation profitability).

The performance data reveal several important patterns. In the early 1990s, the largest banks ($10B+) had the lowest ROA (0.38% in 1990) but their ROE (6.68%) was closer to the industry average due to higher leverage. During the 1995–2006 period, bank performance improved significantly, with ROA peaking at 1.40% in 2003 and ROE at 15.34% in 2003 for all banks.

The financial crisis of 2007–2009 caused severe deterioration. In 2008, ROA for all banks fell to 0.16% and ROE to 1.62%. The smallest banks (under $100M) had a slightly positive ROA of 0.36% in 2008, while mid-sized banks ($1B–$10B) experienced negative ROA (−0.10%) and ROE (−0.90%). In 2009, the smallest banks' ROA fell to 0.06% and mid-sized banks' ROA to −0.35%.

The recovery was uneven. The biggest banks ($10B+) returned to positive ROA (0.15%) and ROE (1.44%) in 2009, while smaller banks' ROA and ROE remained negative until 2010. This faster recovery for large banks is attributable to greater government assistance and monitoring. By 2021, the industry had largely recovered, with all-bank ROA at 1.24% and ROE at 12.27%.

Key Insight

Large banks recovered faster from the 2007–2009 crisis than small banks due to greater government assistance, stress testing, and capital injections. This pattern reverses the historical advantage that small banks held in terms of ROA stability during economic downturns.
