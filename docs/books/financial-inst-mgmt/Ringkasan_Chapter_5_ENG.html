1. Introduction: Mutual Funds and Hedge Funds

Mutual funds and hedge funds are financial institutions that pool the financial resources of individuals and companies and invest in diversified portfolios of assets. The core purpose of these pooled investment vehicles is to provide investors—especially small investors—with access to professionally managed, diversified portfolios that would be difficult or costly to construct individually.

Open-ended mutual fund (the major type) continuously stands ready to sell new shares to investors and redeem outstanding shares on demand at fair market value. This liquidity feature distinguishes them from many other investment vehicles. Mutual funds generate economies of scale by incurring lower transaction costs and commissions than individual investors would face buying securities directly.

The mutual fund industry boomed in the 1990s due to rising equity markets (e.g., S&P 500 returns >25% in 1997 and 1998) and the low-cost opportunity funds provided. The early 2000s slowdown and the 2008–2009 financial crisis caused a $2.4 trillion (20%) drop in industry assets in 2008. Despite this, by 2020 more than 7,600 mutual fund companies held total assets of $23.90 trillion.

Hedge funds are a type of investment pool that solicit funds from wealthy individuals and other investors (e.g., commercial banks) and invest on their behalf. Similar to mutual funds in pooling, but investments in hedge funds are restricted to more wealthy clients. Prior to 2010, hedge funds were generally unregulated; after prominent frauds and failures (e.g., Madoff, Bear Stearns funds), regulations now require hedge funds above a threshold to register with the SEC under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and report financial information to assess systemic risk.

Analogy

Think of a mutual fund as a community garden: individuals contribute seeds (money), professional gardeners (fund managers) plant a diverse crop, and everyone shares the harvest. A hedge fund is like a private club garden with high entry fees and fewer, but wealthier, participants.

Key Insight

Mutual funds democratize investing through diversification and professional management, but they also expose investors to market risk and fund-manager risk. Hedge funds serve a different clientele and historically operated with less regulatory oversight.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual funds and hedge funds pool investor money into diversified portfolios.
  • Open-end mutual funds stand ready to issue and redeem shares at NAV.
  • Hedge funds are restricted to wealthy investors and now must register with the SEC.
  • The mutual fund industry is the largest FI group by assets, surpassing commercial banking.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Mutual Fund Industry

2.1 Historical Trends

The first mutual fund was founded in Boston in 1924. Growth was slow at first; by 1970 there were 361 funds with about $50 billion in assets. Key growth drivers included the advent of money market mutual funds in 1972, tax-exempt money market funds in 1979, and the explosion of special-purpose equity, bond, emerging market, and derivative funds in the 1990s.

Table 5–1 documents the growth from 1940 to 2020. Total net assets grew from $0.45 billion in 1940 to $11,999.71 billion in 2007, fell to $9,620.27 billion in 2008, then recovered to $23,895.84 billion in 2020. The number of funds increased from 68 in 1940 to a peak of 8,154 in 2000, then declined to 7,636 in 2020. The financial crisis caused a sharp drop in equity fund outflows and record inflows to U.S. government money market funds.

In 2008, total mutual fund assets fell by $2.4 trillion (20%). Equity funds suffered substantial outflows, while U.S. government money market funds saw record inflows. This illustrates flight-to-quality behavior during financial crises.

Analogy

The mutual fund industry's growth is like a mall that expands with new stores (fund types) and attracts more shoppers (investors). The financial crisis acted like a sudden fire drill, causing many to rush to safe exits (government money funds).

Key Insight

Industry asset size is highly sensitive to market returns and investor sentiment. The 2008 drop was due both to investment losses and investor redemptions, showing how leverage and liquidity risk interact.

2.2 Types of Mutual Funds

The mutual fund industry is divided into short-term funds and long-term funds.

  • Equity funds: common and preferred stock securities. They are usually well diversified; risk is mostly systematic (market-based).
  • Bond funds: fixed-income securities with maturity over one year. They carry extensive interest rate risk.
  • Hybrid funds: both bonds and stocks.
  • Money market mutual funds (MMMFs): various money market securities. Their principal risk is interest rate risk, mitigated by short maturities (often <60 days); they have virtually no liquidity or default risk.

2.3 Money Market Mutual Funds: Special Features

MMMFs provide an alternative to interest-bearing bank deposits. They generally offer higher returns because they are not subject to bank regulatory costs (reserve requirements, capital requirements, deposit insurance premiums) but they are not FDIC-insured.

The financial crisis exposed MMMF vulnerability. In September 2008, Reserve Primary Fund "broke the buck" (NAV fell below $1) due to Lehman Brothers commercial paper holdings. This triggered a run on MMMFs with over $200 billion outflows in days. The U.S. Treasury temporarily guaranteed MMMF accounts (up to $50 billion) to restore confidence.

Analogy

MMMFs were like a bank that always promised to return $1 for every dollar deposited. When one fund couldn't, it was like a broken promise that caused a panic among all similar institutions.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual funds are classified into short-term (MMMFs) and long-term (equity, bond, hybrid).
  • MMMFs are not FDIC-insured but historically were very safe; exceptional events can break the buck.
  • Market conditions drive the shift between long-term and short-term funds.

3. Investor Returns and Net Asset Value

3.1 Sources of Investor Return

An investor's return from mutual fund shares reflects three aspects of the underlying portfolio:

  1. Income and dividends earned on assets.
  2. Capital gains when the fund sells assets at a profit.
  3. Capital appreciation in the market value of assets held.

Assets are marked-to-market daily, meaning fund managers calculate the current value of each share by computing the daily market value of the fund's total assets and dividing by the number of shares outstanding. The resulting value is the Net Asset Value (NAV).

Net Asset Value (NAV)
\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Market value of fund assets}}{\text{Number of shares outstanding}} \]

Symbols: Market value of fund assets is the sum of current market prices of all securities held; shares outstanding is the number of mutual fund shares held by investors. NAV is the price at which investors buy or sell shares on that day.

The NAV changes daily with changes in asset values (capital appreciation) and with the number of shares outstanding (due to purchases/redemptions). In practice, NAV is used to execute purchase and redemption orders.

Example (Source Example 5–1: Impact of Capital Appreciation on NAV)

A fund holds 4,000 Macy's ($22.00), 1,200 Exxon/Mobil ($77.00), 1,500 AT&T ($33.75), with 15,000 shares outstanding. Initial NAV = [(4,000×$22) + (1,200×$77) + (1,500×$33.75)] / 15,000 = $15.402. If prices rise to $26.50, $78, $35 respectively, new NAV = $16.807. Interpretation: NAV rose solely due to capital appreciation; number of shares unchanged. Lesson: Capital appreciation directly boosts share value.

Example (Source Example 5–2: Impact of Investment Size on NAV)

If 1,000 new investors buy at $15.402, the fund receives $15,402, which buys 700 additional Macy's shares. New portfolio: 4,700 Macy's, 1,200 Exxon, 1,500 AT&T. At end-of-month prices, NAV = [(4,700×$26.50) + (1,200×$78) + (1,500×$35)] / 16,000 = $16.916. Interpretation: The increase in shares outstanding dilutes the effect of appreciation? Actually the fund gained slightly higher NAV because it bought more Macy's shares that appreciated. This shows that new investments change the portfolio composition and affect NAV.

3.2 Open-End vs. Closed-End Funds

Most mutual funds are open-end: the number of shares fluctuates daily with purchases and redemptions. Investors buy and sell directly with the fund. NAV is determined solely by the market value of underlying securities.

Closed-end investment companies have a fixed number of shares outstanding. Investors buy and sell shares on stock exchanges, like corporate stock. Since the supply of shares is fixed, the market price is determined by both the NAV and the demand for the shares themselves. If the price is above NAV, the fund trades at a premium; if below, at a discount.

Closed-End Fund Premium/Discount
\[ \text{Premium (Discount)} = \text{Market Price per Share} - \text{NAV per Share} \]

Symbols: Market Price per Share is the current trading price; NAV per Share is the underlying asset value. A positive value indicates a premium; negative indicates a discount. The source also demonstrates with aggregate market value of shares versus portfolio market value.

Example (Source Example 5–3: Market Value of Closed-End Mutual Fund Shares)

Fund A: NAV per share = $800/50 = $16, but shares trade at $20, so premium = $4 per share (or aggregate premium $200). Fund B: NAV per share = $3,000/100 = $30, but shares trade at $25, so discount = $5 per share (aggregate discount $500). Interpretation: Closed-end fund prices are driven by demand in addition to fundamental value.

Analogy

Open-end funds are like a bakery that bakes bread only when customers order; closed-end funds are like a fixed batch of bread that shoppers trade among themselves at whatever price the market sets.

3.3 Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

ETFs are investment companies with a fixed number of shares (like closed-end) but trade intraday on exchanges at market-determined prices. ETFs offer a proportionate share in a pool of assets. Unlike traditional mutual funds, ETF shares are created and redeemed by Authorized Participants (APs)—large financial institutions that deposit a block of securities with the ETF in exchange for ETF shares. Retail investors can only buy/sell ETFs on exchanges, not directly with the fund.

ETFs are typically index funds replicating a specific index (e.g., SPDRs, VIPERs). They offer intraday trading, can be bought on margin, sold short, and defer capital gains until sale. The creation/redemption mechanism keeps ETF prices close to NAV.

Key Takeaways

  • NAV = market value of assets / shares outstanding; determines buy/sell price for open-end funds.
  • Open-end funds adjust share count daily; closed-end funds have fixed shares and can trade at premiums/discounts.
  • ETFs trade intraday and are created/redeemed via authorized participants, keeping prices aligned with NAV.

4. Mutual Fund Costs and Share Classes

4.1 Load vs. No-Load Funds

A load fund charges an upfront sales commission (sometimes as high as 5.75%), compensating sales agents. A no-load fund has no upfront commission. Some funds impose a back-end load (deferred sales charge) when shares are sold.

The argument for load funds is more personal attention, but data show that after adjusting for loads, many funds underperform. By 2020, no-load funds represented 85% of long-term mutual fund assets, reflecting investor awareness of cost disadvantage.

Annualized Sales Load
\[ \text{Annualized Load} = \frac{\text{Load Percentage}}{\text{Investment Horizon (Years)}} \]

Because the load is a one-time charge, it is converted to an annualized figure to compare with annual fees. If the horizon is long, the annualized load is small; if short, it can be significant.

4.2 Fund Operating Expenses and 12b–1 Fees

Annual fees cover fund operating expenses: management fees (administration, shareholder services) and 12b–1 fees (marketing and distribution). 12b–1 fees cannot exceed 1% per year, with marketing/service capped at 0.25% and management at 0.75%. These fees are deducted from fund assets, so investors indirectly bear them.

Total Shareholder Cost
\[ \text{Total Annual Cost} = \text{Annualized Sales Load} + \text{Annual Expense Ratio (incl. 12b–1)} \]

Example (Source Example 5–4: Calculation of Mutual Fund Costs)

Suppose an investor buys a fund with a 4% front-end load and expects to hold for 10 years. Annualized load = 4%/10 = 0.4% per year. If the fund expense ratio (including 12b–1) is 1% per year, total annual cost = 0.4% + 1% = 1.4% per year. Interpretation: The investor's return is reduced by 1.4% annually from costs.

4.3 Share Classes

Funds often offer multiple share classes (A, B, C, and institutional) investing in the same portfolio but with different fee structures.

  • Class A: front-end load + annual management/12b–1 fees (typically 25–35 basis points).
  • Class B: no front-end load, but higher annual fees (usually 1%) and a back-end load that declines over time; often converts to Class A after 6–8 years.
  • Class C: no front-end load, annual fees of 1%, back-end load only in first year (1%), no conversion.
  • Institutional shares (e.g., I, R, N, X, Y): low fees, high minimums (often $1 million+, but 401(k) pooled accounts can qualify).

Example

Vanguard offers three share classes for some funds: Investor shares require $1,000–$3,000 and have average expense ratio 0.18%; Admiral shares require $3,000–$100,000 and have 0.11%; Institutional shares require $5 million and have 0.05%. Lesson: Higher minimums buy lower costs, improving net returns.

Key Insight

Lower expense ratios typically lead to higher net returns. In the 2000s, misuse of 12b–1 fees and share-class disclosure led to regulatory changes and fee reductions; average fees have declined significantly.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual fund costs include sales loads (front/back) and annual expenses (management + 12b–1).
  • Total shareholder cost = annualized load + annual expense ratio.
  • Share classes offer different trade-offs between upfront fees and annual fees.
  • Institutional shares have lowest costs and best returns due to high minimums.

5. Balance Sheet and Recent Trends

5.1 Money Market Mutual Fund Assets

Table 5–8 shows the distribution of MMMF assets from 1990 to 2021. In 2021, 89.9% of assets were in short-term securities such as repurchase agreements, Treasury securities, commercial paper, and deposits. During the 2008 crisis, MMMFs shifted dramatically into U.S. government securities (from 13.6% in 2007 to 35.5% in 2008) to reduce risk.

MMMF shares are typically fixed at $1. Interest rate changes are adjusted by increasing or reducing the number of $1 shares an investor owns. Example 5–5 illustrates: if assets rise from $100 to $110, the number of shares increases to 110.

Money Market Fund Share Adjustment
\[ \text{Number of Shares} = \text{Market Value of Assets} \div \$1.00 \]

Symbols: Market value of assets is the mark-to-market value; $1.00 represents the fixed par value per share. This mechanism keeps NAV at $1, with gains/losses reflected in share count.

Example (Source Example 5–5)

Before interest rate drop, assets = $100, shares = 100. After drop, assets = $110, shares = 110. Interpretation: Interest rate changes alter the number of shares held, but the per-share value remains $1.

5.2 Long-Term Fund Assets

Table 5–9 shows long-term funds' assets. In 2007, equities were over 70% of long-term fund assets; by 2008, equities fell to 55.5% as the crisis hit. Total financial assets dropped 30.6% in 2008. By 2021, equities recovered to 69.5% of total.

Key Insight

Long-term funds bear substantial market risk. The 2008 experience demonstrates how concentrated equity exposure can cause massive asset declines.

Key Takeaways

  • MMMFs invest primarily in short-term debt; during crises, they shift to Treasury securities.
  • Long-term funds' asset composition varies with market conditions; equity exposure dropped sharply in 2008.
  • In 2020, COVID-19 caused huge inflows to government MMMFs as liquidity demand surged.

6. Regulation of Mutual Funds

Mutual funds are heavily regulated because they manage small investors' savings. The SEC is the primary regulator.

  • Securities Act of 1933: requires registration and prospectus.
  • Securities Exchange Act of 1934: antifraud provisions; NASD supervises distribution.
  • Investment Advisers Act of 1940: regulates advisors.
  • Investment Company Act of 1940: prevents conflicts of interest, fraud, excessive fees.
  • Insider Trading and Securities Fraud Enforcement Act of 1988: mechanisms to avoid insider trading.
  • Market Reform Act of 1990: allows SEC to introduce circuit breakers.
  • National Securities Markets Improvement Act (NSMIA) of 1996: reduces state regulatory burden.
  • Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002: audit committee requirements.

6.1 Abuses and Reforms

Allegations in the early 2000s revealed four categories of abuses:

  1. Market timing: short-term trading exploiting stale prices, especially in international funds.
  2. Late trading: buying/selling after 4 PM at that day's price.
  3. Directed brokerage: fund companies directing trades to brokers that promote their funds.
  4. Improper fee assessment: 12b–1 fees misrepresented, making no-load funds less transparent.

Reforms (2004–2005) required independent boards, chief compliance officers, "hard close" at 4 PM, expanded disclosure of market timing risks, and fair value pricing. The SEC also required portfolio managers to report trading in funds they manage.

Example

In 2004, Bank of America and FleetBoston agreed to a $675 million settlement for improper mutual fund trading practices. They paid $125 million and $70 million in fines respectively, plus restitution and fee reductions.

Key Insight

Regulation aims to align fund manager behavior with investor interests. But loopholes existed; ongoing vigilance is needed.

Key Takeaways

  • Mutual funds operate under extensive federal regulation.
  • Abuses: market timing, late trading, directed brokerage, improper fees.
  • Reforms strengthened governance, transparency, and compliance.

7. Hedge Funds

Hedge funds are pooled investment vehicles that solicit funds from wealthy individuals and institutions. They are similar to mutual funds in pooling, but restrict investors to wealthy clients. Historically lightly regulated, they faced increased oversight after 2010.

Key difference: hedge funds often use leverage, derivatives, and aggressive strategies. They are not required to register with the SEC unless above a threshold. Fund advisors must report financial information to assess systemic risk.

Analogy

If mutual funds are like public buses (anyone can ride, fixed routes), hedge funds are like private jets (only the wealthy, flexible routes, higher risk).

Key Insight

Hedge funds' lack of regulation pre-2010 allowed fraud and systemic risk. Registration requirements are designed to increase transparency without stifling innovation.

Key Takeaways

  • Hedge funds target wealthy investors and face less regulation.
  • Post-2010 regulations require SEC registration above asset thresholds.
  • Transparency and reporting are meant to mitigate systemic risk.

8. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

The following cautions summarize potential pitfalls when interpreting mutual fund data and investing in funds:

  • NAV is not the same as market price for closed-end funds. Closed-end funds can trade at premiums or discounts to NAV, so NAV alone does not determine market value.
  • Expense-free does not mean risk-free. Some funds advertise zero expense ratios but still carry management fees, trading costs, and market risk.
  • Past performance does not guarantee future results. Many active funds underperform index funds; high fees erode returns.
  • Loads matter. A 5.75% front-end load reduces invested capital; annualizing over a short horizon reveals true cost.
  • Do not confuse error and residual in fund performance attribution. (This is more generic but relevant in quantitative analysis.)
  • Omitted-variable problems: Comparing fund returns without adjusting for risk (e.g., beta) can mislead. The chapter emphasizes that equity funds have systematic risk; sector funds have both systematic and unsystematic risk.
  • Market timing abuses indicate that stale prices can be exploited; investors should be aware of fair value pricing.

Example

In Table 5–6, the American Funds group charged a 5.75% load. Their 1-year returns ranged from −9.7% to 15.4% before load; after adjusting for load, the range fell to −15.4% to 9.6%. This shows the significant impact of fees on net returns.

Key Insight

Always evaluate mutual fund costs and risk relative to index benchmarks. A fund's gross return does not reflect investor net return; fees and expenses do.

Key Takeaways

  • Understand the difference between NAV and market price.
  • Costs (loads, expense ratios) directly reduce investor returns.
  • Risk (systematic vs. unsystematic) must be considered when evaluating fund performance.
  • Regulation improves transparency but does not eliminate the need for investor vigilance.
