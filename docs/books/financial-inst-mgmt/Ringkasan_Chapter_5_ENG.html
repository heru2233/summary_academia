Chapter 5 Summary

Financial Services: Mutual Fund and Hedge Fund Companies

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 5. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

Mutual funds and hedge funds are financial institutions that pool the financial resources of individuals and companies and invest in diversified portfolios of assets. An open-end mutual fund (the major type) continuously stands ready to sell new shares to investors and to redeem outstanding shares on demand at their fair market value. Thus, these funds provide opportunities for small investors to invest in financial securities and diversify risk, while generating greater economies of scale by incurring lower transaction costs and commissions than individual investors buying securities directly.

The mutual fund industry boomed in the 1990s as the market value of financial assets rose, for example the S&P 500 index returned more than 25 percent in 1997 and 1998. The severe financial crisis of 2008 to 2009 resulted in the largest ever drop in the value of industry assets, with losses on investments and liquidation of shares by investors causing a drop of $2.4 trillion (or 20 percent). Despite these issues, in 2020 more than 7,600 different mutual fund companies held total assets of $23.90 trillion.

Hedge funds are a type of investment pool that solicit funds from (wealthy) individuals and other investors, for example commercial banks, and invest these funds on their behalf. They are similar to mutual funds in that they are pooled investment vehicles, but investments in hedge funds are restricted to wealthier clients and, prior to 2010, they were generally unregulated. As a result of highly publicized hedge fund failures and near failures (the result of fraud by fund managers, for example Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities, and the financial crisis, for example Bear Stearns High Grade Structured Credit Strategies Fund), in 2010 federal regulators increased oversight, requiring hedge funds with assets under management exceeding some threshold to register with the SEC under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940.

2. Size, Structure, and Composition of the Mutual Fund Industry

2.1 Historical Trends

The first mutual fund was founded in Boston in 1924. The industry grew slowly at first, with 361 funds holding about $50 billion in assets by 1970. Rapid growth followed the advent of money market mutual funds (MMMFs) in 1972, tax-exempt MMMFs first established in 1979, and an explosion of special-purpose equity, bond, emerging market, and derivative funds in the 1990s. Total assets invested in mutual funds grew from $0.45 billion in 1940 to $11,999.71 billion in 2007 (of which $8,913.94 billion was invested in long-term funds), and the number of funds increased from 68 in 1940 to 8,040 in 2007.

The financial crisis produced a sharp drop in mutual fund activity. At the end of 2008, total assets fell to $9,620.27 billion, with equity funds suffering substantial outflows while inflows to U.S. government money market funds reached record highs. As the economy recovered starting in 2009, assets grew to $11,111.16 billion by year-end and to $23,895.84 billion in 2020 (well surpassing 2007 levels), of which $19,562.56 billion was invested in long-term funds.

Growth has also been the result of the rise in retirement funds under management by mutual funds. The retirement fund market increased from about $4 trillion in 1990 to $34.9 trillion in 2020, and mutual funds manage approximately 70 percent of this market. About 80 percent of all retirement plan investments are in institutional funds, which manage retirement plans for an institution's employees, with very low costs because there are no additional distribution fees and the retirement plan can use its bargaining power to get the best deals.

In terms of asset size, the mutual fund industry has surpassed the commercial banking industry, making mutual funds the most important FI group in the United States. Banks' share of all mutual fund assets managed was about 4 percent in 2020, and insurance companies managed 5 percent. Low barriers to entry have kept the industry from being increasingly concentrated, but the largest 25 sponsors managed 81 percent of industry assets in 2020, compared to 25 percent in 1990.

2.2 Different Types of Mutual Funds

The mutual fund industry is usually divided into two sectors, short-term funds and long-term funds. Long-term funds include equity funds (comprised of common and preferred stock securities), bond funds (comprised of fixed-income securities with a maturity of longer than one year), and hybrid funds (comprised of both bond and stock securities). Short-term funds include taxable MMMFs and tax-exempt MMMFs.

Long-term equity funds typically are well diversified, and the risk is more systematic or market based. Bond funds have extensive interest rate risk because of their long-term, fixed-rate nature. Sector, or industry-specific, funds have systematic and unsystematic risk. The principal type of risk for short-term funds is interest rate risk, because of the predominance of fixed-income securities, but because of the shortness of maturity of the assets (often less than 60 days) this risk is mitigated to a large extent, and short-term funds generally have virtually no liquidity or default risk.

By 2020, the share of long-term equity and bond funds was 81.9 percent of all funds, while money market funds decreased to 18.1 percent. The 2008 to 2009 financial crisis temporarily reversed this trend, with the share of long-term funds plunging to 60.2 percent and money market funds rising to 39.8 percent at the end of 2008, partly because the U.S. Treasury extended government insurance to all money market mutual fund accounts on a temporary basis.

MMMFs provide an alternative investment to interest-bearing deposits at commercial banks. The major difference between the two is that interest-bearing deposits (below $250,000 in size) are fully insured by the FDIC, but due to bank regulatory costs (reserve requirements, capital requirements, and deposit insurance premiums), interest-bearing deposits generally offer lower returns than noninsured MMMFs. Thus, the net gain in switching to MMMFs is higher returns in exchange for the loss of deposit insurance coverage.

An exception occurred during the financial crisis. In September 2008, Reserve Primary Fund, a large and reputedly conservative money market fund, had holdings of $785 million in commercial paper issued by Lehman Brothers. As a result of Lehman's failure, shares in Reserve Primary Fund broke the buck (fell below $1), the first ever incidence of a share price dipping below a dollar for any MMMF open to the general public, leading to a broad run that saw more than $200 billion flow out within a few days. The U.S. Treasury stopped the run by extending government insurance to all MMMF accounts held in participating funds as of the close of business on September 19, 2008, with coverage lasting one year.

2.3 Household Ownership

Household ownership of mutual funds has remained relatively steady since 2000. As of 2020, 58.7 million (45.7 percent) of U.S. households owned mutual funds. Ownership is concentrated among individuals in their prime saving and investing years, with 62 percent of households owning mutual funds headed by Baby Boomers and Generation X. The typical fund-owning household had $126,700 invested in a median number of four mutual funds. Compared to 1995, 2020 saw an increase in the median age of holders (from 44 to 50 years) and large increases in median household financial assets (from $50,000 to $300,000) and median mutual fund assets owned (from $18,000 to $126,700), while holdings of equity funds increased from 73 to 90 percent of all households.

3. Mutual Fund Objectives

Regulations require that mutual fund managers specify the investment objectives of their funds in a prospectus available to potential investors, including a list of the securities the fund holds. In 1998, the SEC adopted a new procedure in which key sections of all funds' prospectuses must be written in plain English instead of legal boilerplate, to increase investors' ability to understand the risks related to the investment objectives or profile of a fund. In the same year, the SEC adopted an initiative requiring mutual funds to disclose more information about their return risk as well as the returns themselves, enabling investors to compare return-risk trade-offs across funds.

A growing number of long-term mutual funds are index funds, in which fund managers buy securities in proportions similar to those included in a specified major stock index (such as the S&P 500). In 2020, 490 index funds managed total net assets of $4.8 trillion, compared to 6,806 active funds with assets of $14.8 trillion. Because little research or aggressive management is necessary for index funds, management fees are lower and returns are often higher. Morningstar data over 2011 to 2021 found that actively managed funds generally underperformed index funds across nearly all asset classes examined, with only 26 percent of all active funds having a 10-year success rate above their index fund counterparts. The cheapest funds succeeded about twice as often as the priciest ones (a 35 percent success rate versus 18 percent), reflecting both cost advantages and differences in survival (68 percent of the cheapest funds survived, versus 54 percent of the most expensive).

4. Investor Returns from Mutual Fund Ownership

The return an investor receives from investing in mutual fund shares reflects three aspects of the underlying portfolio, (1) income and dividends earned on the assets, (2) capital gains when assets are sold at prices higher than the purchase price, and (3) capital appreciation in the underlying values of the assets held. With respect to capital appreciation, mutual fund assets are normally marked-to-market daily, meaning managers calculate the current value of each share by computing the daily market value of the fund's total asset portfolio and dividing it by the number of shares outstanding. The resulting value is called the net asset value (NAV) of the fund, which is the price the investor gets when selling shares back to the fund that day or buying any new shares.

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Market value of total portfolio}}{\text{Shares outstanding}} \]

Example 5-1. Impact of Capital Appreciation on NAV

Suppose a mutual fund contains 4,000 shares of Macy's Inc. currently trading at $22.00, 1,200 shares of Exxon/Mobil currently trading at $77.00, and 1,500 shares of AT&T currently trading at $33.75. The fund has 15,000 shares outstanding. Today, the NAV is calculated as

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{(4{,}000 \times \$22.00) + (1{,}200 \times \$77.00) + (1{,}500 \times \$33.75)}{15{,}000} = \$15.402 \]

If next month Macy's shares increase to $26.50, Exxon/Mobil shares to $78, and AT&T shares to $35, the NAV (assuming the same number of shares outstanding) would increase to

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{(4{,}000 \times \$26.50) + (1{,}200 \times \$78) + (1{,}500 \times \$35)}{15{,}000} = \$16.807 \]

Example 5-2. Impact of Investment Size on NAV

Consider the fund in Example 5-1, but suppose today 1,000 additional investors buy into the fund at the current NAV of $15.402, giving the manager $15,402 in additional funds. If the manager uses these funds to buy additional shares in Macy's, at the market price of $22.00 he or she can buy $15,402 divided by $22.00, or 700 additional shares. The new portfolio would be 4,700 shares of Macy's, 1,200 of Exxon/Mobil, and 1,500 of AT&T, with 16,000 shares outstanding. At the end of the month, the NAV would be

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{(4{,}700 \times \$26.50) + (1{,}200 \times \$78) + (1{,}500 \times \$35)}{16{,}000} = \$16.916 \]

A comparison with Example 5-1 indicates that the additional shares alone enabled the fund to gain a slightly higher NAV than had the number of shares remained static ($16.916 versus $16.807).

4.1 Closed-End Funds and Premium or Discount

Open-end mutual funds can be compared to closed-end investment companies, which have a fixed number of shares outstanding at any given time. With closed-end funds, investors must buy and sell the company's shares on a stock exchange similar to corporate stock. Since the number of shares available is fixed, the NAV of the shares is determined not only by the value of the underlying shares but also by the demand for the investment company's shares themselves. When demand is high, the shares can trade at a premium (more than the fair market value of the securities held); when the value is less than the NAV of its assets, the shares are said to trade at a discount. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) are closed-end investment companies that specialize in investing in mortgages, property, or real estate company shares.

Example 5-3. Market Value of Closed-End Mutual Fund Shares

Because of high demand, the 50 shares of a closed-end investment company are trading at $20 per share. The market value of the equity-type securities in the fund's asset portfolio, however, is $800, or $16 per share. The market value balance sheet is as follows.

Assets Liabilities and Equity
Market value of asset portfolio, $800 Market value of closed-end fund shares (P × N, 50 × $20), $1,000
Premium, $200

The fund's shares are trading at a premium of $4 per share ($200 divided by 50). Conversely, because of low demand for a second closed-end fund, its 100 shares outstanding are trading at $25 per share while the market value of the portfolio is $3,000, or a NAV of $30 per share, so its shares trade at a discount of $5 per share ($500 divided by 100).

\[ \text{Premium or discount per share} = \frac{\text{Total premium or discount}}{N} \]

4.2 Exchange-Traded Funds

Similar to closed-end funds in that a fixed number of shares are outstanding at any point in time, an exchange-traded fund (ETF) is an investment company with shares that trade intraday on stock exchanges at market-determined prices. Like a mutual fund, an ETF offers investors a proportionate share in a pool of stocks, bonds, and other assets, and ETFs may be bought or sold through a broker like shares of any publicly traded company. ETF shares are created by an institutional investor's depositing a specified block of securities with the ETF, receiving in return a fixed amount of ETF shares, some or all of which may then be sold on an exchange. Retail investors cannot purchase or redeem shares directly from the ETF as with a traditional mutual fund.

Most ETFs are long-term mutual funds designed to replicate a particular stock market index, such as Standard & Poor's Depository Receipts (SPDRs) and Vanguard Large-Cap Index Participation Equity Receipts (VIPERs). Unlike index funds, ETFs can be traded during the day, purchased on margin, and sold short. Because ETFs behave like stocks, investors are subject to capital gains taxes only when they sell their shares, so they can defer capital gains for as long as they hold the ETF. In 2020, assets invested in the 2,204 ETFs in existence totaled $5.45 trillion, up from $66 billion invested in a total of 80 funds in 2000.

The price at which an ETF trades closely tracks the market value of the securities held in the portfolio, one reason being the ability of authorized participants (APs) to create or redeem ETF shares at NAV at the end of each trading day. APs, typically large financial institutions, are the only investors allowed to interact directly with the fund and play a key role in the primary market for ETF shares. Creations and redemptions are processed through the National Securities Clearing Corporation (NSCC) with the same guarantee as a domestic stock trade. On average, daily aggregate ETF creations and redemptions are a fraction (10 percent) of total primary and secondary market activity, and account for less than 0.5 percent of the funds' total net assets.

The past several decades have seen a significant shift in assets from active to passive investment strategies. As of December 2020, passive funds accounted for 40 percent of combined U.S. mutual fund and ETF assets under management, up from 4 percent in 1996 and 15 percent in 2007. Contributing factors include the efficient markets hypothesis of the 1950s and 1960s, the introduction of the first stock index funds in the 1970s, the relatively lower costs of passive investing, evidence of underperformance of active managers, the growing popularity of ETFs, and greater regulatory focus on the fees of investment products.

5. Mutual Fund Costs

Mutual funds charge shareholders a price or fee for the services they provide, and the total cost to the shareholder is the sum of the annualized sales load and other fees charged. Two types of fees are incurred by investors, sales loads and fund operating expenses.

5.1 Load versus No-Load Funds

An investor who buys a mutual fund share may be subject to a sales charge, sometimes as high as 5.75 percent, in which case the fund is called a load fund. Funds that directly market shares to investors without commission-based sales agents are called no-load funds. The argument in favor of load funds is that their managers provide more personal attention and advice, but the cost of this increased attention may not be worthwhile. In 2003, load funds represented 43 percent of long-term mutual fund assets and no-load funds 57 percent, but at the end of 2020 no-load funds represented 85 percent. Because the load fee is a one-time charge, it must be converted to an annualized charge over the life of the investment, so the longer the investment horizon, the smaller the annualized load fee.

Many companies, particularly discount brokers, now offer mutual fund supermarkets with non-transaction fee programs, through which investors can buy and sell mutual fund shares from several sponsors through a single broker, with the broker generally paid from the fund's 12b-1 fees.

5.2 Fund Operating Expenses and 12b-1 Fees

In contrast to one-time upfront load charges, annual fees are charged to cover all fund-level expenses as a percent of fund assets. A management fee covers operating costs such as administration and shareholder services, while annual marketing and distribution fees are known as 12b-1 fees after the SEC rule covering such charges. These annual fees cannot exceed 1 percent of a fund's average net assets per year, with marketing and servicing costs capped at 0.25 percent and management fees at 0.75 percent per year, for a total maximum 12b-1 charge of 1 percent. Because these fees are paid out of the fund's assets, investors indirectly bear these expenses.

Example 5-4. Calculation of Mutual Fund Costs

Suppose an investor purchases fund shares with a 4 percent front-end load and expects to hold the shares for 10 years. The annualized sales load incurred by the investor is

\[ \text{Annualized load} = \frac{4\%}{10 \text{ years}} = 0.4\% \text{ per year} \]

If the fund has a total fund expense ratio (including 12b-1 fees) of 1 percent per year, the annual total shareholder cost is calculated as

\[ 0.4\% + 1\% = 1.4\% \text{ per year} \]

In 2020 there were 23,894 share classes offered by 7,636 mutual funds, representing 3 share classes per fund on average, compared to one share class per fund in 1980. Most funds sold in multiple classes offer three payment plans through share classes A, B, and C, each with different mixes of sales loads and management and 12b-1 fees. Class A shares carry a front-end load charged at the time of purchase, class B shares are offered at NAV without a front-end load but with a back-end load and typically convert to class A after six to eight years, and class C shares are offered at NAV with no front-end load, recovering distribution costs through annual management and 12b-1 fees of 1 percent and a back-end load of 1 percent only in the first year. Institutional share classes (such as I, R, N, X, and Y) are available to those with high net worth or institutional investors and usually carry the lowest fees. For example, Vanguard offers investor shares with an average expense ratio of 0.18 percent, Admiral shares of 0.11 percent, and institutional shares of 0.05 percent.

The average fees and expenses paid by mutual fund investors continue to fall. Investors paid 0.71 percent on average for actively managed equity mutual funds in 2020, down from 1.06 percent in 2000, and expense ratios for actively managed bond funds fell to 0.50 percent in 2020 from 0.78 percent in 2000. Scrutiny of incomplete disclosure and investors' inability to understand different fees emerged in the early 2000s, including a $675 million settlement by Bank of America and FleetBoston in 2004. While lower expense ratios are good, investors need to remember that expense-free does not mean risk-free, and management fees are not the only cost of owning a fund since trading and taxes can crimp returns.

6. Balance Sheet and Recent Trends for the Mutual Fund Industry

6.1 Money Market Funds

In 2021, $4,680.50 billion (89.9 percent of total assets) of MMMF assets was invested in short-term financial securities such as foreign deposits, domestic checkable deposits and currency, time and savings deposits, repurchase agreements (RPs, or repos), open market paper (mostly commercial paper), and U.S. government securities, up from 2007 when $2,094.4 billion (69.0 percent) was invested in short-term securities. As financial markets tumbled in 2008, MMMFs moved investments out of corporate and foreign bonds (12.4 percent of the total in 2007 and 6.1 percent in 2008) into safer securities such as U.S. government securities (13.6 percent in 2007 and 35.5 percent in 2008). Short maturity asset holdings reflect the objective of these funds to retain the deposit-like nature of their share liabilities, and most consumer-oriented MMMF shares have their values fixed at $1, with asset value fluctuations adjusted by increasing or reducing the number of $1 shares owned.

Example 5-5. Calculation of Number of Shares Outstanding in an MMMF

Due to a drop in interest rates, the market value of the assets held by a particular MMMF increases from $100 to $110. The market value balance sheet before the drop shows assets of $100 and equity of $100 (100 shares times $1), and after the drop assets of $110 and equity of $110 (110 shares times $1). The interest rate drop results in 10 (110 minus 100) new equity-type shares held by investors, reflecting the increase in the market value of the MMMF's assets of $10 (10 new shares of $1 each).

\[ \text{New shares} = \frac{\text{Increase in asset value}}{\$1 \text{ per share}} = \frac{\$10}{\$1} = 10 \text{ shares} \]

6.2 Long-Term Funds

The asset composition of long-term mutual funds reflects the popularity of different types of bond or equity funds at any point in time. In 2007, stocks composed over 70.0 percent of total long-term mutual fund asset portfolios, with credit market instruments at 28.1 percent. In 2008, when equity markets were plummeting, equities made up only 55.5 percent of long-term fund portfolios and credit market instruments 41.9 percent. Total financial assets fell from $7,829.0 billion in 2007 to just $5,435.3 billion in 2008, a drop of 30.6 percent. In 2021, long-term funds held financial assets totaling $21,208.70 billion, of which 69.5 percent were corporate equities, still not back to pre-crisis levels proportionally even ten years after the crisis began.

7. Regulation of Mutual Funds

Because mutual funds manage and invest small investors' savings, this industry is heavily regulated, with the SEC as the primary regulator. The Securities Act of 1933 requires a mutual fund to file a registration statement with the SEC and sets rules regarding the fund's prospectus. The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 makes the purchase and sale of mutual fund shares subject to various antifraud provisions, requires full and accurate information, and appointed the National Association of Securities Dealers (NASD) to supervise mutual fund share distributions. In 1940, Congress passed the Investment Advisers Act, regulating the activities of mutual fund advisors, and the Investment Company Act, setting out rules to prevent conflicts of interest, fraud, and excessive fees or charges for fund shares.

Other applicable laws include the Insider Trading and Securities Fraud Enforcement Act of 1988, the Market Reform Act of 1990 allowing the SEC to introduce circuit breakers after the 1987 stock market crash, and the National Securities Markets Improvement Act (NSMIA) of 1996, which exempts mutual fund sellers from oversight by state securities regulators, reducing their regulatory burden.

Despite many regulations, several allegations of trading abuses and improper assignment of fees were revealed and prosecuted in the early 2000s, falling into four general categories, market timing (short-term trading exploiting discrepancies between a fund's share price and out-of-date values of portfolio securities, common in international funds due to time zone differences), late trading (buying or selling shares long after the price was set at 4 PM eastern time), directed brokerage (arrangements between fund companies and brokerage houses improperly influencing which funds brokers recommended), and improper assessment of 12b-1 fees allowing some brokers to trick investors into believing they were buying no-load funds.

The result of these abuses was new rules in 2004 and 2005, including requirements that funds have an independent board headed by an independent chairman, and application of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 requiring audit committees to include at least one person familiar with GAAP and experienced in internal auditing controls. The SEC also closed a loophole allowing improper trading to go unnoticed by requiring portfolio managers to report trading in funds they manage, requiring a hard closing (all orders received by 4 PM when the daily price is calculated), expanding disclosure of the risks of frequent trading to address market timing, and requiring mutual funds to hire chief compliance officers starting October 5, 2004, who report directly to fund directors, not to executives of the fund management company.

In March 2009, the SEC adopted amendments requiring key information to appear in plain English in a standardized order at the front of the statutory prospectus, including a new option for a summary prospectus with the statutory prospectus provided on a website. After the financial crisis, the SEC adopted new regulations for money market funds under the Investment Company Act of 1940, tightening the risk-limiting conditions of Rule 2a-7. The 2010 amendments included no more than 5 percent of total assets in illiquid securities, a minimum of 10 percent in daily liquid securities, and a minimum of 30 percent in weekly liquid assets.

In July 2014, the SEC adopted further amendments to Rule 2a-7, with compliance required by October 16, 2016, including a floating NAV for institutional prime money market funds, and liquidity fees and redemption gates giving fund boards the ability to impose fees and gates during periods of stress. The amendments also include enhanced diversification, disclosure, and stress-testing requirements. The shift from prime to government funds was larger than originally expected, about $1 trillion, and the Federal Home Loan Banks played an important role in the market adjustment by buying government securities.

When the COVID-19 pandemic hit, the market experienced a dash for cash in March 2020, with prime and tax-exempt funds experiencing large outflows. The Federal Reserve established a range of facilities including the Money Market Fund Liquidity Facility (MMLF), easing pressures on short-term credit markets and reversing the outflows. In December 2021, the SEC proposed its third set of money market reforms, including raising daily and weekly liquid asset minimums to 25 percent and 50 percent (from 10 percent and 30 percent), preventing MMMFs from imposing limits on redemptions through fees and gates that exacerbated the run in March 2020, and requiring institutional prime and tax-exempt funds to implement swing pricing so that costs from shareholder redemptions are passed on to redeeming shareholders.

8. Global Issues in the Mutual Fund Industry

Mutual funds have been the fastest-growing sector in the U.S. financial institutions industry throughout the 1990s and into the 2000s, only curtailed by the worldwide financial crisis and the worst worldwide recession since the Great Depression. Combined assets invested in non-U.S. mutual funds are approximately 15 percent greater than those invested in U.S. funds. Investments in mutual funds worldwide (other than in the United States) increased more than 211 percent, from $4.545 trillion in 1999 to $14.130 trillion in 2007, compared with growth of 75 percent in U.S. funds. Non-U.S. funds also experienced bigger losses during the crisis, falling to $9.316 trillion (minus 34.1 percent) in 2008 while U.S. funds fell to $9.601 trillion (minus 20.1 percent). By 2020, worldwide (non-U.S.) investments increased to $33.71 trillion (an increase of 262 percent from 2008), while U.S. investments increased to $29.35 trillion (an increase of 206 percent).

The worldwide mutual fund market is most active in countries with the most sophisticated securities markets, such as Japan, France, Germany, Australia, and the United Kingdom. Barriers to entry overseas are typically higher than in the United States, and the U.S. mutual fund industry has worked to lower them, including achieving a true cross-border market in Europe and reducing barriers for U.S. sponsors seeking to offer products in China and other Asian countries.

9. Hedge Funds

Hedge funds are a type of investment pool that solicits funds from wealthy individuals and other investors and invests these funds on their behalf. Unlike mutual funds, hedge funds are not subject to the numerous regulations that apply to mutual funds for the protection of individuals, such as liquidity requirements, redeemability at any time, protection against conflicts of interest, fairness in pricing, disclosure, and limits on leverage, offering a high degree of privacy for their investors. Until 2010, hedge funds were not required to register with the SEC and were subject to virtually no regulatory oversight.

Historically, hedge funds avoided regulations by limiting the number of investors to fewer than 100 individuals who must be deemed accredited investors, with a net worth of more than $1 million or an annual income of at least $200,000 ($300,000 if married). Even with increased oversight after 2010, hedge funds that do not exceed $100 million in assets under management do not register with the SEC, so their data cannot be independently tracked and much data are self-reported. It is estimated that in June 2021 there were more than 8,200 hedge funds in the world, with managed assets estimated at $3.96 trillion, compared to $23.90 trillion in mutual fund assets and $5.45 trillion in ETF assets.

Hedge funds grew in popularity in the 1990s as investors saw returns of more than 40 percent after management fees (often more than 25 percent of the fund's profits). Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM) nearly collapsed in the late 1990s, and the potential impact of its failure was so great that the Federal Reserve felt it necessary to intervene by brokering a $3.6 billion bailout by a consortium of some of the world's largest financial institutions. LTCM's strategy, which used computer models detecting a price discrepancy between U.S. Treasury markets and other bonds, failed when in 1998 large drops in many foreign stock markets caused money to pour into U.S. Treasury markets, driving Treasury prices up and yields down, which pushed down the prices of many mortgage-backed securities and drained funds from European and high-yield corporate bond markets. With all positions going wrong, LTCM experienced huge losses, partly because it was two to four times more leveraged than the typical fund.

The failures of two Bear Stearns hedge funds (Bear Stearns High-Grade Structured Credit Fund and Enhanced Leveraged Fund) in the summer of 2007 also resulted from managers' failure to predict how the subprime bond market would behave under extreme circumstances, and investors lost $1.6 billion. The strategy was simple, purchasing collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) that paid an interest rate over and above the cost of borrowing, using as much leverage as possible, while buying credit default swaps (CDSs) as insurance. As subprime problems unraveled, the market behaved well outside expectations, margin calls forced bond sales, and within a short time the funds experienced a complete loss of capital.

9.1 Types of Hedge Funds

Most hedge funds are highly specialized, relying on the specific expertise of the fund manager(s). Based on risk classification, hedge funds are divided into three main groups.

  • More risky (market directional), seeking high returns using leverage, typically investing based on anticipated events, with objectives such as aggressive growth (equities of high-growth, high-risk firms with accelerated earnings per share growth), emerging markets (equity or debt of emerging markets), macro (changes in global economies brought about by shifts in government policy affecting interest rates), market timing (allocating assets among asset classes based on the manager's view), and short selling (selling securities assessed as overvalued to buy them back later at a lower price)
  • Moderate risk (market neutral or value orientation), more traditional funds similar to mutual funds with only a portion of the portfolio hedged, with objectives such as distressed securities (buying equity, debt, or trade claims of firms in or facing bankruptcy at deep discounts), fund of funds (mixing hedge funds and other pooled vehicles for more stable returns), opportunistic (changing strategy as opportunities arise, such as IPOs or hostile takeover bids), multistrategy, and special situations (event-driven investments such as mergers or leveraged buyouts)
  • Risk avoidance (market neutral), striving for moderate, consistent returns with low risk, with objectives such as income (focus on yield), market neutral-arbitrage (offsetting positions, for example long convertible bonds and short the firm's equity), market neutral-securities hedging (investing equally in long and short equity portfolios), and value (securities selling at deep discounts relative to intrinsic values)

9.2 Hedge Fund Performance

Using traditional risk-adjusted measures of performance (such as Sharpe ratios), hedge fund performance has been very strong compared to traditional financial investments. The average hedge fund lost 15.7 percent in 2008, the worst performance on record, with nearly three-quarters of all funds experiencing losses. Nevertheless, many funds outperformed the underlying markets such as the S&P 500 index, and BlueGold Global Fund earned 209.4 percent in 2008, a year when the S&P 500 earned minus 37.0 percent. Performance improved significantly in 2009 with the average fund earning more than 20 percent, the highest level since 2003 and the second best return in 10 years, although the 2009 return on the S&P 500 was 26.46 percent.

Assets under management in the hedge fund industry fell by nearly 30 percent (to $1.5 trillion) in 2008, the largest decline on record, attributed to negative performance, a surge in redemptions, and liquidations. Performance continued to lag into the 2010s, with hedge funds making 3.4 percent on average a year from 2010 to 2018, compared with 6.4 percent from 2000 to 2010 and 18.3 percent from 1989 to 2000. In 2018, Hedge Fund Research's main index was down 4.07 percent, compared with a 4.38 percent decline for the S&P 500, and six in 10 hedge funds lost money. Net outflows in 2018 reached $34 billion, and fund launches fell to 561 compared to 735 in 2017. More than 3,000 hedge funds have been liquidated since the beginning of 2015, but performance started to improve, and in 2021 an estimated 614 new funds launched (the highest since 2017) while 527 funds liquidated (the lowest since 2004), with the main Hedge Fund Research index gaining 10.3 percent for the full year 2021.

9.3 Fees on Hedge Funds

Hedge fund managers generally charge two types of fees, a management fee computed as a percentage of total assets under management (typically between 1.5 and 2.0 percent) and a performance fee unique to hedge funds, giving the manager a share of any positive returns. The average performance fee has long been around 20 percent but varies widely, for example Steven Cohen's SAC Capital Partners used to charge a performance fee of 50 percent. Performance fees are paid before returns are paid to fund investors. Hedge funds often specify a hurdle rate, a minimum annualized performance benchmark that must be realized before a performance fee can be assessed, and a high-water mark, under which the manager does not receive a performance fee unless the value of the fund exceeds the highest NAV it has previously achieved, linking the manager's incentives more closely to investors and reducing the incentive to increase risk.

Given hedge funds' recent record of low returns, fees are one of the key issues affecting the industry. The standard 2/20 fee model (2 percent management fee and 20 percent performance fee) is slowly giving way to new structures. In 2014, CalPERS liquidated its entire hedge fund portfolio ($4 billion) to cut back on fees. As an example of new structures, Adage Capital Management changed to a flat 0.5 percent management fee and 20 percent performance fee on trading gains or losses in excess of the S&P 500, refunding up to half of those fees if performance falls below the benchmark, and the fund doubled in size in the four years after the change.

9.4 Offshore Hedge Funds

Hedge funds organized in the United States are designated as domestic hedge funds and require investors to pay income taxes on all earnings. Funds located outside the United States and structured under foreign laws are designated as offshore hedge funds. Major offshore financial centers include the Cayman Islands (estimated to be the location of approximately 85 percent of all hedge funds), Bermuda, Dublin, and Luxembourg. Offshore hedge funds must obey the rules of the host country, which are generally not burdensome and provide anonymity, and they are not subject to U.S. income taxes on distributions of profit or to U.S. estate taxes on fund shares. Offshore hedge funds have been found to trade more intensely due to low or zero capital gains tax, engage less often in positive feedback trading, and herd less than domestic hedge funds.

9.5 Regulation of Hedge Funds

While mutual funds are very highly regulated, hedge funds have generally been unregulated. Although hedge funds fall within the same statutory category as mutual funds, they operate under two exemptions from registration requirements in the Investment Company Act of 1940, having fewer than 100 investors and having accredited investors, and they are sold only via private placements so they may not be offered or advertised to the general investing public.

In 2010, the Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act required hedge fund advisors with private pools of capital exceeding $100 million in assets to register with the SEC as investment advisors by July 2011. Managers with less than $100 million in assets under management are overseen by the state where the manager is domiciled. Registration subjects hedge funds to periodic inspections by SEC examiners, and hedge funds are required to report information about their trades and portfolios necessary for assessing systemic risk, with the data kept confidential and shared only with the Financial Stability Oversight Council. Should the government determine that a hedge fund has grown too large or risky, the fund is placed under the supervision of the Federal Reserve. Nevertheless, the regulations imposed on hedge funds continue to be much less onerous than those imposed on mutual funds.

Hedge funds are prohibited from abusive trading practices, and a number got mixed up in the scandals plaguing the mutual fund industry in the 2000s, for example Canary Capital Partners, which agreed to pay $30 million from illicit profits plus a $10 million penalty, and in March 2007 the SEC charged 14 defendants in a scheme involving insiders at UBS Securities, Morgan Stanley, and several hedge funds with $15 million in illicit profits. Two highly publicized scandals occurred in the late 2000s, the Ponzi scheme of Bernard L. Madoff, with client statements showing $65 billion in fictitious stock holdings, resulting in a 150-year prison sentence with restitution of $170 billion, and the Galleon Group LLC insider trading scandal that criminally charged 20 people including co-founder Raj Rajaratnam in the biggest prosecution of alleged hedge fund insider trading in the United States. More recently, SAC Capital Advisors owned by Steven A. Cohen agreed to pay $1.8 billion in penalties in 2013, the largest penalty on record for an insider trading case, and Cohen was barred from managing money for clients for two years until January 1, 2018.

10. Key Formulas and Calculations

10.1 Net Asset Value (NAV)

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{\text{Market value of total portfolio}}{\text{Shares outstanding}} \]

Mutual fund assets are marked-to-market daily, and NAV is the price an investor gets when selling shares back to the fund or buying new shares on that day.

10.2 Worked NAV Calculations

\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{(4{,}000 \times \$22.00) + (1{,}200 \times \$77.00) + (1{,}500 \times \$33.75)}{15{,}000} = \$15.402 \]
\[ \text{NAV} = \frac{(4{,}000 \times \$26.50) + (1{,}200 \times \$78) + (1{,}500 \times \$35)}{15{,}000} = \$16.807 \]

and with 1,000 new investors adding 700 more Macy's shares, NAV becomes $16.916 with 16,000 shares outstanding.

10.3 Closed-End Fund Premium or Discount

\[ \text{Premium or discount} = P_S \times N_S - \text{NAV} \times N_S \]

For example, 50 shares trading at $20 per share with a portfolio value of $800 ($16 per share) produce a premium of $4 per share, and 100 shares trading at $25 with a portfolio value of $3,000 ($30 per share) produce a discount of $5 per share.

10.4 Annualizing the Sales Load

\[ \text{Annualized load} = \frac{\text{Front-end load}}{\text{Investment horizon (years)}} \]
\[ \text{Total annual cost} = \text{Annualized load} + \text{Expense ratio} \]

For example, a 4 percent front-end load with a 10-year horizon produces an annualized load of 0.4 percent, plus a 1 percent expense ratio, for a total of 1.4 percent per year.

10.5 Number of MMMF Shares

\[ \text{Number of shares} = \frac{\text{Market value of assets}}{\$1} \]

An increase in MMMF asset value from $100 to $110 creates 10 new shares ($10 divided by $1 per share), keeping the share value fixed at $1.

10.6 Growth in Worldwide (Non-U.S.) Assets

\[ \text{Growth} = \frac{V_{2007} - V_{1999}}{V_{1999}} \times 100\% = \frac{\$14.130 - \$4.545}{\$4.545} \times 100\% \approx 211\% \]

and from 2008 to 2020, non-U.S. investments increased 262 percent while U.S. investments increased 206 percent.

10.7 Other Key Data

  • Total U.S. mutual fund assets, $23.90 trillion (2020), with 7,636 funds
  • Retirement fund assets managed by mutual funds, about 70 percent of the $34.9 trillion market (2020)
  • ETFs, 2,204 funds with assets of $5.45 trillion (2020)

11. Summary and Conclusions

This chapter provided an overview of the mutual fund and hedge fund industries, two types of financial institutions that pool funds from individuals and corporations and invest in diversified asset portfolios. Mutual funds provide a cost-effective way for investors, especially small investors, to participate in financial markets and diversify risk, while hedge funds serve wealthier clients with more aggressive strategies and much lighter regulation.

The mutual fund industry grew tremendously since the 1990s, surpassing commercial banking as the largest FI group in the United States, but experienced severe shocks during the 2008 to 2009 crisis before recovering to record levels. Investor returns are reflected in NAV, calculated as the market value of the portfolio divided by shares outstanding, and ownership costs include sales loads and annual operating expenses such as 12b-1 fees. Regulation, primarily by the SEC, protects small investors from manager abuses, and post-crisis reforms tightened money market fund rules. Meanwhile, hedge funds, much more lightly regulated and only registered with the SEC since 2010, offer high returns with great risk, as shown by the failures of LTCM and the Bear Stearns funds, and continue to face fee pressure and increasing oversight.
