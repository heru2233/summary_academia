Chapter 1 Summary

Why Are Financial Institutions Special?

Source, Saunders, A. and Cornett, M. M. Financial Institutions Management, A Risk Management Approach, 11th Edition, Chapter 1. Prepared as self-study material.

1. Introduction

Financial institutions (FIs), such as commercial banks, savings institutions, credit unions, insurance companies, securities firms, investment banks, finance companies, and mutual funds, perform the essential function of channeling funds from those with surplus funds (suppliers of funds) to those with shortages of funds (users of funds).

In 2021, U.S. FIs held assets totaling more than $83.23 trillion. By comparison, the U.S. motor vehicle and parts industry (e.g., General Motors and Ford Motor Corp.) held total assets of about $0.80 trillion. Of the largest 50 companies in the United States by total assets, 31 were financial institutions.

Although FIs differ in type and services, they all face several common risks

  1. Credit risk, because FIs hold some assets potentially subject to default
  2. Interest rate risk, because FIs tend to mismatch the maturities of their assets and liabilities
  3. Liquidity risk, because the claims FIs sell to liability holders can be withdrawn
  4. Underwriting risk, through the sale of securities or the issue of credit guarantees on or off the balance sheet
  5. Operating risk, because producing financial services requires real resources and back-office support systems (labor and technology)

The main theme of the book is risk measurement and management. This chapter answers three big questions, namely what special functions FIs provide, what makes some FIs more special than others, and how unique and long-lived those special functions really are.

2. Financial Institutions' Specialness

2.1 The World Without FIs

To understand the economic function of FIs, imagine a simple world in which FIs do not exist. Households with excess savings would have only two basic choices, holding cash as an asset or investing in securities issued by corporations. Both choices involve three disincentives

  1. High monitoring costs. Investors must monitor borrowers to ensure they comply with the covenants in their securities contracts, and this is expensive for individuals
  2. Liquidity costs. Corporate securities are illiquid because they are difficult to sell quickly without large losses
  3. Price risk. Security values can fluctuate sharply, so investors face great uncertainty when selling

The existence of FIs overcomes these problems by transforming the financial claims offered to savers into claims that are more liquid, carry lower risk, and have shorter maturities.

2.2 Broker versus Asset Transformer

FIs can act as brokers, merely bringing together buyers and sellers of securities without transforming claims. However, the more fundamental function is that of an asset transformer, where the FI buys primary securities issued by corporations and sells secondary securities with different characteristics to savers, such as liquid, low-risk deposits.

2.3 Delegated Monitor and Information Costs

FIs act as delegated monitors, monitoring borrowers on behalf of many savers at once. By aggregating funds from many parties, an FI can collect information about customers (e.g., corporations) and monitor their actions at a lower average cost (economies of scale) than individuals could on their own. This is the information costs area in Table 1-1.

2.4 Liquidity, Price Risk, and Transaction Costs

FIs provide financial claims to household savers with superior liquidity attributes and lower price risk. In addition, an FI's size results in economies of scale in transaction costs (transaction cost services), just as in information production.

2.5 Maturity Intermediation

FIs can better bear the risk of mismatching the maturities of their assets and liabilities (maturity intermediation). The common practice is borrowing short term (e.g., deposits) and lending long term (e.g., mortgages), so the FI absorbs interest rate risk on behalf of savers. Larger FIs are generally better able to manage this risk than smaller ones.

3. Other Aspects of Specialness

3.1 Transmission of Monetary Policy

Depository institutions' deposit contracts lie at the core of the two most commonly used definitions of the money supply, M1 and M2. Because depository institutions' liabilities are a significant component of the money supply that affects inflation, they play a key role in transmitting monetary policy from the central bank to the rest of the economy.

  • M1 ($3,738.4 billion outstanding in January 2019) consists of currency outside the U.S. Treasury, Federal Reserve Banks, and depository institution vaults, plus traveler's checks, demand deposits, and other checkable deposits (OCDs)
  • M2 ($14,504.7 billion outstanding in January 2019) consists of M1 plus savings and small time deposits (under $100,000) and other nondeposit obligations of depository institutions

Monetary policy tools include open market operations (purchases and sales of U.S. Treasury securities), setting the discount rate (the rate charged on lender of last resort borrowing from the Federal Reserve), and setting reserve requirements (the minimum reserve assets depository institutions must hold against deposits). A major reason the U.S. and world governments bailed out many depository institutions and raised the deposit insurance limit from $100,000 to $250,000 per person per bank during the financial crisis was so that central banks could implement aggressive monetary policy actions to combat collapsing financial markets.

3.2 Credit Allocation

FIs are often viewed as the major, and sometimes only, source of financing for particular sectors of the economy, such as farming, small business, and residential real estate. Regulation is often used to ensure credit keeps flowing to these sectors.

3.3 Intergenerational Wealth Transfers (Time Intermediation)

FIs, especially life insurance companies and pension funds, provide savers with the ability to transfer wealth from one generation to the next, enabling long-term financial planning and the passing of assets across time.

3.4 Payment Services

The efficiency with which depository institutions provide payment services such as check clearing and funds transfers (e.g., Fedwire, which processes trillions of dollars in payments each day) directly benefits the economy by speeding up the circulation of money.

3.5 Denomination Intermediation

Many financial assets have large minimum denominations, for example negotiable certificates of deposit (CDs) of at least $100,000 and securities with face values of $250,000 or more. Individually, small savers cannot purchase such assets. Mutual funds overcome this constraint by pooling the funds of many small investors, while also providing information services and diversification.

4. Specialness and Regulation

4.1 Negative Externalities and Net Regulatory Burden

FI failure can create negative externalities, costs borne by parties other than the FI itself. An example is the costs faced by small businesses in a one-bank town if the local bank fails, because they may find it difficult to get financing elsewhere, and the failure may have a contagious effect on the economic prospects of the whole community.

Although regulation may be socially beneficial, it also imposes private costs on FI owners and managers, for example prohibiting banks from making loans exceeding 15 percent of their equity capital to a single borrower even when the loan has positive net present value. The private costs of regulation relative to its private benefits, for producers of financial services, is called the net regulatory burden. To the extent that these additional costs help avoid negative externalities and ensure the smooth and efficient operation of the economy, the net regulatory burden is positive.

4.2 Six Types of Regulation

  1. Safety and soundness regulation
  2. Monetary policy regulation
  3. Credit allocation regulation
  4. Consumer protection regulation
  5. Investor protection regulation
  6. Entry and chartering regulation

Regulations are imposed differentially on the various types of FIs. Depository institutions are the most heavily regulated, while finance companies are subject to far fewer regulations. Regulation can be imposed at the federal or state level and occasionally at the international level, as in the case of bank capital requirements.

4.3 Four Layers of Safety and Soundness Protection

To protect depositors and borrowers against the risk of FI failure (for example, due to a lack of diversification), regulators have developed layers of protective mechanisms

  1. Asset diversification. Banks are required not to make loans exceeding 15 percent of their equity capital to any one company or borrower. As an example, a bank that funds 10 percent of its assets with its own capital (and therefore 90 percent with deposits) can lend no more than 1.5 percent of its assets to any one party
  2. Minimum capital. FI owners must contribute a minimum level of capital or equity. The higher the proportion of capital contributed by owners, the greater the protection against insolvency risk for outside claim holders such as depositors and insurance policyholders
  3. Disclosure and examination. FIs must provide financial reports and are subject to examinations to ensure their financial health
  4. Deposit insurance and lender of last resort. The final safety net consists of deposit insurance and emergency borrowing access from the central bank. Because these layers were effective, even during the worst of the financial crisis, bank runs at banks, savings institutions, and credit unions did not occur

5. Dynamics of Specialness

5.1 Trends in Asset Shares

FI asset shares have changed dramatically over more than a century. The share of depository institutions declined from 89.2 percent in 1860 to about 30.2 percent in 2021, while total FI assets grew from $0.001 trillion to $83.23 trillion. Investment companies (mutual funds) grew rapidly and became the largest FI category by 2021 (about 31 percent), insurance companies stayed stable at about 15 percent, and pension funds shrank to about 5 percent. Finance companies and other categories (securities brokers, REITs) hold small shares.

5.2 Changes in How FIs Deliver Services

Traditional FIs are increasingly unable to produce their services as cost efficiently as before, partly because their net regulatory burden is higher than that of investment companies. In response, the U.S. Congress passed the Financial Services Modernization (FSM) Act, which repealed the 1933 Glass-Steagall barriers between commercial banks, investment banks, and insurance companies. Holding company structures became more common, and the business model shifted from originate to hold toward originate to distribute, where loans are made and then sold or securitized, changing incentives in credit risk management.

5.3 The Financial Crisis and Key Lessons

The financial crisis of the late 2000s showed how FI specialness can fail. Subprime delinquency rates reached 17.1 percent in June 2007 and more than 20 percent in August 2007, while securities delinquent by 90 days or more reached 10.09 percent, substantially higher than the 5.37 percent rate in May 2005. The crisis underscored the importance of risk measurement and management. In response, regulators applied stress tests. In May 2009, results showed that 10 of the 19 depository institutions needed to raise a total of $74.6 billion in capital, and within a month of the May 7, 2009 release, they had raised $149.45 billion.

5.4 ERM, Fintech, and Global Trends

After the crisis, risk management shifted from managing risk by functional area (e.g., credit risk or liquidity risk separately) toward enterprise risk management (ERM), a unified approach to the full spectrum of risks. Financial technology (fintech) developments are also changing how FIs operate and serve customers. Globally, FI structure and regulation vary across countries, for example in the roles of insurance, pension funds, and foreign ownership, as well as rules such as the Qualified Thrift Lender (QTL) test, which requires thrifts to hold at least 65 percent of their assets in residential mortgage-related assets.

6. Key Calculations and Formulas

This section summarizes all the important calculations in Chapter 1 together with their formulas.

6.1 Maximum Lending Limit to a Single Borrower

Banks may not lend more than 15 percent of their equity capital to any one borrower.

\[ L_{\max} = 15\% \times E \]

where \( L_{\max} \) is the maximum loan and \( E \) is the bank's equity capital. For example, a bank funding 10 percent of its assets with its own capital can lend at most

\[ L_{\max} = 15\% \times 10\% = 1.5\% \]

of its total assets to any one party.

6.2 Reserve Requirements

Required reserves are calculated as a percentage of deposits.

\[ RR = r \times D \]

where \( RR \) is required reserves, \( r \) is the reserve requirement ratio, and \( D \) is deposits. Using the Bank Three example with transaction deposits \( D = \$600 \) million

  • At \( r = 10\% \), then \( RR = 10\% \times \$600 \) million \( = \$60 \) million
  • If \( r \) is decreased to \( 8\% \), then \( RR = 8\% \times \$600 \) million \( = \$48 \) million
  • If \( r \) is increased to \( 12\% \), then \( RR = 12\% \times \$600 \) million \( = \$72 \) million

6.3 Excess Reserves

When the reserve requirement is decreased from 10 percent to 8 percent, part of the formerly required reserves becomes freely usable.

\[ ER = TR - RR = \$60 \text{ million} - \$48 \text{ million} = \$12 \text{ million} \]

where \( ER \) is excess reserves and \( TR \) is total reserves. The bank can lend out these $12 million of excess reserves.

6.4 The Deposit Multiplier

If the bank withdraws all of its excess reserves and makes loans, and borrowers eventually return all of these funds to the bank as transaction deposits, total deposits can increase by

\[ \Delta D = \frac{1}{r} \times \Delta R = \frac{1}{0.08} \times \$12 \text{ million} = \$150 \text{ million} \]

where \( \Delta D \) is the change in deposits and \( \Delta R \) is the change in reserves. This illustrates the multiplier effect of money creation by the banking system.

6.5 Definitions of the Money Supply

\[ M2 = M1 + \text{savings deposits} + \text{small time deposits} \]

In January 2019, \( M1 = \$3{,}738.4 \) billion and \( M2 = \$14{,}504.7 \) billion.

6.6 Other Key Data

  • Deposit insurance limit raised from $100,000 to $250,000 per person per bank
  • 2009 stress test results, $74.6 billion capital need and $149.45 billion actually raised
  • Subprime delinquency of 17.1 percent (June 2007) and more than 20 percent (August 2007), and 10.09 percent versus 5.37 percent for 90-day delinquencies
  • QTL test, at least 65 percent of thrift assets in residential mortgage-related assets
  • Total U.S. FI assets in 2021, $83.23 trillion

7. Summary and Conclusion

FIs are special because they provide nine functions that benefit the economy, from reducing information and transaction costs, providing liquidity and managing price risk, maturity and denomination intermediation, to the transmission of monetary policy, credit allocation, intergenerational transfers, and payment services. Because of these functions, FIs receive special regulatory attention through six types of regulation and four layers of safety and soundness protection.

Specialness is dynamic. The share of depository institutions has shrunk, investment companies have grown into the largest category, and business models have shifted toward originate to distribute. The 2007-2009 financial crisis proved that FI specialness can fail and that risks must be managed in an integrated way (ERM). Understanding special functions, regulation, and risk calculations such as reserve requirements and lending limits is a key foundation for the study of financial institutions management.
