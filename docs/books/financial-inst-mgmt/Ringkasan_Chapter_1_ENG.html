Ringkasan: Why Are Financial Institutions Special?

Why Are Financial Institutions Special?

Summarized from original source

Introduction

The chapter opens by tracing the evolution of the financial services industry over the past century. Initially, banks operated as full‑service providers, offering commercial banking, investment banking, insurance, and related services under one roof. The Great Depression of the 1930s prompted a regulatory separation of these activities, leading to the Glass‑Steagall era. In the 1970s and 1980s, relatively unregulated sectors such as mutual funds and brokerage firms emerged, further fragmenting the industry. By the early 2000s, technological change, financial innovation, and the relaxation of regulatory barriers allowed large conglomerates—financial services holding companies—to reassemble a full suite of services. JPMorgan Chase, for example, operates in 62 countries, combines a commercial bank (JPMorgan Chase Bank) with an investment bank (J.P. Morgan Securities), and during the 2008‑2009 crisis acquired Washington Mutual and Bear Stearns.

The late 2000s witnessed a financial crisis comparable in magnitude to the Great Depression, triggering massive failures (Bear Stearns, Lehman Brothers) and a subsequent call for renewed separation of activities. Throughout these shifts, the measurement and management of risk have remained central themes. Financial institutions (FIs) channel funds from surplus units (household savers) to deficit units (corporate borrowers). In 2021, U.S. FIs held assets exceeding $83.23 trillion, dwarfing the $0.80 trillion of the motor‑vehicle sector; 31 of the 50 largest U.S. companies by assets were FIs.

Despite functional diversity (banks, credit unions, insurers, mutual funds, etc.), FIs share common risk exposures: credit/default risk, interest‑rate risk from maturity mismatches, liquidity risk from liability withdrawals, underwriting risk from securities sales or guarantees, and operational risk from the use of labor and technology. These risks, together with the socially vital functions FIs perform, justify special regulatory attention.

The chapter’s objectives are threefold: (1) identify the special functions FIs provide and their economic benefits; (2) examine why some FIs are “more special” than others; (3) assess the durability of these functions, especially in light of the pre‑crisis shift toward originate‑to‑distribute models and the rise of shadow banking.

Financial Institutions' Specialness

To appreciate the economic role of FIs, consider a hypothetical world without them. Households with excess savings would either hold cash or purchase corporate securities directly. Direct investment imposes three major costs on the saver:

  • Monitoring (information) costs: verifying that corporate managers do not abscond with or waste funds.
  • Liquidity and price risk: corporate securities are often long‑term and lack active secondary markets, making early sale costly and uncertain.
  • Transaction costs: brokerage commissions, bid‑ask spreads, and other fees erode returns, especially for small investors.

Because these costs are high relative to the size of an individual household’s portfolio, rational savers may prefer to hold cash or forgo saving altogether, reducing the flow of funds to productive investment.

Financial institutions overcome these impediments through two core functions:

  1. Brokerage function: acting as an agent, FIs provide information, research, and trade execution services (e.g., full‑service securities firms, discount brokers, insurance brokers). This reduces transaction and information costs for savers.
  2. Asset‑transformation function: FIs issue claims that are more attractive to savers than the primary securities issued by corporations. By purchasing corporate equities and bonds (primary securities) and financing them with deposits, insurance policies, or mutual‑fund shares (secondary securities), FIs transform risk, improve liquidity, and lower price risk.

The remainder of this section details how FIs mitigate each of the three saver costs.

Information Costs and the Delegated Monitor Role

The fundamental problem is asymmetric information: savers cannot observe whether borrowers use funds productively. Monitoring each borrower individually is prohibitively expensive for a small saver. By pooling many savers’ funds, an FI gains a large stake in any given borrower, creating a strong incentive to monitor. Moreover, the average cost of information collection falls due to economies of scale: a $100 broker’s report is trivial for an FI managing $10 million but prohibitive for a $10 000 individual investment.

This arrangement makes the FI a delegated monitor acting on behalf of the pooled savers. In addition to monitoring, FIs may produce specialized securities that improve monitoring incentives. The classic example is the bank loan: its short‑term nature allows frequent renegotiation, giving the bank timely information about the borrower’s financial condition and reducing reliance on inflexible bond covenants. Through delegated monitoring and better information production, FIs diminish information asymmetry between ultimate savers and borrowers, enhancing the efficiency of capital allocation.

Liquidity and Price Risk

Even if monitoring were costless, savers face liquidity risk: the need to convert investments into cash without suffering large price discounts. Corporate equity and long‑term debt often lack ready secondary markets, exposing sellers to price risk—the chance that the sale price falls below the purchase price.

FIs address this by issuing liabilities with superior liquidity attributes. Depository institutions offer demand deposits withdrawable at par; money‑market mutual funds provide shares with stable net asset value and competitive yields; life insurers allow policy loans on short notice. The puzzle is how FIs can promise such liquidity while holding relatively illiquid, risky assets (e.g., corporate loans, mortgages). The answer lies in risk diversification.

By holding a large, diversified portfolio, an FI can rely on the law of large numbers to predict its aggregate return with greater precision. If the returns on individual assets are not perfectly correlated, diversification reduces idiosyncratic risk. Mathematically, the variance of an equally weighted portfolio of n assets is

\[ \sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i^2 + \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i\neq j}\text{Cov}(R_i,R_j). \]

When assets are independent (\(\text{Cov}=0\)), variance falls as \(1/n\). With moderate diversification (e.g., 15–20 securities), a significant portion of firm‑specific risk is eliminated, leaving mainly systematic risk. A sufficiently large, globally diversified FI can therefore generate an almost risk‑free return on its asset base, enabling it to credibly guarantee fixed‑value, liquid claims to savers.

Thus, the FI’s ability to diversify away firm‑specific risk underwrites the liquidity and low price‑risk attributes of its liabilities, making its claims more attractive than direct corporate securities.

Reduced Transaction Costs

Just as information production benefits from scale, so do transaction costs. In secondary markets, fixed commissions once prevailed; after their abolition in 1975, large traders enjoyed lower effective costs per share due to smaller bid‑ask spreads and the ability to negotiate fees. Small retail investors, however, face higher proportional costs.

FIs mitigate this by pooling investor assets and trading in bulk. Mutual funds, pension funds, and large‑scale securities firms achieve economies of scale in trade execution, lowering the average commission per dollar invested. Moreover, the bid‑ask spread for a security tends to narrow with increased trading volume, further reducing transaction costs for pooled investors.

Consequently, savers who invest through an FI enjoy lower effective transaction costs than if they traded the same securities individually.

Maturity Intermediation

Household savers typically prefer short‑term, liquid claims, whereas corporations often seek long‑term financing for plant, equipment, or infrastructure. Direct matching of these preferences would leave many profitable projects unfunded. FIs resolve this mismatch by engaging in maturity intermediation: they accept short‑term deposits (liabilities) and use the proceeds to fund long‑term loans or bonds (assets).

This creates a maturity gap on the FI’s balance sheet, exposing it to interest‑rate risk. However, large FIs are better equipped to manage this risk through:

  • Access to deep secondary markets for loan sales and securitization.
  • Use of derivatives such as futures, swaps, options, caps, floors, and collars for hedging.
  • Sophisticated asset‑liability management (ALM) systems that match the duration of assets and liabilities.

By bearing and managing interest‑rate risk, FIs provide the economy with long‑term credit while still offering savers the liquidity they desire.

Other Aspects of Specialness

Beyond the core functions of information cost reduction, liquidity provision, transaction cost savings, and maturity intermediation, FIs contribute to the economy through several additional channels that may justify their existence even in a frictionless information environment.

Transmission of Monetary Policy

Depository institutions’ liabilities (checking accounts, savings deposits, etc.) constitute a large share of the money supply. The Federal Reserve defines two aggregates:

  • M1**: currency outside banks + traveler’s checks + demand deposits + other checkable deposits (OCDs). In January 2019, M1 ≈ $3,738.4 billion.
  • M2**: M1 + savings and small time deposits (< $100,000) + other nondeposit obligations of depository institutions. In January 2019, M2 ≈ $14,504.7 billion.

Because these deposits are a major component of “inside money,” changes in the Fed’s policy tools (open‑market operations, discount rate, reserve requirements) affect the volume of deposits and, consequently, the amount of credit banks can extend. The money multiplier illustrates this:

\[ m = \frac{1}{rr}, \]

where rr is the required reserve ratio. An increase in reserves ΔR leads to a potential increase in deposits ΔD = m·ΔR. Thus, depository institutions act as the conduit through which monetary policy influences aggregate spending, investment, and inflation.

During the 2008‑2009 crisis, the Fed’s aggressive policy actions (quantitative easing, near‑zero rates) relied on a stable, insured deposit base to transmit stimulus to the broader economy, prompting the temporary raise of the deposit insurance limit to $250,000.

Credit Allocation

FIs often serve as the primary—or sole—source of financing for certain sectors deemed socially important, such as residential real estate, agriculture, and small business. Governments may encourage this specialization via subsidies, tax incentives, or directed lending programs (e.g., Federal Farm Credit Banks). By channeling credit to these sectors, FIs help promote affordable housing, farm viability, and entrepreneurial activity, contributing to social welfare and economic stability.

Intergenerational Wealth Transfers

Life insurance companies and pension funds enable savers to transfer wealth across generations. Premiums paid today fund future death benefits or retirement income, allowing individuals to provide for heirs without incurring immediate taxation on the transferred amount. This function supports long‑term household planning and reduces the burden on public welfare systems.

Payment Services

Depository institutions excel at providing efficient payment mechanisms. Two critical wholesale systems are:

  • Fedwire**: a real‑time gross settlement system for large‑value transfers.
  • CHIPS** (Clearing House Interbank Payments System): a net‑settlement system handling high‑volume dollar transactions.

On any given day, trillions of dollars move through these networks, facilitating trade, payroll, and settlement. Efficient payment systems reduce transaction friction, lower the cost of commerce, and support overall economic activity.

Denomination Intermediation

Many securities are issued in large minimum denominations that exceed the capacity of individual savers. Examples include negotiable certificates of deposit (≥ $100,000) and commercial paper (often ≥ $250,000). Small investors cannot efficiently purchase such instruments directly, leading to either exclusion or highly undiversified portfolios.

Money‑market and debt‑equity mutual funds solve this by pooling investor contributions and buying the large‑denomination assets in bulk. Each investor receives a proportional share (a fund unit) that reflects ownership of the underlying pool. This process—denomination intermediation—allows small savers to access markets otherwise out of reach, achieve diversification, and potentially earn higher returns.

Specialness and Regulation

The socially valuable services described above generate positive externalities, but their disruption can impose negative externalities on the economy. A negative externality occurs when the actions of one agent impose costs on others without compensation. For instance, the failure of a sole bank in a one‑bank town can deprive local businesses of credit, reduce employment, and lower community income.

Because such spillovers can be severe, regulators oversee FIs to protect the provision of their special functions. Regulation aims to increase the net social welfare benefit of FI activities while mitigating the private costs borne by FI owners and managers. The difference between private benefits and private costs of regulation is termed the net regulatory burden. When the burden is positive, regulation yields a net social gain.

Six major categories of regulation shape FI behavior:

  1. Safety and soundness regulation: ensures FIs remain solvent and able to meet obligations.
  2. Monetary policy regulation: governs tools that affect the money supply and credit conditions.
  3. Credit allocation regulation: directs lending toward socially prioritized sectors.
  4. Consumer protection regulation: guards against unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices toward depositors and borrowers.
  5. Investor protection regulation: safeguards investors in securities markets, mutual funds, and related products.
  6. Entry and chartering regulation: controls who may establish an FI and what activities a charter permits.

Regulatory intensity varies by FI type. Depository institutions (banks, thrifts, credit unions) face the most extensive oversight, while finance companies encounter fewer rules. Regulations may be imposed at federal, state, or international levels (e.g., Basel capital standards). Historically, the U.S. system featured institution‑specific rules (e.g., Community Reinvestment Act applications to individual banks), though deregulation and consolidation have liberalized many of these.

Safety and Soundness Regulation

Regulators employ several layers to protect depositors and borrowers from FI failure:

  1. Asset diversification requirements: limit concentration risk. For example, a bank may not lend more than 15 % of its equity capital to any single borrower. If a bank’s equity funds 10 % of its assets, the maximum loan to one party is 1.5 % of assets.
  2. Minimum capital (equity) requirements: ensure owners bear losses before depositors or policyholders. The capital‑to‑risk‑assets ratio is a key metric; higher ratios increase loss‑absorbing capacity.
  3. Guaranty funds: such as the FDIC’s Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF) for banks, the SIPC for securities firms, and state guaranty funds for insurers. These funds compensate claim holders when an FI’s equity is wiped out, preserving confidence in the system.
  4. Monitoring and surveillance: on‑site examinations, regular reporting, and off‑site analysis to detect emerging problems.
  5. Cost considerations: while regulation adds compliance expenses (legal, accounting, reporting), it aims to prevent far larger systemic losses.

The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) of 2008‑2009 illustrated these layers: the Capital Purchase Program (CPP) injected equity into qualifying institutions, and stress tests of the 19 largest depository institutions revealed a needed $74.6 billion in capital, which the industry raised to $149.45 billion within a month.

Monetary Policy Regulation

The Federal Reserve influences the economy primarily through three tools:

  • Open‑market operations (OMO): buying or selling U.S. Treasury securities to adjust bank reserves.
  • Discount rate: the interest rate charged on short‑term loans to banks from the Fed’s discount window.
  • Reserve requirements: the fraction of deposits that banks must hold as reserves (either vault cash or deposits at the Fed).

When the Fed purchases securities, it pays with newly created reserves, expanding banks’ capacity to lend. The resulting increase in the money supply can be estimated via the money multiplier. Conversely, selling securities drains reserves and contracts credit.

Changes in the discount rate affect banks’ marginal cost of borrowing from the Fed; a lower rate encourages borrowing and lending, while a higher rate has the opposite effect. Although the Fed can influence reserves directly via OMO, it often uses reserve requirements to make the money‑supply link more predictable, though banks may view required reserves as a cost akin to a tax if they earn little or no interest.

Credit Allocation Regulation

Examples include:

  • Quality Thrift Lender (QTL) test: requires savings institutions to hold at least 65 % of assets in residential mortgage‑related assets to retain a thrift charter.
  • Usury laws: state‑level caps on interest rates for mortgages and consumer loans.
  • Former Regulation Q: previously set maximum interest rates on time and savings deposits (now abolished).

Such rules aim to promote affordable housing, farm credit, and consumer protection. However, they can increase FI costs by limiting pricing flexibility or forcing suboptimal asset allocations, thereby contributing to a positive net regulatory burden when the social benefits outweigh the private costs.

Consumer Protection Regulation

Key statutes are the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) and the Home Mortgage Disclosure Act (HMDA). HMDA mandates that lenders disclose detailed information about mortgage applications, enabling regulators and the public to assess whether credit is being allocated fairly across geographic, racial, age, and income lines. The FFIEC processed over 13.6 million HMDA‑reported mortgage transactions in 2020 from 4,472 institutions, illustrating the substantial information‑production burden.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), created by the Dodd‑Frank Act (2010), consolidates consumer‑protection authority across the financial sector, targeting unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts and practices.

Investor Protection Regulation

Core legislation includes the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934, the Investment Company Act of 1940, and the Dodd‑Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. These laws address insider trading, disclosure requirements, fiduciary duties, and fraud. Compliance entails significant reporting, auditing, and legal costs, contributing to the net regulatory burden for securities firms, mutual funds, and investment banks.

Entry and Chartering Regulation

Regulators shape industry structure by:

  • Setting minimum capital or equity thresholds for new charters.
  • Restricting permissible activities under a given charter (e.g., separating commercial banking from insurance under Glass‑Steagall).
  • Influencing the competitive landscape: high entry costs protect incumbents, while low entry barriers foster competition and potentially lower charter value.

The Financial Services Modernization (Gramm‑Leach‑Bliley) Act of 1999 repealed Glass‑Steagall, allowing the formation of financial services holding companies that could engage in banking, insurance, and securities activities simultaneously. This increased the potential charter value for firms able to diversify across sectors, while also raising concerns about systemic risk due to increased complexity.

The Changing Dynamics of Specialness

The importance and profitability of various FI functions evolve with shifts in demand, technology, and regulation. Table 1‑2 presents the percentage share of total assets held by each FI category from 1860 to 2021, highlighting several long‑term trends.

Trends in the United States

Depository institutions: their share fell from 62.7 % in 1948 to 30.2 % in 2021, reflecting a relative decline in payment services, transaction‑cost services, and information‑cost intermediation as a proportion of total FI activity.

Insurance companies: share declined from 23.4 % to 15.1 % over the same period.

Investment companies (mutual funds & money‑market funds): experienced the most dramatic growth, rising from 1.1 % in 1948 to 31.1 % in 2021. These firms provide low‑cost access to direct securities markets, leveraging scale and diversification while placing less emphasis on maturity transformation.

Pension funds: share rose from 1.2 % in 1948 to a peak of 18.9 % in 2015, then receded to 5.3 % by 2021, reflecting shifts in retirement‑plan design and the growth of defined‑contribution plans.

Finance companies: share grew modestly from 0.63 % in 1960 to 0.9 % in 2021.

Securities brokers and dealers: share expanded from 0.27 % in 1960 to 52.78 % of total FI assets in 2021, underscoring the rising importance of market‑making, underwriting, and advisory services.

In absolute terms, total FI assets grew from $0.001 trillion in 1860 to $83.23 trillion in 2021, illustrating the sector’s immense expansion.

The Rise of Financial Services Holding Companies

The asset‑share trends indicate that savers increasingly value the denomination‑intermediation and information services offered by mutual funds, while traditional FIs face higher net regulatory burdens. Recognizing this, Congress passed the Financial Services Modernization (FSM) Act of 1999, which dismantled the 1933 Glass‑Steagall barriers separating commercial banking, insurance, and investment banking. The act permitted the creation of financial services holding companies capable of engaging in banking, insurance, and securities activities under a single corporate umbrella.

As a result, the holding‑company form has become dominant in terms of total assets. JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, and Wells Fargo exemplify this model, each combining commercial banking, investment banking, and, in many cases, substantial insurance or wealth‑management operations.

The Shift from “Originate and Hold” to “Originate and Distribute”

A pivotal development preceding the 2008‑2009 crisis was the transformation of the banking business model. Under the traditional originate and hold approach, banks accepted short‑term deposits, extended long‑term loans to businesses and consumers, and retained those loans to maturity. This structure gave banks an ongoing incentive to screen and monitor borrowers, as the loan’s performance directly affected the bank’s portfolio.

In contrast, the originate and distribute model involves warehousing loans briefly and then selling them to investors via securitization or loan syndicates. The originating bank retains little or no exposure to the loan’s credit performance after sale, diminishing its incentive to conduct thorough due diligence. Figure 1‑3 illustrates the explosive growth in bank‑loan secondary‑market trading from 1991 to 2021, with notable expansion even during the crisis years.

Several factors drove this shift:

  • Regulatory changes (e.g., FSM Act) that permitted broader activities and encouraged fee‑based income.
  • Technological advances in loan trading, pricing, and risk‑transfer infrastructure.
  • The pursuit of improved return‑risk trade‑offs by moving loans off the balance sheet and reducing regulatory capital charges.
  • The emergence of the shadow‑banking system, which performed similar intermediation functions outside the traditional banking perimeter.

Consequently, many large banks reorganized as financial service holding companies to facilitate underwriting, trading, and securitization activities.

Shadow Banking and Systemic Risk

The shadow‑banking system comprises entities that perform bank‑like intermediation without holding a bank charter. Participants include structured investment vehicles (SIVs), special‑purpose vehicles (SPVs), asset‑backed commercial paper (ABCP) conduits, money‑market mutual funds (MMMFs), credit hedge funds, and limited‑purpose finance companies. In this system:

  • Savers place funds with money‑market funds or similar vehicles, which then invest in the liabilities of other shadow banks.
  • Borrowers obtain loans from finance companies or other shadow‑bank intermediaries rather than from traditional banks.
  • Credit intermediation occurs through a chain of entities, each performing a slice of the overall process (e.g., loan origination, pooling, tranching, sale).

Because risk is transferred off the originator’s balance sheet, the traditional incentives for monitoring and due diligence are weakened. The originate‑to‑distribute model, amplified by shadow banking, contributed to the erosion of underwriting standards, particularly in the subprime mortgage market.

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)

In response to the crisis, FIs have increasingly adopted enterprise risk management, which treats the full spectrum of risks—credit, market, liquidity, operational, underwriting, and others—as an interrelated portfolio. ERM emphasizes:

  • Risk culture and governance aligned with a clearly defined risk appetite.
  • Integration of risk considerations into strategic planning, capital allocation, and performance‑measurement processes.
  • Use of quantitative tools (e.g., value‑at‑risk, stress testing) complemented by qualitative assessments of emerging threats.
  • Active oversight by a chief risk officer (CRO) and a board‑level risk committee.

The Industry Perspectives box highlights JPMorgan Chase’s ERM framework, which includes independent risk‑management functions, clear lines of accountability, and a disciplined compensation structure that ties incentives to risk‑adjusted performance.

Fintech and Digital Disruption

Financial technology (fintech) refers to the use of technology to deliver financial services that compete with traditional methods. The Financial Stability Board defines fintech as “technology‑enabled innovation in financial services that could result in new business models, applications, processes, or products with a material effect on the provision of financial services.” Fintech advantages include:

  • Freedom from legacy IT systems and branch networks.
  • Lower regulatory burden in many jurisdictions (at least initially).
  • Agility, innovation mindset, and a consumer‑centric focus.

Examples such as cryptocurrencies (e.g., Bitcoin) and blockchain enable peer‑to‑peer value transfer without intermediaries, potentially reducing transaction costs and disrupting existing organizational forms. However, fintech also introduces new risks (cybersecurity, operational, regulatory uncertainty).

Traditional FIs are responding by forming partnerships, acquiring fintech startups, and investing heavily in internal technology. JPMorgan Chase, for instance, reported a 2017 technology budget of $9.5 billion and a 2021 budget of $10.8 billion, with over $5 billion earmarked for new investments in areas such as artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and data analytics.

Global Trends

U.S. FIs now compete not only domestically but also with foreign institutions offering comparable services. This increasing foreign presence intensifies competition for deposits, lending, and fee‑based income, pressuring U.S. FIs to improve efficiency, innovate, and manage cross‑border risks.

Summary

This chapter has elucidated why financial institutions occupy a special position in the economy. Their core specialness stems from four interrelated functions:

  1. Reducing information (monitoring) costs through delegated monitoring and economies of scale in information production.
  2. Providing liquid assets with low price risk by diversifying firm‑specific risk across large portfolios.
  3. Lowering transaction costs via economies of scale in trade execution and narrower bid‑ask spreads.
  4. Engaging in maturity intermediation—transforming short‑term saver deposits into long‑term borrower loans—while managing the resulting interest‑rate risk through sophisticated hedging and asset‑liability management.

Beyond these, FIs contribute to monetary‑policy transmission, credit allocation to socially important sectors, intergenerational wealth transfers, efficient payment services, and denomination intermediation that democratizes access to high‑minimum‑denomination securities.

The specialness of FIs generates positive externalities, but disruptions can impose significant negative externalities (e.g., bank runs, credit crunches). Consequently, regulators impose a layered framework—safety and soundness, monetary policy, credit allocation, consumer protection, investor protection, and entry rules—to preserve the net social benefits of FI activity. The net regulatory burden is positive when the societal gains from stable, well‑functioning FIs outweigh the private costs of compliance.

Over time, the relative importance of these functions has shifted. Depository institutions’ share of total FI assets has declined, while investment companies, securities brokers, and holding companies have grown. The financial‑services modernization act enabled the rise of conglomerates that combine banking, insurance, and securities under one charter. Simultaneously, a strategic shift from the originate‑to‑hold model to originate‑to‑distribute, amplified by shadow banking, weakened traditional monitoring incentives and contributed to the subprime mortgage crisis.

In the aftermath, FIs have embraced enterprise risk management to better capture the interconnected nature of risks, invested heavily in fintech to remain competitive, and navigated an increasingly global landscape where foreign banks hold a growing share of U.S. assets. The enduring challenge for FIs—and for regulators—is to preserve the socially valuable functions that make them special while adapting to technological change, evolving consumer preferences, and the persistent need for effective risk measurement and management.
