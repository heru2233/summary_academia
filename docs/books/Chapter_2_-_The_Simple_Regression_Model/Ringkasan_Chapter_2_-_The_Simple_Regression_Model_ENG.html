1. Definition of the Simple Regression Model

1.1 The Population Model

The simple regression model is the starting point for empirical economic analysis. It attempts to explain one variable, \(y\), using another variable, \(x\). The core premise is that \(y\) and \(x\) are two variables describing some population, and the researcher is interested in explaining \(y\) in terms of \(x\), or studying how \(y\) changes when \(x\) changes. The model is called simple, two-variable, or bivariate linear regression because it relates only two variables, \(x\) and \(y\).

Writing down such a model forces the researcher to confront three issues. First, no exact relationship exists between two variables in the social sciences, so the model must allow for other factors to affect \(y\). Second, the functional relationship between \(y\) and \(x\) must be specified. Third, the researcher must decide whether the model can uncover a ceteris paribus relationship, that is, the effect of \(x\) on \(y\) holding all other relevant factors fixed.

The simple linear regression model solves these issues by writing \(y\) as a linear function of \(x\) plus an error term. The error term \(u\) collects all factors other than \(x\) that affect \(y\). Because those factors are unobserved, the error term is often thought of as standing for "unobserved." The coefficient \(\beta_1\) is the slope parameter and measures the effect of \(x\) on \(y\) when the other factors in \(u\) are held fixed. The coefficient \(\beta_0\) is the intercept, or constant term, and determines the value of \(y\) when \(x = 0\) and the average unobserved factors are zero.

Simple Linear Regression Model
\[ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u \]

The linearity assumption means that if other factors in \(u\) are held fixed, so \(\Delta u = 0\), then a one-unit change in \(x\) is associated with a change of exactly \(\beta_1\) in \(y\). This is expressed as \(\Delta y = \beta_1 \Delta x\). This is a constant marginal effect: the effect of a one-unit increase in \(x\) is the same regardless of the initial value of \(x\). For many economic relationships, this is unrealistic. For example, an additional year of education may have a larger effect on wages for someone with little education than for someone with many years of education. Later, logarithmic transformations are introduced to allow for constant percentage effects instead of constant unit effects.

The term "regression" has historical origins not essential for modern practice, but the framework remains central to applied econometrics. The key point is that \(u\) represents all omitted factors, and \(\beta_1\) represents the ceteris paribus effect of \(x\) on \(y\). That interpretation, however, is only valid if \(u\) is appropriately unrelated to \(x\). Without restrictions on how \(u\) and \(x\) are related, the model cannot identify the causal effect of \(x\) on \(y\).

1.2 Terminology

The variable \(y\) has many names: dependent variable, explained variable, response variable, predicted variable, and regressand. The variable \(x\) is called the independent variable, explanatory variable, control variable, predictor variable, and regressor. The term "covariate" is also used for \(x\). These names are used interchangeably in econometrics, although "explained" and "explanatory" are the most descriptive. It is important to note that "independent" in this context does not mean statistically independent from \(y\); it only refers to the role of the variable in the regression equation.

1.3 Assumptions About the Error Term

Because \(u\) and \(x\) are random variables, the relationship between them must be described probabilistically. One assumption can always be made without loss of generality if the intercept is included: the average value of \(u\) in the population is zero, \(E(u) = 0\). This does not restrict the relationship between \(u\) and \(x\); it only normalizes the mean of the unobserved factors to zero.

The crucial assumption is that the average value of \(u\) does not depend on \(x\). This is called mean independence, written \(E(u|x) = E(u)\). Combined with \(E(u) = 0\), this gives the zero conditional mean assumption \(E(u|x) = 0\). This assumption is much stronger than saying \(u\) and \(x\) are uncorrelated. Correlation only captures linear association, so it is possible for \(u\) to be uncorrelated with \(x\) but still related to functions of \(x\), such as \(x^2\). The zero conditional mean assumption rules out all such dependence and is needed for the causal interpretation of regression.

Zero Conditional Mean Assumption
\[ E(u|x) = 0 \]

Under the zero conditional mean assumption, the population regression function (PRF) is linear: \(E(y|x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\). This tells us how the average value of \(y\) changes with \(x\), not how each individual \(y\) changes. For example, if \(E(colGPA | hsGPA) = 1.5 + 0.5 hsGPA\), then students with a high school GPA of 3.6 have an average college GPA of 3.3, but individual college GPAs can be above or below that value depending on unobserved factors.

The zero conditional mean assumption is not automatically satisfied. In the wage equation, if innate ability is part of \(u\), then \(E(u|x) = 0\) requires the average level of ability to be the same for all education levels. If more able people tend to obtain more education, the assumption fails. Similarly, in the fertilizer example, if farmers apply more fertilizer to higher-quality plots, then average land quality changes with fertilizer, violating \(E(u|x) = 0\). Whether this assumption holds is ultimately an empirical and conceptual question that cannot be settled by data alone.

Example

Soybean yield and fertilizer. Let \(y = yield\) and \(x = fertilizer\). The model is \(yield = \beta_0 + \beta_1 fertilizer + u\). The error term \(u\) contains land quality, rainfall, and other plot characteristics. The coefficient \(\beta_1\) measures the effect of fertilizer on yield holding those other factors fixed. If fertilizer amounts are chosen independently of land quality, then \(E(u|x) = 0\) holds. But if farmers put more fertilizer on better land, the expected unobserved quality changes with fertilizer and the assumption fails.

Wage and education. Let \(y = wage\) in dollars per hour and \(x = educ\) in years. The model is \(wage = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + u\). The slope \(\beta_1\) measures the change in hourly wage from another year of education, holding labor force experience, ability, tenure, work ethic, and other factors fixed. The zero conditional mean assumption requires that average ability is the same across education levels, which may be false if people with more ability choose more education.

Analogy

Think of the simple regression model as a recipe. The dependent variable \(y\) is the final dish. The explanatory variable \(x\) is the main ingredient that the recipe focuses on, and \(u\) is all the other ingredients, cooking conditions, and chef skill that also affect the final dish. The coefficient \(\beta_1\) tells you how much the final dish changes when you add one more unit of the main ingredient while keeping everything else fixed. The zero conditional mean assumption is like requiring that the "other ingredients" do not systematically change when the main ingredient changes. If people who add more sugar also tend to add more salt, then you cannot isolate the pure effect of sugar.

Key Insight

The entire causal interpretation of the simple regression model rests on the zero conditional mean assumption \(E(u|x) = 0\). Without this assumption, OLS only describes correlation, not causation. The assumption cannot be tested directly because \(u\) is unobserved; it must be justified by economic reasoning or by the design of the data collection process.

Key Takeaways

  • The simple regression model is \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + u\), where \(u\) contains all factors other than \(x\) that affect \(y\).
  • The slope \(\beta_1\) measures the ceteris paribus effect of \(x\) on \(y\): a one-unit increase in \(x\) changes \(y\) by \(\beta_1\), holding other factors fixed.
  • The zero conditional mean assumption \(E(u|x) = 0\) is crucial for interpreting \(\beta_1\) as a causal effect.
  • Under this assumption, the population regression function is \(E(y|x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\).

2. Deriving the Ordinary Least Squares Estimates

2.1 Population Moments and Sample Counterparts

To estimate \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), we need a random sample of size \(n\) from the population: \(\{(x_i, y_i): i = 1, \ldots, n\}\). For each observation, the population model implies \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\). The error term \(u_i\) contains all unobserved factors affecting \(y_i\) for observation \(i\).

The zero conditional mean assumption implies two population moment conditions: \(E(u) = 0\) and \(Cov(x, u) = E(xu) = 0\). The first condition says the average unobserved factor is zero; the second says the unobserved factor is uncorrelated with the explanatory variable. In terms of observable variables and unknown parameters, these become \(E(y - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x) = 0\) and \(E[x(y - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x)] = 0\).

The method of moments approach replaces population expectations with sample averages. The estimators \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) are chosen to satisfy the sample versions of these two equations. Solving those equations yields the ordinary least squares estimates.

Sample Moment Conditions
\[ n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right) = 0 \]
\[ n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \left(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right) = 0 \]

2.2 OLS Formulas

The first sample moment condition can be rewritten using sample averages. If \(\bar{y}\) and \(\bar{x}\) are the sample averages of \(y_i\) and \(x_i\), then \(\bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\). This means the OLS regression line passes through the sample means of the data. Rearranging gives \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\), so the intercept is determined once the slope is known.

Substituting this expression into the second sample moment condition and using summation algebra gives the formula for \(\hat{\beta}_1\): the sample covariance between \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) divided by the sample variance of \(x_i\). The formula requires that \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 > 0\), which fails only if all \(x_i\) are identical. If all observations have the same value of \(x\), there is no variation in \(x\) with which to explain variation in \(y\), so OLS cannot be computed.

OLS Slope and Intercept Estimates
\[ \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \]
\[ \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \]

An alternative expression for the OLS slope is \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \hat{r}_{xy} \cdot (s_x / s_y)\), where \(\hat{r}_{xy}\) is the sample correlation between \(x_i\) and \(y_i\), and \(s_x\), \(s_y\) are sample standard deviations. This shows that the sign of \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is the same as the sign of the sample correlation. If \(x\) and \(y\) are positively correlated in the sample, \(\hat{\beta}_1 > 0\); if negatively correlated, \(\hat{\beta}_1 < 0\). It also highlights that simple regression is fundamentally an analysis of correlation between two variables. Without experimental data or additional assumptions, one must be careful before giving \(\hat{\beta}_1\) a causal interpretation.

2.3 Fitted Values, Residuals, and Least Squares

For a given intercept and slope, the predicted value of \(y\) when \(x = x_i\) is the fitted value \(\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\). The residual is the difference between the actual value and the fitted value: \(\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\). Residuals are not the same as population errors \(u_i\); they are sample-based quantities that depend on the estimated parameters.

The OLS estimates are defined as the values of \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) that minimize the sum of squared residuals, \(\sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i^2\). The first order conditions for this minimization are exactly the sample moment conditions above. The name "ordinary least squares" comes from the fact that these estimates minimize the sum of squared residuals. Minimizing other functions of the residuals, such as the sum of absolute values, is sometimes useful but has drawbacks: no closed-form formulas exist, and the statistical theory is much more complicated.

Fitted Value and Residual
\[ \hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i \]
\[ \hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i \]
Sum of Squared Residuals
\[ \min_{\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\right)^2 \]

After computing OLS estimates, the OLS regression line is \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x\). This is also called the sample regression function (SRF) because it is the estimated version of the population regression function \(E(y|x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\). The PRF is fixed but unknown; the SRF changes from sample to sample. The slope estimate \(\hat{\beta}_1\) tells us the predicted change in \(y\) for a one-unit increase in \(x\): \(\Delta \hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_1 \Delta x\).

Example

CEO salary and return on equity. For 209 CEOs, let \(salary\) be annual salary in thousands of dollars and \(roe\) be average return on equity as a percentage. The estimated regression is \(\widehat{salary} = 963.191 + 18.501 \, roe\), \(n = 209\), \(R^2 = 0.0132\). If \(roe = 0\), predicted salary is $963,191. If \(roe\) increases by one percentage point, predicted salary increases by $18,501. This is the same predicted change regardless of the initial level of \(roe\) because the model is linear.

Wage and education. For 526 workers in 1976, the estimated equation is \(\widehat{wage} = -0.90 + 0.54 \, educ\), \(n = 526\). The negative intercept is literally the predicted wage for zero years of education, which is \(-90\) cents per hour. This is not economically meaningful, but only 18 people in the sample have fewer than eight years of education, so the regression line should not be trusted at very low education levels. The slope implies that one more year of education raises predicted hourly wage by 54 cents.

Voting outcomes and campaign expenditures. For 173 U.S. House races, let \(voteA\) be the percentage of the vote received by Candidate A and \(shareA\) be Candidate A's percentage of total campaign expenditures. The estimated equation is \(\widehat{voteA} = 26.81 + 0.464 \, shareA\), \(n = 173\), \(R^2 = 0.856\). A one-percentage-point increase in spending share is associated with a 0.464-percentage-point increase in vote share. When \(shareA = 50\), the predicted vote share is about 50 percent.

Analogy

Imagine drawing a scatterplot of points on a graph and trying to place a straight line through the cloud of points. OLS is like choosing the line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances from each point to the line. If you instead minimized the absolute vertical distances, the line would be harder to compute by hand and would have less convenient statistical properties. The OLS line is the "best fit" line in the least squares sense, similar to finding the line that balances the total squared errors as small as possible.

Key Insight

OLS estimates are easy to compute and have very useful algebraic properties, but their causal interpretation depends on the assumptions of the population model. Simple regression alone is essentially an analysis of correlation, and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) should not be interpreted as a causal effect unless the zero conditional mean assumption is justified.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS estimates solve sample moment conditions derived from \(E(u) = 0\) and \(Cov(x,u) = 0\).
  • The OLS slope is the sample covariance of \(x\) and \(y\) divided by the sample variance of \(x\).
  • The OLS intercept makes the regression line pass through the sample means: \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\).
  • OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals, producing fitted values and residuals for every observation.

3. Properties of OLS on Any Sample of Data

3.1 Fitted Values and Residuals

Once OLS estimates are obtained, each observation has a fitted value \(\hat{y}_i\) and a residual \(\hat{u}_i\). The fitted value lies on the OLS regression line. If the residual is positive, the regression line underpredicts \(y_i\); if the residual is negative, the line overpredicts \(y_i\). In most datasets, no observations lie exactly on the line; the residuals represent the vertical distances from the points to the fitted line.

Every \(y_i\) can be decomposed into a fitted value plus a residual: \(y_i = \hat{y}_i + \hat{u}_i\). This decomposition is central to understanding how OLS separates the variation in \(y\) into an explained part and an unexplained part.

Decomposition of \(y_i\)
\[ y_i = \hat{y}_i + \hat{u}_i \]

3.2 Three Algebraic Properties

The OLS estimates are constructed to satisfy several algebraic properties that hold for any sample of data. These properties are not statistical assumptions; they are mathematical consequences of the way OLS estimates are computed. They hold by construction, regardless of whether the population model is correctly specified.

The first property is that the sum of the OLS residuals is zero. This follows from the first order condition \(\sum (y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i) = 0\). Consequently, the sample average of the residuals is also zero. The second property is that the sample covariance between the explanatory variable \(x_i\) and the residuals \(\hat{u}_i\) is zero. This follows from the second first order condition \(\sum x_i \hat{u}_i = 0\). It means the residuals contain no linear association with \(x\). The third property is that the point \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) is always on the OLS regression line, which follows from \(\bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\).

Algebraic Properties of OLS
\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i = 0 \]
\[ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \hat{u}_i = 0 \]
\[ \bar{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x} \]

Using these properties, one can also show that the sample covariance between the fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\) and the residuals \(\hat{u}_i\) is zero. Thus, OLS splits each \(y_i\) into a part related to \(x\) and a part unrelated to \(x\). This decomposition is the foundation for measuring how well the regression explains variation in \(y\).

3.3 Goodness-of-Fit: SST, SSE, and SSR

To measure how well the explanatory variable explains the dependent variable, define three sums of squares. The total sum of squares (SST) measures the total sample variation in \(y_i\) around its mean. The explained sum of squares (SSE) measures the sample variation in the fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\) around their mean. The residual sum of squares (SSR) measures the sample variation in the residuals \(\hat{u}_i\).

Because the fitted values and residuals are uncorrelated in the sample, the total variation can be split into explained and unexplained variation: \(SST = SSE + SSR\). This decomposition holds exactly for any sample when an intercept is included in the regression.

Total, Explained, and Residual Sums of Squares
\[ SST = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \]
\[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \]
\[ SSR = \sum_{i=1}^n \hat{u}_i^2 \]
\[ SST = SSE + SSR \]

The \(R\)-squared, or coefficient of determination, is defined as the ratio of the explained sum of squares to the total sum of squares: \(R^2 = SSE/SST = 1 - SSR/SST\). Because \(SSE\) cannot exceed \(SST\), \(R^2\) always lies between zero and one. Multiplying by 100 gives the percentage of sample variation in \(y\) explained by \(x\). It can be shown that \(R^2\) equals the square of the sample correlation between \(y_i\) and the fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\).

A low \(R^2\) does not necessarily mean the regression is useless. In cross-sectional data, especially in the social sciences, low \(R^2\) values are common. The OLS estimate of a ceteris paribus relationship can still be valuable even if \(x\) explains only a small fraction of the variation in \(y\). Conversely, a high \(R^2\) does not prove causality. Students often overemphasize \(R^2\) when evaluating regression equations; the sign and magnitude of the slope estimate and the plausibility of the zero conditional mean assumption are usually more important.

Example

CEO salary regression. The estimated equation \(\widehat{salary} = 963.191 + 18.501 \, roe\) has \(n = 209\) and \(R^2 = 0.0132\). Thus, return on equity explains only 1.3 percent of the variation in CEO salaries. The other 98.7 percent of salary variation is left unexplained by this simple regression, which is not surprising because many factors affect CEO compensation.

Voting outcomes regression. The estimated equation \(\widehat{voteA} = 26.81 + 0.464 \, shareA\) has \(n = 173\) and \(R^2 = 0.856\). Here, the share of campaign expenditures explains more than 85 percent of the variation in election outcomes. This is a large \(R^2\) for cross-sectional data.

Fitted values and residuals for CEOs. For the first CEO in the sample, \(roe = 14.1\) and \(salary = 1095\) (in thousands). The fitted value is \(\hat{salary} = 963.191 + 18.501(14.1) = 1224.058\). The residual is \(1095 - 1224.058 = -129.058\), meaning the CEO earns less than predicted based on the firm's return on equity. Positive residuals indicate CEOs who earn more than predicted.

Analogy

Think of \(SST\) as the total amount of "scatter" in the \(y\) values, like the spread of exam scores around the class average. The fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\) represent the part of that scatter that can be predicted from \(x\), while the residuals represent the part that cannot. \(R^2\) is the fraction of the total scatter that is accounted for by the regression line. If \(R^2 = 0.85\), the line explains 85 percent of the variation, much like a weather forecast that correctly anticipates most of the day-to-day temperature changes.

Key Insight

A low \(R^2\) does not mean the regression is useless, and a high \(R^2\) does not mean the regression is causally valid. The appropriate use of \(R^2\) is as a descriptive measure of explanatory power, not as the main criterion for judging an econometric analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • The OLS residuals sum to zero, are uncorrelated with \(x\), and the regression line always passes through the sample means of \(x\) and \(y\).
  • The total variation in \(y\) can be decomposed as \(SST = SSE + SSR\).
  • \(R^2 = SSE/SST = 1 - SSR/SST\) measures the fraction of sample variation in \(y\) explained by \(x\).
  • \(R^2\) is invariant to changes in units of measurement and should not be overemphasized in evaluating regression results.

4. Units of Measurement and Functional Form

4.1 Changing Units of Measurement

In applied economics, variables are often measured in different units: dollars versus thousands of dollars, percentages versus decimals, or hours versus minutes. It is essential to know how OLS estimates change when the units of measurement change. The good news is that the changes are entirely predictable and do not require re-estimating the model.

If the dependent variable is multiplied by a constant \(c\), then both the OLS intercept and slope estimates are multiplied by \(c\). For example, if salary is measured in thousands of dollars and the estimated slope is \(18.501\), then measuring salary in dollars requires multiplying both the intercept and slope by \(1,000\). The resulting equation has exactly the same economic interpretation. If the independent variable is divided or multiplied by a constant \(c\), the OLS slope is multiplied or divided by \(c\), respectively. The intercept is unaffected by scaling the independent variable because \(x = 0\) remains \(x = 0\) after such scaling.

The goodness-of-fit measure \(R^2\) is invariant to changes in the units of measurement of either \(y\) or \(x\). Intuitively, the fraction of variation explained should not depend on whether a variable is measured in dollars or cents. Mathematically, the correlation between \(y\) and \(\hat{y}\) is unaffected by linear rescaling.

Effects of Rescaling the Dependent Variable
\[ \text{If } y^* = c y, \text{ then } \hat{\beta}_0^* = c \hat{\beta}_0 \text{ and } \hat{\beta}_1^* = c \hat{\beta}_1 \]
Effects of Rescaling the Independent Variable
\[ \text{If } x^* = c x, \text{ then } \hat{\beta}_1^* = \hat{\beta}_1 / c \]

Example

CEO salary measured in dollars. The original regression is \(\widehat{salary} = 963.191 + 18.501 \, roe\), where \(salary\) is in thousands of dollars. Let \(salardol = 1000 \cdot salary\) be salary in dollars. Then the estimated equation becomes \(\widehat{salardol} = 963{,}191 + 18{,}501 \, roe\). If \(roe = 0\), predicted salary is $963,191. If \(roe\) increases by one percentage point, predicted salary increases by $18,501. The two equations are equivalent.

Return on equity as a decimal. Let \(roedec = roe/100\). The regression of \(salary\) on \(roedec\) gives \(\widehat{salary} = 963.191 + 1{,}850.1 \, roedec\). The slope is 100 times larger because \(roedec\) is 100 times smaller than \(roe\). A one-percentage-point change in \(roe\) is \(0.01\) in decimal form, so the predicted salary change is \(1850.1(0.01) = 18.501\), exactly as before.

Salary measured in hundreds of dollars. If \(salarhun = 10 \cdot salary\), the intercept becomes \(9{,}631.91\) and the slope becomes \(185.01\). This preserves the interpretation in hundreds of dollars.

4.2 Logarithmic Functional Form

Linear models impose a constant marginal effect: a one-unit change in \(x\) changes \(y\) by the same amount everywhere. For many economic relationships, a constant percentage effect is more plausible. For example, each additional year of education may increase wages by a constant percentage, rather than by a constant dollar amount. The natural logarithm is used to turn a constant percentage effect into a linear model.

Consider the model \(\log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + u\). When \(\Delta u = 0\), one more year of education changes \(\log(wage)\) by \(\beta_1\). Multiplying by 100 gives the approximate percentage change in wage: \(\%\Delta wage \approx 100 \cdot \beta_1 \Delta educ\). This model implies that the dollar increase in wage becomes larger as education increases, because a constant percentage of a larger wage base is a larger dollar amount. This is often a more realistic characterization than the linear model.

Constant Percentage Effect Model
\[ \log(wage) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 educ + u \]
\[ \%\Delta wage \approx (100 \cdot \beta_1) \Delta educ \]

Example

A log wage equation. Using the same wage data as the linear wage equation, the estimated log wage equation is \(\widehat{\log(wage)} = 0.584 + 0.083 \, educ\), \(n = 526\), \(R^2 = 0.186\). The slope \(0.083\) means that each additional year of education is associated with an approximate 8.3 percent increase in hourly wage. This is a constant percentage effect: an extra year of education raises wages by about 8.3 percent whether the worker currently has 6 or 16 years of education. In dollar terms, however, the increase is larger for a highly paid worker than for a low-paid worker.

Analogy

Imagine a savings account that earns compound interest. A linear model would say the account grows by the same dollar amount every year. A log-linear model says the account grows by the same percentage every year, so the dollar increase grows over time as the balance grows. The log wage model treats education like an investment with a constant percentage rate of return, which is usually more natural for human capital.

Key Insight

Changing the units of measurement only rescales the coefficients and does not affect \(R^2\). Using logarithmic transformations changes the economic interpretation from a constant unit effect to a constant percentage effect. The choice of functional form is not a technical detail; it determines what kind of economic relationship is being estimated.

Key Takeaways

  • Multiplying the dependent variable by \(c\) multiplies both OLS coefficients by \(c\); multiplying the independent variable by \(c\) divides the OLS slope by \(c\).
  • \(R^2\) is invariant to changes in the units of measurement of \(y\) and \(x\).
  • Log-dependent-variable models approximate constant percentage effects: \(\%\Delta y \approx 100 \beta_1 \Delta x\).
  • The log wage model implies increasing dollar returns to education even though the percentage return is constant.
