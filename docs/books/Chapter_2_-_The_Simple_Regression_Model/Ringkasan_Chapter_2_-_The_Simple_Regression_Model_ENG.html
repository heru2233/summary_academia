1. Definition of the Simple Regression Model

1.1 The Model

Applied econometric analysis often begins with a simple premise: y and x are two variables in some population, and we are interested in explaining y in terms of x, or in studying how y varies with changes in x. Examples include soybean yield and fertilizer, hourly wage and years of education, and community crime rate and number of police officers. Writing a model that explains y in terms of x requires confronting three issues: how to allow for factors other than x that affect y; what functional relationship to assume between y and x; and how to ensure that the relationship captures a ceteris paribus effect if that is the goal.

The simple regression model resolves these issues by writing an equation that is assumed to hold in the population of interest. The model is called the simple linear regression model, the two-variable linear regression model, or the bivariate linear regression model. It relates the two variables x and y through a linear equation containing an intercept, a slope, and an unobserved error term.

Simple Regression Model
\[ y = b_0 + b_1 x + u \]

In this equation, y is the dependent variable, explained variable, response variable, predicted variable, or regressand. x is the independent variable, explanatory variable, control variable, predictor variable, or regressor; the term covariate is also used for x. The label “independent” does not mean statistical independence between random variables. The variable u is the error term or disturbance and represents factors other than x that affect y; it can be thought of as “unobserved.” The intercept parameter is \(b_0\), sometimes called the constant term. The slope parameter is \(b_1\), the parameter of primary interest in most applied work.

The model is not a claim that y is exactly determined by x. It breaks y into a systematic part \(b_0 + b_1 x\), which is the part explained by x, and an unsystematic part \(u\), which is the part not explained by x. The zero conditional mean assumption, discussed below, gives this systematic part a precise interpretation as the average value of y given x.

1.2 The Slope as a Ceteris Paribus Effect

Change in y Holding u Fixed
\[ \Delta y = b_1 \Delta x \quad \text{if } \Delta u = 0 \]

Here \(\Delta y\) is the change in y, \(\Delta x\) is the change in x, and \(b_1\) is the slope. If the unobserved factors in u are held fixed, so \(\Delta u = 0\), then a one-unit increase in x changes y by \(b_1\) units. Thus \(b_1\) measures the linear effect of x on y holding all other factors in u fixed. This is the ceteris paribus interpretation of the slope. The linearity implies that a one-unit change in x has the same effect on y regardless of the initial value of x, which is restrictive and often unrealistic in economics.

In practice, this equation means that if we know \(b_1\), we can compute the predicted change in y for any given change in x. The slope coefficient is not automatically a causal effect unless the assumptions about u are satisfied.

Example

Example 2.1 Soybean Yield and Fertilizer. The model \(\text{yield} = b_0 + b_1 \text{fertilizer} + u\) measures the effect of fertilizer on yield, holding other factors fixed. The error term contains land quality, rainfall, and other plot-specific factors. The coefficient \(b_1\) is the effect of an additional unit of fertilizer on yield when those other factors are held constant.

Example 2.2 A Simple Wage Equation. The model \(\text{wage} = b_0 + b_1 \text{educ} + u\) measures the change in hourly wage from one more year of education, holding all other factors fixed. Those other factors include labor force experience, innate ability, tenure with current employer, work ethic, and many other unobservables.

1.3 The Zero Conditional Mean Assumption

Zero Mean of Errors
\[ E(u) = 0 \]

Because the intercept \(b_0\) is included in the model, nothing is lost by assuming that the average value of the unobserved factors in the population is zero. This assumption says nothing about how u is related to x; it is simply a normalization of the distribution of unobserved factors. In the soybean example, unobserved factors such as land quality can be normalized to have average zero across all cultivated plots. In the wage example, unobservables such as average ability can be assumed to average zero in the working population.

This normalization alone does not allow causal interpretation. A stronger assumption is needed about the relationship between u and x.

Mean Independence Assumption
\[ E(u|x) = E(u) \]

The conditional expectation \(E(u|x)\) is the average value of the unobservables at a given value of x. The mean independence assumption states that this average value does not depend on x. Combined with \(E(u)=0\), this gives the zero conditional mean assumption: \[ E(u|x) = 0. \] This is the critical assumption. It says that, for every slice of the population defined by x, the average of the omitted factors is the same, and in particular it is zero.

In the wage-education example, suppose u is innate ability. Then \(E(u|x)=0\) requires that average ability is the same for all education levels: \(E(\text{abil}|\text{educ}=8)\) must equal \(E(\text{abil}|\text{educ}=16)\). If people with more ability tend to become more educated, average ability increases with education, and the assumption fails. In the fertilizer example, if fertilizer amounts are chosen independently of plot quality, the assumption holds; if more fertilizer is placed on higher-quality plots, the expected value of u changes with fertilizer level, and the assumption fails.

Zero correlation between u and x is not sufficient. Correlation measures only linear dependence. It is possible for u to be uncorrelated with x yet correlated with functions of x such as \(x^2\). The conditional mean assumption is stronger and more appropriate for regression.

1.4 The Population Regression Function

Population Regression Function (PRF)
\[ E(y|x) = b_0 + b_1 x \]

Taking the expected value of \(y = b_0 + b_1 x + u\) conditional on x and using \(E(u|x)=0\) gives the population regression function. This function describes how the average value of y changes with x. The linearity means that a one-unit increase in x changes the expected value of y by \(b_1\). For any given x, the distribution of y is centered at \(E(y|x)\).

The PRF does not imply that every individual with a given x has \(y = b_0 + b_1 x\). For example, if \(E(\text{colGPA}|\text{hsGPA}) = 1.5 + 0.5\text{hsGPA}\), then for \(\text{hsGPA}=3.6\) the average college GPA is \(1.5 + 0.5(3.6)=3.3\). Some students with that high school GPA will have a higher college GPA and some a lower one, depending on the unobserved factors in u.

Analogy

The equation \(y = b_0 + b_1 x + u\) is like a recipe with a predictable average outcome and individual variation. The systematic part \(b_0 + b_1 x\) tells the average result for a given amount of the main ingredient x; the error term u represents all other factors that make each individual outcome differ from the average.

Key Insight

The zero conditional mean assumption is what gives \(b_1\) a causal or ceteris paribus interpretation. Without \(E(u|x)=0\), the simple regression slope is merely a description of association, not a valid estimate of the effect of x on y holding other factors fixed.

Key Takeaways

  • The simple regression model is \(y = b_0 + b_1 x + u\), with y the explained variable, x the explanatory variable, and u the unobserved error.
  • The slope \(b_1\) measures the change in y for a one-unit change in x when \(u\) is held fixed.
  • The assumption \(E(u|x)=0\) is necessary for \(b_1\) to be interpreted as the ceteris paribus effect of x on y.
  • Under \(E(u|x)=0\), the population regression function is \(E(y|x)=b_0+b_1x\).

2. Deriving the Ordinary Least Squares Estimates

2.1 Sample Data and Population Moment Conditions

To estimate \(b_0\) and \(b_1\), we need a random sample of size n from the population: \(\{(x_i, y_i): i=1,\dots,n\}\). Because these data come from the population model, we can write \(y_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i + u_i\) for each observation i. Here \(u_i\) is the error term for observation i, containing all factors affecting \(y_i\) other than \(x_i\).

The assumptions \(E(u)=0\) and \(E(u|x)=0\) imply that, in the population, u has zero mean and is uncorrelated with x. In terms of observable variables and unknown parameters, these become moment conditions that must hold at the true values of \(b_0\) and \(b_1\).

Population Moment Conditions
\[ E(y - b_0 - b_1 x) = 0 \]
\[ E[x(y - b_0 - b_1 x)] = 0 \]

The first condition states that the average residual in the population is zero. The second states that the error is uncorrelated with the explanatory variable; because \(E(u)=0\), this is equivalent to \(Cov(x,u)=0\). These two conditions identify the two unknown parameters \(b_0\) and \(b_1\).

In practice, we do not observe the population distribution. We use the sample analogs of these moment conditions and choose estimates \(\hat b_0\) and \(\hat b_1\) that satisfy them. This is the method of moments approach to estimation.

Sample Counterparts
\[ n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat b_0 - \hat b_1 x_i) = 0 \]
\[ n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(y_i - \hat b_0 - \hat b_1 x_i) = 0 \]

The first equation implies that the sample average of the fitted residuals is zero. The second implies that the sample covariance between x and the fitted residuals is zero. Solving these equations gives the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates. The OLS estimates minimize the sum of squared residuals, and these two equations are called the first order conditions for that minimization.

2.2 The OLS Formulas

OLS Intercept Estimate
\[ \hat b_0 = \bar y - \hat b_1 \bar x \]

Here \(\bar y = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\) is the sample average of the dependent variable, \(\bar x\) is the sample average of the explanatory variable, and \(\hat b_1\) is the OLS slope estimate. This formula shows that the OLS regression line always passes through the point \((\bar x, \bar y)\). Once the slope is known, the intercept is determined by the sample means.

OLS Slope Estimate
\[ \hat b_1 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2} \]

This is the sample covariance between \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) divided by the sample variance of \(x_i\). The numerator measures whether \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) move together in the sample. The denominator measures how much x varies in the sample. The slope estimate is computable provided the denominator is positive: \[ \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2 > 0. \] This condition fails only when all \(x_i\) are the same value. If x does not vary in the sample, there is no way to estimate the slope of the relationship between x and y.

The formula can also be written in terms of the sample correlation \(\hat r_{xy}\) and sample standard deviations \(\hat s_x, \hat s_y\): \[ \hat b_1 = \hat r_{xy} \left(\frac{\hat s_x}{\hat s_y}\right). \] Therefore, \(\hat b_1\) has the same sign as the sample correlation: positive if \(x_i\) and \(y_i\) are positively correlated, negative if they are negatively correlated. This highlights an important limitation: simple regression, without further assumptions, is essentially an analysis of correlation between two variables.

2.3 Fitted Values and Residuals

Fitted Value and Residual
\[ \hat y_i = \hat b_0 + \hat b_1 x_i \]
\[ \hat u_i = y_i - \hat y_i = y_i - \hat b_0 - \hat b_1 x_i \]

The fitted value \(\hat y_i\) is the value of y predicted by the OLS line when \(x = x_i\). There is a fitted value for each observation. The residual \(\hat u_i\) is the difference between the actual value \(y_i\) and the fitted value. If \(\hat u_i\) is positive, the line underpredicts \(y_i\); if negative, the line overpredicts \(y_i\). Residuals are not the same as the population errors \(u_i\): residuals are computed from the sample and fitted line, while errors are unobserved population quantities.

OLS chooses \(\hat b_0\) and \(\hat b_1\) to make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible: \[ \min_{\hat b_0, \hat b_1} \sum_{i=1}^n \hat u_i^2 = \min_{\hat b_0, \hat b_1} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat b_0 - \hat b_1 x_i)^2. \] The OLS estimates are the values satisfying the first order conditions for this minimization problem. The name “ordinary least squares” comes from minimizing the sum of squared residuals.

2.4 The OLS Regression Line

Sample Regression Function
\[ \hat y = \hat b_0 + \hat b_1 x \]

The OLS regression line, also called the sample regression function (SRF), is the estimated version of the population regression function \(E(y|x)=b_0+b_1x\). The intercept \(\hat b_0\) is the predicted value of y when \(x=0\), although setting \(x=0\) is not always meaningful. The slope \(\hat b_1\) is the primary quantity in most applications.

The slope estimate is often written as \[ \hat b_1 = \frac{\Delta \hat y}{\Delta x}, \] so that \[ \Delta \hat y = \hat b_1 \Delta x. \] This says that for any change in x, the predicted change in y is \(\hat b_1\) times that change. In particular, when \(x\) increases by one unit, the predicted value of y changes by \(\hat b_1\) units.

Examples

Example 2.3 CEO Salary and Return on Equity. Using data on 209 CEOs, the estimated equation is \[ \widehat{\text{salary}} = 963.191 + 18.501\,\text{roe}, \] where salary is in thousands of dollars and roe is the average return on equity in percent. If roe = 0, predicted salary is \(963.191\), or \(\$963,191\). If roe increases by one percentage point, predicted salary increases by \(18.501\), or \(\$18,500\). This is a predicted change, not a guarantee that any particular CEO’s salary rises by that amount. The linear equation implies the same predicted effect regardless of the initial level of roe.

Example 2.4 Wage and Education. Using 526 observations from WAGE1, the estimated equation is \[ \widehat{\text{wage}} = -0.90 + 0.54\,\text{educ}. \] The intercept of \(-0.90\) literally means a person with zero education has a predicted hourly wage of \(-90\) cents, which is not meaningful; very few people in the sample have very low education. For a person with 8 years of education, predicted wage is \(-0.90 + 0.54(8)=3.42\), or \(\$3.42\) per hour. The slope estimate implies that one more year of education increases hourly wage by 54 cents. Four more years increase predicted wage by \(4(0.54)=2.16\), or \(\$2.16\) per hour.

Example 2.5 Voting Outcomes and Campaign Expenditures. Using 173 congressional races, the estimated equation is \[ \widehat{\text{voteA}} = 26.81 + 0.464\,\text{shareA}. \] If Candidate A’s share of campaign spending increases by one percentage point, Candidate A is predicted to receive about 0.464 percentage points more of the vote. If shareA = 50, predicted voteA is about 50. Whether this is a causal effect is unclear; it could simply reflect the correlation between spending and electoral success.

2.5 Terminology: Regressing y on x

When an OLS regression has been run, we often say that we regress y on x, where y is the dependent variable and x is the independent variable. For example, we “regress salary on roe” to obtain equation (2.26), and “regress voteA on shareA” to obtain equation (2.28). Unless stated otherwise, this means the intercept is estimated along with the slope.

Analogy

The OLS slope is like the tilt of a line drawn through a cloud of points: it summarizes the average direction of the relationship. A positive sample correlation tilts the line upward; a negative correlation tilts it downward.

Key Insight

Computing OLS estimates requires only sample variation in \(x\), not the zero conditional mean assumption. The formulas always produce a line. Interpreting \(\hat b_1\) as the ceteris paribus effect of x on y is another matter: that interpretation requires \(E(u|x)=0\) and careful reasoning about causality.

Key Takeaways

  • OLS estimates are obtained by solving sample versions of the population moment conditions \(E(u)=0\) and \(E(xu)=0\).
  • The OLS slope is the sample covariance of \(x\) and \(y\) divided by the sample variance of \(x\).
  • The OLS intercept is \(\hat b_0 = \bar y - \hat b_1 \bar x\), so the line passes through \((\bar x, \bar y)\).
  • Fitted values and residuals satisfy \(\hat u_i = y_i - \hat y_i\); OLS minimizes the sum of squared residuals.
  • OLS regression is, by itself, an analysis of correlation; causality requires additional assumptions.

3. Properties of OLS on Any Sample of Data

3.1 Algebraic Properties of Fitted Values and Residuals

Some properties of OLS hold by construction for any sample of data, regardless of whether the population assumptions are true. These properties are useful for interpreting results, detecting mistakes, and understanding what happens when data are manipulated.

Zero Sum of Residuals
\[ \sum_{i=1}^n \hat u_i = 0 \]

The OLS estimates are chosen so that the residuals add up to zero. Equivalently, the sample average of the residuals is zero. This property follows from the first OLS first order condition. It does not say that any particular residual is zero; individual residuals can be positive or negative, but they must cancel out in sum.

Zero Covariance Between Regressor and Residuals
\[ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \hat u_i = 0 \]

Because the sample average of the residuals is zero, this condition means that the sample covariance between the explanatory variable \(x_i\) and the residuals \(\hat u_i\) is zero. In other words, the part of y left unexplained by the OLS line is not linearly related to x in the sample. This is true by construction for OLS with an intercept.

The Sample Means Lie on the Regression Line
\[ \bar y = \hat b_0 + \hat b_1 \bar x \]

This property follows directly from the formula \(\hat b_0 = \bar y - \hat b_1 \bar x\). It means that the point \((\bar x, \bar y)\) is always on the OLS regression line. If the sample average of x is plugged into the estimated equation, the predicted value is the sample average of y.

Each actual \(y_i\) can be written as its fitted value plus its residual: \[ y_i = \hat y_i + \hat u_i. \] From the properties above, the sample average of the fitted values equals the sample average of the actual values, and the fitted values and residuals are uncorrelated in the sample.
