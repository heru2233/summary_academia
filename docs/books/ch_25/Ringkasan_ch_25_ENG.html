Overview: The Evolution of Bank Regulation

Interpretation

Chapter 25 provides a comprehensive historical account of the international regulatory frameworks that govern banks and insurance companies, focusing on the evolution from the 1988 Basel Accord (Basel I) through the 1996 Amendment to Basel II, and concluding with Solvency II for insurers. The central theme is that bank regulation is an evolutionary process: new regulations modify previous ones while preserving many of the core approaches. Understanding this historical development is essential for comprehending the current regulatory environment and the post-2007 crisis reforms covered in subsequent chapters.

The chapter emphasizes that the primary purpose of bank regulation is to ensure banks maintain sufficient capital for the risks they take. The authors argue that regulation is necessary because banks, left to their own devices, may not manage risks prudently. Deposit insurance programs, while protecting depositors, create moral hazard by reducing depositor discrimination, making capital regulation a necessary complement. The chapter also highlights the importance of systemic risk, which was dramatically illustrated during the 2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis.

The chapter traces the key developments: the 1988 Basel Accord established the Cooke ratio and risk-weighted assets; the G-30 recommendations (1993) set best practices for derivatives risk management; netting provisions reduced counterparty credit risk; the 1996 Amendment added market risk capital requirements with VaR-based internal models; and Basel II (implemented around 2007) overhauled credit risk capital, added operational risk capital, and introduced a three-pillar structure. Solvency II for insurance companies in the EU (implemented 2016) follows a broadly similar philosophy.

Key Insight

The evolution of bank regulation is a story of regulators responding to financial innovation and past crises. Each successive framework—Basel I, the 1996 Amendment, Basel II—was designed to address the weaknesses of its predecessor while retaining its successful elements. The 1988 Accord's core insight was that capital requirements should be risk-based rather than simply a ratio of capital to total assets. Basel II built on this by making the risk weights more granular and incorporating internal models for sophisticated banks.

The most profound insight is that regulation itself must evolve because the financial system evolves. The chapter's historical approach reveals that the 2007 crisis cannot be understood without first understanding the regulatory frameworks that preceded it, including their achievements and their shortcomings.

Analogy

Think of bank regulation as the evolution of building codes. Basel I was like the first basic building code that required buildings to have fire escapes (minimum capital ratio to assets). Basel II represents a more sophisticated code that specifies different fire-safety standards for different building types (risk-based weights, internal models for sophisticated banks). The G-30 recommendations are like industry best-practice guidelines from professional architects. Just as building codes evolve after major fires reveal weaknesses, bank regulations evolve after financial crises reveal flaws. Basel I, Basel II, and Solvency II are successive "building codes" designed to make the financial system safer, each learning from the failures of the past.

The Reasons for Regulating Banks

Interpretation

The fundamental purpose of bank regulation is to ensure that banks maintain adequate capital relative to the risks they assume. While it is impossible to completely eliminate the risk of bank failure, governments aim to make the probability of default for any given bank extremely small. This creates a stable economic environment in which individuals and businesses can have confidence in the banking system.

The authors argue that regulation is necessary despite the tempting counterargument that banks would manage risks prudently on their own. Historical evidence does not support this view, and regulation has played an essential role in increasing bank capital and making banks more aware of their risk-taking.

A crucial reason for regulation is the interaction with deposit insurance. Deposit insurance programs, designed to protect depositors, create moral hazard: because depositors are protected, they become less discriminating, allowing banks to take excessive risks without losing their deposit base. Thus, deposit insurance must be accompanied by capital regulation to prevent this moral hazard from manifesting in dangerous risk-taking.

Systemic risk is another major concern. The failure of one large bank can trigger a cascade of failures through the interconnections of the financial system. This creates a dilemma for governments: allowing a failing institution to fail risks the entire financial system, while bailing it out sends the signal that it is "too big to fail" and encourages future recklessness. The 2008 decision to bail out some institutions but let Lehman Brothers fail illustrates this tension.

Key Insight

Regulation and deposit insurance are complementary, not substitutes. Deposit insurance alone creates moral hazard by making depositors indifferent to bank risk-taking. Regulation alone cannot fully protect the banking system without also protecting small depositors who lack the information and sophistication to assess bank risk. Together, they form a system that protects depositors while constraining bank risk-taking. The concept of systemic risk—the "ripple effect"—distinguishes banking regulation from the regulation of most other industries.

The "too big to fail" problem is not a modern invention. The chapter shows that this dilemma existed even before 2008, but the Global Financial Crisis brought it into sharp focus. The decision to let Lehman Brothers fail, which was made to signal that bailouts were not automatic, arguably worsened the crisis—highlighting the fragility of the financial system and the systemic importance of large interconnected institutions.

Analogy

Bank regulation is like building a dam to protect a town from flooding. Individuals and businesses in the town (depositors and the economy) need the dam (regulation) to feel safe. If the town builds flood walls but no dam (deposit insurance without regulation), residents will build houses anywhere because they feel protected—and may build in flood-prone areas (banks take excessive risks). If there's a dam but no flood warning system, the dam might fail without inhabitants being able to prepare. The dam (regulation) must be maintained, upgraded, and adapted to changing rainfall patterns (financial innovation and evolving risks) to effectively protect the town.

Key Takeaways

  • The main purpose of bank regulation is to ensure banks hold enough capital for the risks they take, making the probability of default very small.
  • Regulation is necessary because banks may not manage risks prudently without it; historical evidence does not support the view that self-regulation works.
  • Deposit insurance creates moral hazard by reducing depositor discrimination, making capital regulation an essential complement.
  • Systemic risk—the risk that one large bank's failure triggers cascading failures—is a major concern for regulators.
  • Governments face a dilemma: bailing out large institutions encourages reckless behavior ("too big to fail"), but allowing failure can destabilize the financial system.

Bank Regulation Pre-1988

Interpretation

Before 1988, bank regulators within each country typically set minimum levels for the ratio of capital to total assets. This approach had several critical flaws. First, definitions of capital and acceptable ratios varied significantly across countries. Second, enforcement of regulations was inconsistent; some countries enforced rules more diligently than others. Third, banks were increasingly competing globally, and banks from countries with lax capital regulations had a competitive advantage. Fourth, the growing complexity of bank transactions—particularly the rapid expansion of the over-the-counter derivatives market—meant that total assets no longer captured the true risks banks were taking.

The OTC derivatives market, including interest rate swaps, currency swaps, and foreign exchange options, created significant credit risks that were not reflected in reported asset levels. For example, if a counterparty defaults on an interest rate swap with a positive value to the bank, the bank loses money, but this exposure was not captured in the bank's balance sheet assets. The value of total assets was therefore no longer a good indicator of total risk.

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision was formed in 1974, consisting of representatives from twelve countries, meeting regularly in Basel, Switzerland under the patronage of the Bank for International Settlements. The Committee's first major achievement was the document "International Convergence of Capital Measurement and Capital Standards" in 1988, which became known as the 1988 BIS Accord and later Basel I.

Key Insight

The pre-1988 regulatory regime had become obsolete because of three converging forces: international competition (where lax regulation created competitive advantages), the debt crisis involving less developed countries, and the explosive growth of OTC derivatives. The last factor was critical because derivative exposures were invisible on balance sheets, making the simple capital-to-asset ratio a completely inadequate measure of bank risk. This insight—that off-balance-sheet risks matter—was foundational to Basel I's risk-weighted approach.

Analogy

Pre-1988 bank regulation was like measuring a person's health by only checking their height. Two people of the same height can have very different health statuses based on their weight, blood pressure, cholesterol, and lifestyle. Similarly, two banks with the same total assets can have vastly different risk profiles based on the types of loans they've made, their derivatives exposures, and their off-balance-sheet commitments. The Basel Committee's response was akin to developing a more comprehensive health checkup that measures multiple risk indicators.

The 1988 BIS Accord (Basel I)

The Cooke Ratio and Risk-Weighted Assets

Interpretation

The 1988 BIS Accord was the first international risk-based standard for capital adequacy. Its key innovation was the Cooke ratio, which required banks to hold capital equal to at least 8% of their total risk-weighted assets. The Cooke ratio considered credit risk exposures from three categories: on-balance-sheet assets, off-balance-sheet items, and OTC derivatives.

For on-balance-sheet assets, each asset was assigned a risk weight reflecting its credit risk. Cash and OECD government securities had a 0% weight, loans to OECD banks had a 20% weight, uninsured residential mortgages had a 50% weight, and corporate loans had a 100% weight. For off-balance-sheet items like guarantees and loan commitments, a credit equivalent amount was calculated by applying a conversion factor to the principal amount. For OTC derivatives, the credit equivalent amount was calculated using the current exposure method (CEM).

The capital requirement was 8% of total risk-weighted assets. Capital was divided into Tier 1 (core capital: equity and noncumulative perpetual preferred stock) and Tier 2 (supplementary capital: cumulative perpetual preferred stock, certain 99-year debentures, and subordinated debt with original life over five years). At least 50% of required capital (4% of risk-weighted assets) had to be Tier 1, and at least 2% of risk-weighted assets had to be common equity.

Credit Equivalent Amount for OTC Derivatives (Current Exposure Method)
Credit Equivalent Amount = max(V, 0) + aL

where V is the current value of the derivative to the bank, a is the add-on factor (percentage of principal), and L is the principal amount.

Total Risk-Weighted Assets
Total RWA = Σ(wᵢLᵢ) + Σ(w*ⱼCⱼ)

where wᵢ is the risk weight for the i-th on-balance-sheet asset, Lᵢ is the principal of that asset, w*ⱼ is the risk weight of the counterparty for the j-th derivative/off-balance-sheet item, and Cⱼ is its credit equivalent amount.

Example

Example 25.1 (Risk-Weighted Assets for On-Balance-Sheet Items): A bank has $100 million of corporate loans (100% risk weight), $10 million of OECD government bonds (0% risk weight), and $50 million of residential mortgages (50% risk weight). Total risk-weighted assets = 1.0 × 100 + 0.0 × 10 + 0.5 × 50 = $125 million.

Example 25.2 (Credit Equivalent Amount for a Swap): A bank has a $100 million interest rate swap with four years remaining life and a current value of $2.0 million. The add-on factor from Table 25.2 for an interest rate swap with 1-5 year maturity is 0.5%. Credit equivalent amount = $2.0 million + (0.005 × $100 million) = $2.0 million + $0.5 million = $2.5 million.

Example 25.3 (Risk-Weighted Assets for a Swap): For the swap in Example 25.2, if the counterparty is a corporation (risk weight 50% for off-balance-sheet items), risk-weighted assets = 2.5 × 0.5 = $1.25 million. If the counterparty is an OECD bank (20% risk weight), risk-weighted assets = 2.5 × 0.2 = $0.5 million.

Analogy

The Cooke ratio is like a luggage weight system that charges different rates per kilogram depending on the item. Cash and government securities are like light scarves (0% weight), mortgages are like medium-weight shoes (50% weight), and corporate loans are like heavy suitcases (100% weight). Derivatives and off-balance-sheet items are like items that are not in your carry-on bag (off-balance-sheet) but still count toward the total weight—you need to estimate their equivalent weight using conversion factors and add-on factors. Just as an airline needs to ensure the plane is not overloaded, regulators need to ensure banks hold enough capital for their total risk-weighted "luggage."

Key Insight

Basel I was a major achievement despite its simplicity. It established the principle of risk-based capital requirements, which was a fundamental improvement over simple capital-to-asset ratios. However, its weaknesses were significant: it treated all corporate loans identically regardless of credit rating (a AAA-rated corporate loan and a B-rated loan both required the same capital), it did not model default correlation, and it did not recognize netting for derivatives. These weaknesses set the stage for Basel II.

Capital Requirements and Tiers

Interpretation

The Accord required banks to keep capital equal to at least 8% of risk-weighted assets. Tier 1 capital consists of equity and noncumulative perpetual preferred stock, with goodwill subtracted from equity. Tier 2 capital (supplementary capital) includes cumulative perpetual preferred stock, certain 99-year debenture issues, and subordinated debt with an original life of more than five years. Equity capital is the most important because it absorbs losses immediately; if equity exceeds losses, the bank remains a going concern, but if losses exceed equity, the bank is insolvent. Tier 2 capital provides a cushion for depositors because it is subordinate to depositors' claims.

National supervisors could require higher capital levels, and many banks set internal targets above regulatory minimums. The requirement that at least 50% of required capital be Tier 1 (i.e., 4% of risk-weighted assets) and at least 2% of risk-weighted assets be common equity emphasized the importance of high-quality capital.

The G-30 Policy Recommendations

Interpretation

In 1993, a working group of end-users, dealers, academics, accountants, and lawyers published a report containing 20 risk management recommendations for derivatives dealers and end-users, plus four recommendations for legislators and regulators. Although not a regulatory document, it was highly influential in developing risk management practices. The report was based on a detailed survey of 80 dealers and 72 end-users worldwide.

Key recommendations included: (1) clearly defined risk management policies approved by senior management, ideally at board level; (2) daily marking to market of derivatives positions; (3) use of value at risk for measuring market risk and setting limits; (4) stress testing for extreme market conditions; (5) independent risk management functions separate from trading operations; (6) credit exposure assessment based on current replacement value plus potential future replacement cost; (7) aggregation of credit exposures reflecting enforceable netting agreements; (8) independence of credit limit setters from traders; (9) careful assessment of credit risk mitigation techniques like collateral and downgrade triggers; (10) employment of appropriately skilled individuals in trading and risk roles; (11) adequate systems for data capture, processing, settlement, and reporting; and (12) consistent accounting treatment for risk-management derivatives and the underlying risks.

Key Insight

The G-30 recommendations formalized many risk management practices that are now standard: VaR for market risk, independent risk management, daily marking-to-market, stress testing, and netting recognition. The report's emphasis on the independence of the risk management function from trading was ahead of its time and remains a cornerstone of effective risk governance. The recommendation that derivatives dealers use VaR helped legitimize Value at Risk as a risk measurement standard.

Analogy

The G-30 report is like the first comprehensive driving safety manual for a new type of vehicle (derivatives). Before it, drivers (dealers and end-users) had different practices—some tightened their seatbelts, some didn't. The manual standardized best practices: check your mirrors daily (mark-to-market), take a defensive driving course (VaR), practice emergency braking (stress testing), have a co-pilot who isn't the driver (independent risk management), and know your blind spots (aggregate credit exposure with netting).

Netting

Interpretation

Netting is a clause in the ISDA master agreement that states that in the event of a default, all transactions covered by the agreement are considered as a single transaction. If a company defaults on one transaction, it must default on all transactions covered by the master agreement. Netting substantially reduces credit risk.

Without netting, a bank's exposure on a portfolio of derivatives with a defaulting counterparty is the sum of all positive values: Σ max(Vᵢ, 0). With netting, it is max(ΣVᵢ, 0)—the exposure is the positive net value of the portfolio. This is analogous to the difference between a portfolio of options (without netting) and an option on a portfolio (with netting).

The 1988 Accord initially did not recognize netting. By 1995, however, netting had been successfully tested in courts in many jurisdictions, so the Accord was modified to allow banks to reduce credit equivalent totals when enforceable bilateral netting agreements were in place. The net replacement ratio (NRR) was calculated as the ratio of net current exposure to gross current exposure, and the credit equivalent amount was adjusted.

Net Replacement Ratio (NRR)
NRR = max(ΣVᵢ, 0) / Σmax(Vᵢ, 0)
Credit Equivalent Amount with Netting (1995 modification)
max(ΣVᵢ, 0) + (0.4 + 0.6 × NRR) × ΣaᵢLᵢ

Example (Example 25.4 from the text)

Consider a portfolio of three derivatives with a counterparty:

  • 3-year interest rate swap: Principal = 1,000; Current value = −60; Add-on = 5
  • 6-year foreign exchange forward: Principal = 1,000; Current value = 70; Add-on = 75
  • 9-month option on a stock: Principal = 500; Current value = 55; Add-on = 30

Current exposure with netting = −60 + 70 + 55 = 65. Current exposure without netting = 0 + 70 + 55 = 125. NRR = 65/125 = 0.52. Total add-ons = 5 + 75 + 30 = 110.

Credit equivalent with netting = 65 + (0.4 + 0.6 × 0.52) × 110 = 65 + 0.712 × 110 = 65 + 78.32 = 143.32.

Without netting, credit equivalent amount = 125 + 110 = 235.

If the counterparty is an OECD bank (risk weight 20%), risk-weighted assets with netting = 0.2 × 143.32 = 28.66. Without netting = 0.2 × 235 = 47.

Analogy

Netting is like a utility bill that combines electricity, gas, and water. If you owe $50 for electricity, are owed a $30 credit for gas (because you overpaid), and owe $20 for water, without netting the utility company would demand the full $50 + $20 = $70 if you missed a payment. With netting, they combine everything: you owe $50 + $20 − $30 = $40. If you default on electricity, you're considered to have defaulted on all services, so all bills are netted together. This reduces your total exposure and the utility's credit risk.

Key Insight

Netting transforms credit exposure from a sum of individual positive exposures to a single portfolio-level exposure. This reduction can be dramatic, especially when a portfolio contains offsetting positions. The 1995 modification to Basel I recognized this by allowing banks to reduce their credit equivalent totals, but the formula was still conservative—using NRR to partially apply add-ons even when netting reduced current exposure to zero.

The 1996 Amendment

Interpretation

The 1996 Amendment (implemented in 1998, sometimes called "BIS 98") introduced capital requirements for market risk associated with trading activities. It distinguished between the banking book (loans and other assets held to maturity) and the trading book (assets held for trading purposes, marked-to-market daily). The market risk capital charge applied to the trading book, including debt and equity securities, commodities, foreign exchange positions, and derivatives.

The Amendment outlined two approaches for calculating market risk capital: the standardized approach (assigning capital separately to debt, equity, FX, commodities, and options with no account of correlations) and an internal model-based approach using VaR. Most large banks preferred the internal model-based approach because it reflected diversification benefits and led to lower capital requirements.

The VaR measure used a 10-day time horizon and 99% confidence level. The capital requirement was max(VaR(t−1), m_c × VaR_avg) + SRC, where m_c is a multiplicative factor (minimum 3) and SRC is the specific risk charge. The first term covers broad market variable movements (interest rates, exchange rates, stock indices, commodity prices); the SRC covers company-specific risks (stock price movements, credit spread changes).

Back-testing requirements: regulators would verify the bank's one-day 99% VaR over the previous 250 days. If the number of exceptions (days where actual loss exceeds VaR) was fewer than 5, m_c was set at 3. Exceptions of 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 led to m_c values of 3.4, 3.5, 3.65, 3.75, and 3.85 respectively. Ten or more exceptions required m_c = 4.

The total capital required after the 1996 Amendment was the sum of credit risk capital (8% of RWA) plus market risk capital. For convenience, market risk capital was expressed as 12.5 times the market risk amount to create a "market risk RWA," allowing the combined capital to be expressed as 8% of (credit risk RWA + market risk RWA).

Market Risk Capital Requirement (Internal Model-Based Approach)
Capital = max(VaR(t−1), m_c × VaR_avg) + SRC

where VaR(t−1) is the previous day's 10-day 99% VaR, VaR_avg is the average 10-day 99% VaR over the past 60 days, m_c is the multiplicative factor (minimum 3, up to 4), and SRC is the specific risk charge.

Total Capital Requirement (Post-1996 Amendment)
Total Capital = 0.08 × (Credit Risk RWA + Market Risk RWA)

where Market Risk RWA = 12.5 × Market Risk Capital.

Example (VaR and the 10-day Rule)

Suppose a bank calculates a one-day 99% VaR of $5 million. The 10-day 99% VaR is approximately √10 × $5 = 3.162 × $5 = $15.81 million. If the average one-day 99% VaR over the past 60 days is $4 million, the average 10-day VaR is 3.162 × $4 = $12.65 million. With m_c = 3, the market risk capital (before SRC) = 3 × $12.65 = $37.95 million. This is approximately 9.48 times the average one-day VaR—a substantial multiple designed to provide a safety cushion for model risk and estimation error.

Analogy

The 1996 Amendment is like adding a separate insurance policy for a specific part of the business. Basel I was the general liability insurance (covering credit risk). The 1996 Amendment added a "trading risk" policy (covering market risk). Just as a restaurant might add fire insurance for the kitchen specifically, the 1996 Amendment added market risk capital for the trading book. The internal model-based approach is like having a professionally certified risk assessment instead of using the insurer's fixed formula—the bank can use its own sophisticated models, but the insurer (regulator) requires that models pass rigorous testing (back-testing) and may add a safety margin (multiplicative factor).

Key Insight

The 1996 Amendment represented a milestone: it allowed banks to use internal VaR models to determine regulatory capital for market risk, subject to back-testing and a multiplicative factor to account for model risk. This was a significant departure from the purely standardized, rule-based approach of Basel I. However, the reliance on a 10-day 99% VaR and the maximum multiplier of 4 meant that capital requirements could still be insufficient in extreme market conditions—a weakness that became apparent during the 2007-2008 crisis.

Basel II: Overview and Three Pillars

Interpretation

Basel II overhauled Basel I's credit risk framework and added operational risk capital. It was implemented by many banks in approximately 2007. The Basel II capital requirements applied to "internationally active" banks. In the United States, small regional banks were regulated under Basel IA (similar to Basel I); in Europe, all banks, large and small, were regulated under Basel II, including securities companies.

Basel II is based on three pillars: (1) Minimum Capital Requirements, (2) Supervisory Review, and (3) Market Discipline.

Pillar 1 changed the way credit risk capital is calculated in the banking book, left market risk capital unchanged from the 1996 Amendment, and introduced a new capital charge for operational risk. The general requirement that total capital equals 8% of risk-weighted assets remained. When a capital requirement is calculated directly (not through RWA), it is multiplied by 12.5 to convert it into an RWA-equivalent.

Pillar 2 (Supervisory Review) covers quantitative and qualitative aspects of risk management. Supervisors must ensure that banks maintain capital levels and use better risk management techniques. Banks are expected to hold more than the minimum capital, and regulators may require higher capital for risks not fully covered by Pillar 1 (e.g., concentration risk). Pillar 2 emphasizes early intervention when problems arise.

Pillar 3 (Market Discipline) requires banks to disclose more information about capital allocation and risk-taking, so that shareholders and potential shareholders can pressure banks to make sound risk decisions.

Total Capital Requirement Under Basel II
Total Capital = 0.08 × (Credit Risk RWA + Market Risk RWA + Operational Risk RWA)

Key Insight

Basel II introduced a three-pillar architecture that recognized the limitations of purely quantitative capital requirements. Supervisory review (Pillar 2) and market discipline (Pillar 3) added qualitative and disclosure-based mechanisms to complement the quantitative Pillar 1 requirements. This reflected a more mature understanding that capital alone cannot ensure bank safety—how banks manage risk and how the market disciplines them are equally important.

Analogy

Basel II's three pillars are like a three-legged stool. Pillar 1 (Minimum Capital) is one leg—the quantitative foundation. Pillar 2 (Supervisory Review) is the second leg—the supervisor's active engagement in assessing risk management quality. Pillar 3 (Market Discipline) is the third leg—transparency and disclosure that let the market reward good banks and punish bad ones. Without all three legs, the stool would be unstable. A bank might meet Pillar 1 capital requirements but engage in reckless risk-taking that the supervisor (Pillar 2) fails to catch and the market (Pillar 3) cannot see because of poor disclosure.

Credit Risk Capital Under Basel II

The Standardized Approach

Interpretation

The standardized approach is used by banks that are not sufficiently sophisticated to use internal ratings. It is similar to Basel I but with more granular risk weights based on external credit ratings. The OECD status of a country or bank is no longer important. Risk weights for countries, banks, and corporations range from 0% to 150%, as shown in Table 25.4. For example, a AAA-rated country has a 0% risk weight, while a B-rated country has a 100% weight, and a below-B-rated country has a 150% weight. Corporations with AAA to AA− ratings have a 20% weight, A+ to A− have 50%, BBB+ to BB− have 100%, B+ to B− have 150%, and below B− also have 150%. Unrated exposures typically have a 100% weight.

An interesting observation is that it may be better to have no credit rating at all than a very poor credit rating, because an unrated corporation has a 100% risk weight while a B− rated corporation has a 150% weight. The risk weight for mortgages in the standardized approach was 35%, and for other retail loans 75%.

The simple approach to collateral replaces the counterparty risk weight with the collateral's risk weight for the covered portion of the exposure. The comprehensive approach adjusts the exposure upward (for possible future increases) and the collateral value downward (for possible future decreases), then applies the counterparty risk weight to the new exposure.

Simple Approach for Collateral
RWA = Risk weight of collateral × Collateral value + Risk weight of counterparty × (Exposure − Collateral value)
Comprehensive Approach for Collateral
New Exposure = max(0, (1 + Adjustment_exposure) × Exposure − (1 − Adjustment_collateral) × Collateral Value)

Example (from the text)

Example 25.5: A bank has $100 million of loans to A-rated corporations (50% risk weight), $10 million of AAA-rated government bonds (0% risk weight), and $50 million of residential mortgages (35% risk weight). Under the Basel II standardized approach, RWA = 0.5 × 100 + 0.0 × 10 + 0.35 × 50 = 50 + 0 + 17.5 = $67.5 million. Under Basel I, this was $125 million.

Example 25.6 (Collateral): An $80 million exposure to a B+ rated counterparty is secured by $70 million of collateral consisting of A-rated company bonds. Counterparty risk weight = 150%. Collateral risk weight = 50%. Using the simple approach: RWA = 0.5 × 70 + 1.50 × 10 = 35 + 15 = $50 million. Using the comprehensive approach, with a +10% adjustment for the exposure and a −15% adjustment for the collateral: New exposure = 1.1 × 80 − 0.85 × 70 = 88 − 59.5 = $28.5 million. RWA = 1.50 × 28.5 = $42.75 million.

Key Insight

The standardized approach of Basel II made significant progress by basing risk weights on external credit ratings rather than OECD status. This was a more accurate reflection of credit risk. However, it introduced a new problem: reliance on external credit rating agencies, which later proved to be unreliable during the financial crisis (particularly for mortgage-backed securities). Also, the treatment of unrated exposures (100% risk weight for corporations) meant that firms could be incentivized to avoid getting a rating.

The IRB Approach

Interpretation

The Internal Ratings Based (IRB) approach allows banks to use their own internal models to estimate credit risk components. The model underlying the IRB approach is based on the one-factor Gaussian copula model of time to default. Regulators base the capital requirement on VaR with a one-year time horizon and 99.9% confidence level. Expected losses are typically covered by the way banks price their products, so capital needed is the difference between VaR and expected loss—the "unexpected loss."

The key variables are: PD (probability of default within one year), EAD (exposure at default in dollars), and LGD (loss given default as a proportion of exposure). The worst-case default rate (WCDR) is calculated using the Gaussian copula formula. For corporate, sovereign, and bank exposures, the correlation parameter ρ is a decreasing function of PD. For retail exposures, ρ is either fixed (0.15 for residential mortgages, 0.04 for qualifying revolving exposures) or a decreasing function of PD for other retail.

Under the Foundation IRB approach, banks supply PD while LGD, EAD, and M (maturity) are set by supervisors. LGD is 45% for senior claims and 75% for subordinated claims. M is 2.5 years in most cases. Under the Advanced IRB approach, banks may supply their own estimates of PD, LGD, EAD, and M, subject to regulatory approval.

Worst-Case Default Rate (WCDR) — One-Factor Gaussian Copula
WCDR = N[ (N⁻¹(PD) + √ρ N⁻¹(0.999)) / √(1 − ρ) ]
Correlation for Corporate, Sovereign, and Bank Exposures
ρ = 0.12(1 + exp(−50 × PD)) (approximately)
Correlation for Other Retail Exposures
ρ = 0.03 + 0.13 × exp(−35 × PD) (approximately)
Capital Required for a Counterparty (Corporate, Sovereign, Bank)
Capital = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA

where MA = maturity adjustment = [1 + (M − 2.5) × b] / (1 − 1.5 × b), and b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(PD)]².

RWA under IRB Approach
RWA = 12.5 × EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA
Capital for Retail Exposures
Capital = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) (no maturity adjustment)

Examples (from the text)

Example 25.7 (Corporate loans): A bank has $100 million of loans to A-rated corporations. PD = 0.1%, LGD = 60%, M = 2.5 years. b = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × ln(0.001)]² = [0.11852 − 0.05478 × (−6.9078)]² = [0.11852 + 0.37840]² = 0.2469. MA = 1 / (1 − 1.5 × 0.2469) = 1 / (1 − 0.3704) = 1 / 0.6296 = 1.59. From Table 25.6, WCDR = 3.4%. RWA = 12.5 × 100 × 0.6 × (0.034 − 0.001) × 1.59 = 12.5 × 60 × 0.033 × 1.59 = $39.3 million. Compare: Basel I RWA = $100 million; Basel II standardized = $50 million.

Example 25.8 (Residential mortgages): A bank has $50 million of residential mortgages. PD = 0.5%, LGD = 20%. For residential mortgages, ρ = 0.15. WCDR = N[(N⁻¹(0.005) + √0.15 N⁻¹(0.999)) / √(0.85)] = 0.067 (approximately). RWA = 12.5 × 50 × 0.2 × (0.067 − 0.005) = 12.5 × 50 × 0.2 × 0.062 = $7.75 million. Compare: Basel I RWA = $25 million; Basel II standardized = $17.5 million.

Analogy

The IRB approach is like a sophisticated car insurance company letting good drivers calculate their own premiums based on their telematics data. The insurance company provides the framework (the formula), but the driver supplies their own mileage, braking patterns, and driving times. Foundation IRB is like letting drivers supply their accident probability (PD) while the insurer sets the repair costs (LGD), the value of the car (EAD), and the policy duration (M). Advanced IRB is like the driver supplying all the inputs. The formula ensures that even the best driver pays something (PD floor), and the WCDR is the "worst-case accident rate" the insurer is 99.9% confident won't be exceeded.

Key Insight

The IRB approach was a major innovation because it allowed banks to use internal models to determine capital requirements for credit risk, similar to the internal model approach for market risk. This reflected the belief that sophisticated banks could better assess their own risks than regulators could. However, it also created incentives for banks to underestimate PD and LGD to reduce capital requirements—a problem that became apparent in the financial crisis. The use of the Gaussian copula model and the assumption of a single factor correlation structure were also criticized for being overly simplistic and not capturing tail risks.

Operational Risk Capital Under Basel II

Interpretation

Basel II introduced for the first time a capital charge for operational risk—the risk of losses from failed internal processes, people, and systems, or external events. Three approaches were specified:

1. Basic Indicator Approach: The simplest. Operational risk capital = average annual gross income over the last three years × 0.15. Gross income is net interest income plus non-interest income, and years with negative gross income are excluded.

2. Standardized Approach: Similar to the Basic Indicator approach but applies different factors to gross income from different business lines (e.g., corporate finance, trading, retail banking, commercial banking, etc.).

3. Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA): Banks use their own internal models to calculate the operational risk loss that they are 99.9% certain will not be exceeded in one year. Operational risk capital = this loss minus expected loss. The AMA allowed banks to recognize the risk-mitigating impact of insurance contracts subject to certain conditions. However, because there was very little consistency between calculations at different banks, it has now been replaced by the standardized measurement approach described in Section 20.4.

Analogy

Operational risk capital is like having a contingency fund for accidents in a factory. The Basic Indicator Approach is like setting aside 15% of your annual budget for any accidents regardless of what department you run. The Standardized Approach is more nuanced: it charges different percentages of departmental budgets depending on the department's accident history (e.g., trading and manufacturing might have higher factors than administration). The Advanced Measurement Approach is like hiring actuaries to model all potential accidents using your own historical data and safety procedures to calculate a 99.9% worst-case loss amount.

Key Insight

The addition of operational risk capital to the regulatory framework acknowledged that banks face material risks beyond credit and market risk. The 1995 Barings collapse (caused by unauthorized trading), operational failures, and fraud demonstrated that operational risk can be catastrophic. However, the AMA's flexibility in letting banks use internal models for operational risk created significant inconsistency, and its replacement by the standardized measurement approach reflects the Basel Committee's recognition that comparability and consistency are essential for credible regulation.

Pillar 2: Supervisory Review

Interpretation

Pillar 2 of Basel II sets out four key principles for supervisory review:

1. Banks should have a process for assessing their overall capital adequacy in relation to their risk profile and a strategy for maintaining capital levels.

2. Supervisors should review and evaluate banks' internal capital adequacy assessments and strategies, as well as their ability to monitor and ensure compliance with regulatory capital ratios. They should take appropriate supervisory action if not satisfied.

3. Supervisors should expect banks to operate above the minimum regulatory capital and should have the ability to require banks to hold capital in excess of the minimum.

4. Supervisors should seek to intervene early to prevent capital from falling below minimum levels and require rapid remedial action if capital is not maintained or restored.

The Basel Committee suggested that regulators pay particular attention to interest rate risk in the banking book, credit risk, and operational risk. Key credit risk issues include stress tests, default definitions, credit risk concentration, and the risks from collateral, guarantees, and credit derivatives. The Committee also stressed transparency and accountability in supervisory procedures, especially when supervisors exercise discretion or set capital above the minimum.

Key Insight

Pillar 2 recognizes that a bank's risk profile cannot be fully captured by the Pillar 1 formulas. Bank supervisors must exercise judgment, evaluate risk management quality, and require additional capital when warranted. Pillar 2 also emphasizes early intervention as a critical component of effective supervision—waiting until a bank's capital has fallen below minimums is far too late. The principle that banks should operate above the minimum capital level was particularly prescient, as the 2007-2008 crisis showed that banks at the bare regulatory minimum were extremely vulnerable.

Analogy

Pillar 2 is like having an air traffic controller who doesn't just set altitude and speed rules (Pillar 1) but actively reviews each pilot's flight plan, weather conditions, and fuel requirements. The controller can require a specific plane to carry extra fuel (additional capital) if the route is risky, and can intervene early if a plane strays off course—much better than waiting until it runs out of fuel and crashes.

Pillar 3: Market Discipline

Interpretation

Pillar 3 increases the disclosure requirements for banks about their risk assessment procedures and capital adequacy. The logic is that if shareholders and potential shareholders have more information about a bank's risk-taking and capital allocation decisions, they will exert market pressure on the bank to make sound decisions. However, the extent to which regulators can force disclosure varies by jurisdiction. Banks are unlikely to ignore directives from supervisors, especially because in some cases banks must increase disclosure to be allowed to use particular capital calculation methodologies.

Regulatory disclosures differ from accounting disclosures and do not necessarily need to be made in annual reports. It is largely left to the bank to choose disclosures that are material and relevant. Required disclosures include: (1) entities in the banking group to which Basel II applies and adjustments for non-applied entities; (2) terms and conditions of capital instruments; (3) the list of instruments constituting Tier 1 capital and the amount from each; (4) total Tier 2 capital; (5) capital requirements for credit, market, and operational risk; and (6) other general information on risks to which the bank is exposed.

Key Insight

Pillar 3 embodies the principle that transparency is a complement to capital regulation. In a world of incomplete information, the market cannot properly discipline banks. By requiring disclosure, Pillar 3 enables shareholders, creditors, and counterparties to make more informed decisions, rewarding well-managed banks with lower funding costs and penalizing poorly-managed banks. However, the effectiveness of Pillar 3 depends on the quality and comparability of disclosures, which the financial crisis revealed to have significant gaps.

Analogy

Pillar 3 is like restaurant health inspection scores posted in the window. The restaurant is required to publish information about its kitchen cleanliness (risk management procedures) and compliance with health codes (capital adequacy). Customers (shareholders, creditors) see the score and decide whether to eat there. A high score (good disclosure, strong risk management) attracts more customers; a low score drives them away, forcing the restaurant to improve or go out of business. Just as mandatory disclosure of health scores improves overall restaurant hygiene through market pressure, Pillar 3 aims to improve bank risk management through market discipline.

Key Takeaways

Key Takeaways

  • Basel I (1988 Accord): The first international risk-based capital standard. Introduced the Cooke ratio requiring capital of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets, with risk weights categories: 0% for OECD government debt, 20% for OECD banks, 50% for uninsured residential mortgages, and 100% for corporate loans. Capital split into Tier 1 (equity, noncumulative perpetual preferred) and Tier 2 (subordinated debt, cumulative preferred), with at least 4% of RWA in Tier 1.
  • Current Exposure Method (CEM): For OTC derivatives, credit equivalent amount = max(V, 0) + aL, where V is current value to the bank, a is an add-on factor based on asset class and maturity, and L is the principal.
  • Netting: Under an ISDA master agreement with enforceable netting, the credit equivalent amount was reduced using the net replacement ratio: max(ΣVᵢ, 0) + (0.4 + 0.6 × NRR) × ΣaᵢLᵢ. Netting transforms exposure from a sum of positive exposures to the positive net portfolio value.
  • 1996 Amendment: Added market risk capital for the trading book. Allowed internal model-based approach using 10-day 99% VaR: Capital = max(VaR(t−1), m_c × VaR_avg) + SRC, with m_c minimum of 3 (increasing based on back-test exceptions up to 4). Total capital = 8% × (Credit RWA + Market RWA) where market RWA = 12.5 × market risk capital.
  • Basel II Three Pillars: (1) Minimum Capital Requirements (credit risk RWA + market risk RWA + operational risk RWA); (2) Supervisory Review—emphasizing early intervention and evaluating bank risk management quality; (3) Market Discipline—requiring disclosure of risk and capital information.
  • Credit Risk under Basel II: Three approaches: Standardized (based on external credit ratings, with more granular risk weights than Basel I), Foundation IRB (bank supplies PD; LGD, EAD, M are supervisory), and Advanced IRB (bank supplies PD, LGD, EAD, M). Capital based on one-year 99.9% WCDR from Gaussian copula model: Capital = EAD × LGD × (WCDR − PD) × MA, with MA = maturity adjustment and WCDR calculated using correlation ρ that decreases with PD for corporate exposures.
  • Operational Risk: Basel II introduced the first capital charge for operational risk. Approaches: Basic Indicator (15% of average gross income), Standardized (different factors for different business lines), and Advanced Measurement Approach (internal models for 99.9% worst-case loss). AMA required the capital to equal the 99.9% loss minus expected loss.
  • Basel II's Weaknesses: The reliance on external credit ratings, the complexity of the IRB approach, the use of the Gaussian copula model with its limitations, incentives for regulatory arbitrage, and insufficient capital for many trading-book exposures all contributed to the 2007-2008 crisis. Chapters 26 and 27 address post-crisis reforms (Basel III and related developments).
  • Solvency II: A regulatory framework for insurance companies, broadly similar to Basel II's three-pillar approach, implemented by the European Union in 2016. Though not detailed in depth in this chapter, it represents the same evolutionary philosophy of risk-based capital regulation applied to the insurance industry.
  • Historical perspective: Basel I, Basel II, and Solvency II each built on prior frameworks, preserving useful elements while addressing weaknesses. Understanding this evolutionary development is essential for understanding the current regulatory environment and the reforms implemented since the Global Financial Crisis.

Final Takeaway

The evolution from Basel I to Basel II represents a fundamental shift from a simple one-size-fits-all approach to a more sophisticated, risk-sensitive framework that incorporates internal models, external ratings, and a three-pillar structure. Yet the 2007 crisis demonstrated that even this sophisticated framework was inadequate. The Basel Committee's response—Basel III and other reforms covered in Chapters 26 and 27—is the next chapter in the ongoing evolution of bank regulation.
