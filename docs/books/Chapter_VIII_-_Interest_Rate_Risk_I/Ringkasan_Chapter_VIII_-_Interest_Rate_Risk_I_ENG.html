1. Introduction to Interest Rate Risk

1.1 The Asset-Transformation Function and Interest Rate Risk

Interpretation

Financial institutions (FIs) perform a critical asset-transformation function in the economy: they borrow short-term funds (liabilities) and lend long-term funds (assets). This maturity mismatch is not accidental—it is the very mechanism through which FIs earn profits. By taking in deposits and other short-term liabilities with relatively low interest rates and lending those funds out at longer maturities with higher interest rates, FIs earn a spread. However, this fundamental business model creates an inherent vulnerability: when interest rates move, the value and income generated by the asset side and the cost of the liability side respond differently, exposing the FI to interest rate risk.

Chapter 7 established that this exposure arises naturally from the maturity mismatch embedded in the asset-transformation function. When an FI holds assets with longer maturities than its liabilities, a rise in interest rates will reduce the market value of its assets more than the market value of its liabilities, eroding the FI's net worth. Conversely, when interest rates fall, the FI may face reinvestment risk—the risk that funds coming due must be reinvested at lower rates. The measurement and management of this risk are therefore central to the survival and profitability of any FI. This chapter and Chapter 9 present the primary techniques FIs use to measure interest rate risk exposure. The methods range from the simple repricing gap model (a book value accounting approach) to the more sophisticated maturity and duration models that incorporate market values.

The choice of measurement model matters enormously. Smaller depository institutions, which constitute the vast majority of banks in the United States, have historically relied on the repricing model because of its simplicity and intuitive appeal. Larger banks, which hold the vast majority of total banking industry assets, have adopted more comprehensive market value–based models such as the duration gap model. Regulators, particularly through the Bank for International Settlements (BIS), have encouraged the adoption of more comprehensive models. Understanding the strengths and weaknesses of each approach is essential for anyone managing or regulating financial institutions.

Example

Consider a community bank that takes in $100 million in one-year certificates of deposit (CDs) at 2% interest and lends that money out as 30-year fixed-rate mortgages at 5% interest. The bank earns a spread of 3%—or $3 million annually—on this transformation. However, if interest rates rise and the CDs mature, the bank must replace them with new CDs at, say, 4%, compressing the spread to just 1% ($1 million). If rates rise further, the spread could turn negative. This simple scenario illustrates how the maturity mismatch at the heart of asset transformation creates interest rate risk. The bank's net interest income (NII)—the difference between interest income earned on assets and interest expense paid on liabilities—is the profit variable most directly affected by this risk.

Analogy

Think of an FI as a fruit vendor who buys oranges in bulk at a wholesale price each morning and sells them throughout the day at a retail price. The vendor's profit depends on buying low and selling high. If wholesale prices rise unexpectedly (interest rates on liabilities increase), the vendor's profit margin shrinks unless retail prices can be adjusted immediately (interest rates on assets repricing). But if the vendor has already committed to selling some oranges at a fixed price for a week (fixed-rate long-term assets), the margin can be squeezed severely. The vendor is exposed to "price risk" just as an FI is exposed to interest rate risk—the core problem is the mismatch between the timing of cost changes and revenue changes.

Key Insight

The very business model of FIs—borrowing short and lending long—guarantees exposure to interest rate risk. This is not a bug but a feature of financial intermediation; the challenge lies in measuring and managing the risk rather than eliminating it. A bank that perfectly matched the maturities of assets and liabilities would eliminate interest rate risk but would also likely eliminate its profit margin, since long-term rates typically exceed short-term rates. FIs must therefore strike a delicate balance between profitability and risk.

1.2 The Federal Reserve's Monetary Policy and Interest Rate Risk

Interpretation

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States, and its monetary policy decisions are the single most important external determinant of interest rate levels and movements. Through daily open market operations—buying and selling Treasury securities—the Fed influences the money supply, inflation, and particularly short-term interest rates. The federal funds rate, the rate at which banks lend reserves to each other overnight, is the Fed's primary policy target. Changes to this target ripple through the entire term structure of interest rates, affecting everything from three-month Treasury bill yields to 30-year mortgage rates.

During the 2008–2015 period, the Fed took extraordinary actions to address the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression. It dropped its target federal funds rate to a range between zero and 0.25% and lowered its discount window rate to 0.5% (through 2010) and then 0.75% (through 2015). Critically, the Fed communicated that it intended to keep rates at these historically low levels for an extended period as the economy recovered. This forward guidance reduced uncertainty about future interest rate movements, which in turn reduced the level of interest rate risk FIs faced during this period. When rates are stable and expected to remain so, the gap between when assets and liabilities reprice matters less because the interest rates at which they reprice are not expected to change dramatically.

However, interest rate risk never disappears—it is merely deferred or compressed. As the economy recovers and inflation pressures build, the Fed is compelled to raise rates. Indeed, the Fed raised its target rate seven times between December 2015 and June 2018. The critical question for FI managers is not whether the Fed will act but when and by how much. This uncertainty itself is the essence of interest rate risk. Moreover, the Fed's influence extends beyond U.S. borders. The Bank of Japan, the European Central Bank, and several smaller European central banks have even ventured into negative interest rate territory, where lenders pay borrowers—a once-unthinkable scenario that further complicates interest rate risk management globally.

\[ \text{Fed Funds Target Rate (Dec 2008 – 2015)} = 0\% \text{ to } 0.25\% \]
\[ \text{Discount Window Rate (2008–2010)} = 0.50\% \]
\[ \text{Discount Window Rate (2010–2015)} = 0.75\% \]

Example

In December 2008, with the U.S. economy in freefall, the Fed announced a historic reduction of its target federal funds rate to a range of 0% to 0.25%. This was the lowest level since the 1940s. A small community bank with a negative one-year repricing gap (more liabilities repricing than assets) might have expected to suffer when rates fell. However, because the Fed's announcement signaled that rates would remain near zero for years, the bank could plan with confidence: it knew its liability costs would stay low, and while its asset yields were also low, the stability of the environment meant less uncertainty about future net interest income. The risk was deferred to the moment the Fed signaled a change in policy. In December 2015, when the Fed raised rates for the first time in a decade, banks that had grown complacent during the low-rate environment suddenly faced the prospect of rising liability costs and potentially sticky asset yields—a classic repricing risk scenario.

Analogy

Imagine a sailor navigating a ship through a calm harbor. The Fed's low-rate policy is like a prolonged period of calm weather—the sailor knows conditions will not change drastically, so the risk of capsizing is low. But the sailor must constantly watch the horizon for storms. When the Fed signals a rate increase, it is like dark clouds appearing—the storm may not arrive immediately, but the sailor must prepare. The calm period does not eliminate the need for navigation skills; it merely postpones the moment when those skills will be tested. Similarly, low and stable interest rates do not eliminate interest rate risk—they compress it into the future, when rates eventually move.

Key Insight

Stable interest rates do not mean zero interest rate risk—they mean deferred interest rate risk. FI managers who treat low-rate environments as risk-free periods are simply accumulating a larger exposure that will materialize when the Fed eventually changes course. The 2015–2018 rate hike cycle demonstrated that the transition from a low-rate to a rising-rate environment is precisely when repricing gaps, duration mismatches, and other interest rate risk exposures become most consequential.

Key Takeaways

  • FIs create interest rate risk through the asset-transformation function: borrowing short-term and lending long-term creates inherent maturity mismatches.
  • The Federal Reserve's monetary policy is the primary external driver of interest rate levels and movements, affecting short-term rates directly and longer-term rates through the term structure.
  • The 2008–2015 period of near-zero interest rates reduced but did not eliminate interest rate risk; the 2015–2018 rate hikes demonstrated the resurfacing of this risk.
  • The repricing model (this chapter), the maturity model (Appendix 8A), and the duration model (Chapter 9) are the three primary approaches to measuring interest rate risk exposure.

2. The Level and Movement of Interest Rates

2.1 The Federal Reserve's Open Market Operations

Interpretation

The Fed conducts monetary policy primarily through daily open market operations—the buying and selling of Treasury bonds and Treasury bills. When the Fed buys securities, it injects reserves into the banking system, increasing the money supply and putting downward pressure on short-term interest rates. When it sells securities, it withdraws reserves, reducing the money supply and pushing rates upward. These operations target the federal funds rate, which is the overnight lending rate between banks. Changes in the federal funds rate transmit through the financial system to affect a wide range of interest rates: Treasury yields, corporate bond rates, mortgage rates, CD rates, and commercial paper rates.

The Fed's monetary policy strategy oscillates between two poles: tightening and easing. When the Fed tightens, it raises interest rates to slow down an overheating economy, curb inflation, and reduce excessive borrowing and spending. The normal result is a decrease in business and household spending, particularly spending financed by credit. Conversely, when the Fed eases (expansionary policy), it lowers interest rates to stimulate borrowing, investment, and consumption. The December 2008 decision to cut rates to near-zero was a dramatic example of expansionary policy in response to a severe recession. The choice between tightening and easing is a constant balancing act: tighten too much and the economy stalls; ease too much and inflation accelerates.

Importantly, while the Fed's actions target short-term rates—especially the federal funds rate—changes in short-term rates typically feed through to the entire term structure of interest rates. The linkages between short-term and long-term rates are explained by theories of the term structure (expectations hypothesis, liquidity premium theory, and market segmentation theory), which are explored in Appendix 8B. This transmission mechanism means that a Fed policy decision affects the entire yield curve, with consequences for every FI's cost of funds and return on assets. Understanding this transmission mechanism is essential for FI managers who must forecast interest rate movements as part of their risk management process.

Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanism
\[ \text{Fed Open Market Operations} \rightarrow \text{Money Supply} \rightarrow \text{Federal Funds Rate} \rightarrow \text{Term Structure of Interest Rates} \rightarrow \text{Economic Decisions (Consume vs. Save)} \]

Example

Consider the period from 1965 to 2021, which saw dramatic swings in three-month Treasury bill rates. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the Fed, under Chairman Paul Volcker, raised the federal funds rate to double-digit levels to break the back of double-digit inflation. The three-month T-bill rate peaked at over 16% in the early 1980s. Conversely, in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, the T-bill rate fell to near zero and remained below 1% for years. For an FI, these swings had profound implications. In the high-rate environment of the early 1980s, an FI with a positive repricing gap (more assets repricing than liabilities) would have seen net interest income surge as assets repriced upward faster than liabilities. Conversely, an FI with a negative gap would have seen its funding costs explode relative to its asset yields. The opposite dynamics played out in the post-2008 zero-rate environment.

Analogy

Think of the Fed as the conductor of an orchestra. The federal funds rate is the baton. When the conductor lowers the baton (cuts rates), the string section (short-term rates) responds immediately, and the brass and percussion sections (longer-term rates) follow with some delay and variation. The orchestra's overall sound—like the term structure of interest rates—is shaped by the conductor's cues, but each section has its own characteristics and timing. Similarly, the Fed's policy actions set the tone for the entire interest rate environment, but the exact impact on each maturity segment depends on a complex transmission mechanism that FI managers must understand to forecast their own institution's exposure.

Key Insight

Interest rate risk is fundamentally driven by the uncertainty of Fed policy actions. The direction, timing, magnitude, and speed of Fed rate changes all matter for FI profitability, and the relative movements of short-term versus long-term rates (the shape of the yield curve) are as important as the level of rates themselves. FIs must therefore analyze not just the current rate environment but also anticipate the Fed's future policy trajectory, which requires monitoring economic data, Fed statements, and the policy signals embedded in the yield curve.

2.2 Financial Market Integration and Global Interest Rate Transmission

Interpretation

Over the past several decades, financial markets around the world have become increasingly integrated. Advances in technology, deregulation, the growth of multinational banking, and the proliferation of complex financial instruments have linked national markets more closely than ever before. This integration has profound implications for interest rate risk management. When markets were segmented, the Fed could exert relatively direct control over U.S. interest rates because capital flows between countries were limited. Today, however, financial market integration increases the speed with which interest rate changes and associated volatility are transmitted among countries, making the control of U.S. interest rates by the Federal Reserve more difficult and less certain.

Consider the global bond market. When the Fed signals that it will raise U.S. rates, global investors rebalance their portfolios, selling bonds in other countries and buying U.S. Treasury securities. This capital flow affects interest rates worldwide: yields in other countries rise as bond prices fall, while the dollar strengthens. Conversely, when foreign central banks ease policy, the effects spill over into U.S. markets. This interconnectedness means that an FI manager in the United States must understand not just the Fed's actions but also the policies of the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan, and other major central banks. Even statements by the Fed chair before Congress—mere hints of policy changes—can have major effects on world interest rates, foreign exchange rates, and international stock prices.

The globalization of financial markets has made the measurement and management of interest rate risk a prominent concern for modern FI managers. A regional U.S. bank may find its funding costs influenced by a sovereign debt crisis in Europe, a policy shift by the Bank of Japan, or an unexpected rate move by the European Central Bank. The BIS, recognizing this complexity, requires depository institutions to maintain interest rate risk measurement systems that assess the effects of rate changes on both earnings and economic value, and to identify excessive exposures that might arise. The days when an FI manager could focus solely on domestic interest rates are over.

Global Interest Rate Transmission
\[ \text{Policy Change in One Country} \rightarrow \text{Capital Flows} \rightarrow \text{Exchange Rate Adjustments} \rightarrow \text{Interest Rate Changes Worldwide} \rightarrow \text{FI Cost of Funds and Returns Affected} \]

Example

In March 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic struck the global economy, the Fed slashed the federal funds rate to a target range of 0% to 0.25%. This action did not occur in isolation. The European Central Bank, the Bank of England, and other major central banks simultaneously implemented their own emergency rate cuts and quantitative easing programs. Three-month CD rates in the United States plunged in response. For a global bank with operations in multiple countries, the synchronized easing meant that asset yields and liability costs were falling across all markets simultaneously—though not necessarily at the same speed. An FI holding assets in euros and liabilities in dollars would have experienced different rate adjustments on each side of its balance sheet, illustrating the additional complexity of interest rate risk in a globally integrated financial system. Similarly, when the Fed under Chair Jerome Powell signals a potential rate change before Congress, the immediate reaction in global bond markets can be observed within minutes, demonstrating how rapidly policy signals transmit across borders.

Analogy

Think of the global financial system as a network of interconnected swimming pools. In the past, each pool (country) had its own water supply and drainage, and the water level (interest rate) in each pool was controlled locally. Today, the pools are connected by pipes (capital flows), so when the Fed adds water to the U.S. pool, some of that water flows into other pools, raising their levels too. An FI is like a swimmer with one foot in each of two connected pools—when the water level changes in one pool, it affects the swimmer's balance, but the exact effect depends on the size of the connecting pipes, the pressure in each pool, and the speed at which water reaches equilibrium. The FI manager must monitor not just the local pool but the entire network.

Key Insight

Financial market integration has transformed interest rate risk from a domestic concern into a global one. The Fed's ability to control U.S. rates is now constrained by global capital flows, and FI managers must track international monetary policy developments to understand the full scope of their interest rate risk exposure. The BIS requirement for Interest rate risk measurement systems reflects this reality: modern FIs need systems that capture the impact of global interest rate movements on both earnings and economic value.

Key Takeaways

  • The Fed influences interest rates primarily through open market operations that target the federal funds rate, with effects that transmit through the term structure to affect all maturities.
  • Monetary policy can be expansionary (lowering rates to stimulate borrowing and spending) or contractionary (raising rates to slow the economy and curb inflation).
  • Financial market integration has increased the speed and extent to which interest rate changes and volatility are transmitted among countries, making domestic rate control more difficult.
  • Global factors—including the policies of foreign central banks and international capital flows—now play a substantial role in determining domestic interest rates and FI interest rate risk.
  • The BIS requires FIs to have measurement systems that assess the effects of interest rate changes on both earnings and economic value.

3. The Repricing Model

3.1 Defining the Repricing Gap

Interpretation

The repricing gap model—also known as the funding gap model—is the foundational tool for measuring an FI's interest rate risk exposure. It is essentially a book value accounting cash flow analysis that focuses on the difference between the interest income earned on an FI's assets and the interest expense paid on its liabilities (net interest income, or NII) over a specific time horizon. The model's simplicity is both its greatest strength and its most significant limitation. Smaller U.S. depository institutions—which represent the vast majority of banks—still use this model as their primary measure of interest rate risk, and U.S. bank regulators have historically based their evaluations of bank interest rate risk on it.

The core concept is straightforward: categorize every asset and liability on the balance sheet as either rate-sensitive or rate-insensitive based on whether its interest rate will be repriced within a specified maturity bucket. A rate-sensitive asset (RSA) or rate-sensitive liability (RSL) is one that will be repriced at or near current market interest rates within a certain time horizon. Repricing can occur through a rollover (a loan paid off at maturity and replaced with a new loan at current rates) or because the instrument is a variable-rate instrument (e.g., a variable-rate mortgage that resets quarterly based on movements in the prime rate). The repricing gap for each maturity bucket is the difference between RSAs and RSLs in that bucket: Gap = RSA − RSL. A positive gap (RSA > RSL) exposes the FI to reinvestment risk—the risk that funds coming due must be reinvested at lower rates. A negative gap (RSA < RSL) exposes the FI to refinancing risk—the risk that the cost of rolling over liabilities will rise above the returns earned on assets.

Banks report their repricing gaps quarterly on call reports in interest rate sensitivity reports, typically broken down into six maturity buckets: one day; more than 1 day to 3 months; more than 3 months to 6 months; more than 6 months to 12 months; more than 1 year to 5 years; and more than 5 years. Table 8–1 in the text presents a classic example. This structure allows regulators and managers to see at a glance where the FI's maturity mismatches are concentrated. The cumulative gap (CGAP) sums the gaps across multiple buckets—most commonly the one-year repricing gap—providing an aggregate measure of rate sensitivity over a specific planning horizon.

Repricing Gap for a Maturity Bucket
\[ GAP_i = RSA_i - RSL_i \]
Change in Net Interest Income
\[ \Delta NII_i = (GAP_i) \times \Delta R_i = (RSA_i - RSL_i) \times \Delta R_i \]
Cumulative Repricing Gap
\[ CGAP = \sum_{i=1}^{n} GAP_i \]
Change in NII with CGAP
\[ \Delta NII = CGAP \times \Delta R \]

Example

Consider a simple FI with the following balance sheet positions. In the one-day bucket, the FI has $20 million in rate-sensitive assets and $30 million in rate-sensitive liabilities, producing a gap of −$10 million. Assets and liabilities repriced each day are likely to be interbank borrowings on the federal funds or repurchase agreement markets. If short-term interest rates rise by 1%, the annualized change in the FI's net interest income is calculated as:

ΔNII = (−$10 million) × 0.01 = −$100,000

This negative gap exposes the FI to refinancing risk: interest expense rises by $300,000 (3% of $30 million × 1% actually $30M × 1% = $300,000) while interest income rises by only $200,000 ($20M × 1%), producing a net loss of $100,000. In contrast, if this FI had a positive $20 million gap in the 6-month to 12-month bucket, it would face reinvestment risk: a drop in rates would lower net interest income because interest income would decrease by more than interest expense.

Expanding to the full six-bucket structure from Table 8–1, with buckets showing gaps of −$10M (one day), −$10M (1 day–3 months), −$15M (3–6 months), +$20M (6–12 months), +$10M (1–5 years), and +$5M (over 5 years), the cumulative one-year repricing gap is:

CGAP = (−$10M) + (−$10M) + (−$15M) + $20M = −$15 million

If the average interest rate change affecting assets and liabilities repriced within the year is 1%, the expected change in net interest income is:

ΔNII = CGAP × ΔR = (−$15M) × 0.01 = −$150,000

Analogy

Think of a seesaw. One side of the seesaw represents an FI's rate-sensitive assets and the other side represents its rate-sensitive liabilities. When the seesaw is balanced (GAP = 0), a change in interest rates produces equal changes on both sides, and net interest income stays flat. But when one side is heavier (a positive or negative gap), the seesaw tips. If rates rise and RSAs exceed RSLs (positive gap), the asset side rises faster than the liability side, boosting net interest income—like a heavier child on the seesaw lifting the lighter child higher. If rates fall and the gap is positive, the asset side falls faster, reducing net interest income. The seesaw's tilt direction and speed depend on both the size of the gap (how much heavier one side is) and the magnitude of the rate change (how hard the push is). Just as a seesaw's motion is amplified when the weight difference is large, an FI's net interest income changes are amplified when the absolute value of CGAP is large.

Key Insight

The repricing gap model is fundamentally a book value accounting framework: it measures how changes in interest rates affect the income statement (net interest income) but completely ignores the balance sheet market value effects of interest rate changes. This means the model captures only one dimension of interest rate risk—the income effect—while missing the capital value effect that can destabilize an FI's net worth. A bank could show a zero repricing gap and thus appear fully hedged, yet still face substantial interest rate risk because the market values of its assets and liabilities respond differently to rate changes.

3.2 Rate-Sensitive Assets and Liabilities

Interpretation

Determining which assets and liabilities are rate-sensitive is the most critical and judgment-intensive step in applying the repricing model. The FI manager must ask a simple question for each balance sheet item: Will or can this asset or liability have its interest rate changed within the next year? If the answer is yes, the item is rate-sensitive; if no, it is not. Consider the asset side of a typical balance sheet. Short-term consumer loans with one-year maturity are rate-sensitive because they repriced at the end of the year. Three-month T-bills are rate-sensitive because they are repriced on rollover every three months. Six-month T-notes are similarly rate-sensitive. Perhaps counterintuitively, a 30-year floating-rate mortgage whose rate resets every nine months is classified as rate-sensitive within a one-year repricing horizon, even though the instrument has a 30-year original maturity. The classification depends not on original maturity but on the timing of the next rate reset relative to the repricing horizon. Long-term fixed-rate instruments—such as 10-year fixed-rate mortgages or 3-year Treasury bonds—are by contrast rate-insensitive within a one-year horizon because their interest rates will not change during that period.

The liability side presents similar classification challenges. Three-month CDs, three-month bankers acceptances, six-month commercial paper, and one-year time deposits all clearly meet the one-year rate-sensitivity test. The classification of demand deposits and passbook savings accounts, however, is genuinely contentious. Against inclusion as rate-sensitive liabilities: demand deposits pay zero explicit interest by regulation, and NOW account rates do not fluctuate directly with general interest rate changes. Demand deposits often act as core deposits—stable, long-term sources of funds that FIs rely on for liquidity. However, a powerful argument favors inclusion: demand deposits pay implicit interest because banks do not charge fees that fully cover their checking-service costs. When market interest rates rise, individuals draw down (or "run off") portions of their demand deposit balances to invest in higher-yielding instruments, forcing the bank to replace these funds with more expensive, rate-sensitive liabilities. In a high-rate environment, the opportunity cost of holding funds in demand deposits is substantial, making depositors more likely to shift balances. The same debate applies to passbook savings accounts—although Regulation Q ceilings were abolished in 1986, banks adjust these rates only infrequently, and savers tend to withdraw funds when rates rise, forcing more expensive funding substitutions.

This classification debate is not merely academic. The treatment of demand deposits can swing the calculated CGAP by tens of millions of dollars for a mid-sized bank, changing the apparent direction of the FI's interest rate exposure. Regulators have traditionally viewed transaction accounts and passbook savings as rate-insensitive liabilities, but with the growth of the Internet and competition from financial technology (FinTech) institutions, the mobility of these funds has increased dramatically, making them far more rate-sensitive than in the past. The failure to properly classify such items is one of the key reasons the repricing model can provide misleading signals.

Gap Ratio (Regulatory Version)
\[ \text{Gap Ratio} = \frac{CGAP}{A} \]
Alternative Gap Ratio
\[ \text{Gap Ratio (Alternative)} = \frac{RSA}{RSL} \]

Example

Consider the simplified FI balance sheet in Table 8–2, which shows the following one-year rate-sensitive assets: short-term consumer loans of $50 million, three-month T-bills of $30 million, six-month T-notes of $35 million, and 30-year floating-rate mortgages (rate adjusted every nine months) of $40 million. The total of one-year rate-sensitive assets is $155 million. The remaining $115 million in assets (long-term consumer loans, 3-year Treasury bonds, and 10-year fixed-rate mortgages) are rate-insensitive because their interest rates will not change within the year.

On the liability side, one-year rate-sensitive liabilities include three-month CDs ($40 million), three-month bankers acceptances ($20 million), six-month commercial paper ($60 million), and one-year time deposits ($20 million), for a total of $140 million. The remaining $130 million (equity capital, demand deposits, passbook savings, and two-year time deposits) is rate-insensitive. Based on these figures:

CGAP = $155 million − $140 million = $15 million (positive)

The gap ratio is: $15 million / $270 million = 0.056 = 5.6%

ΔNII = $15M × 0.01 = $150,000

The actual calculation: ΔNII = ($155M × 0.01) − ($140M × 0.01) = $1.55M − $1.40M = $150,000.

Analogy

Classifying rate-sensitive items is like sorting laundry into "machine-safe" and "hand-wash" piles. Some items are obviously machine-safe (like cotton T-shirts—short-term instruments that clearly reprice soon), while others are obviously hand-wash only (like silk blouses—long-term fixed-rate instruments). But some items fall in a gray zone—like a wool sweater that says "dry clean recommended" but might survive a gentle cycle. Demand deposits are the wool sweater of balance sheet classification. Historically, bankers treated them as insensitive (hand-wash only), but in a world of high interest rates and FinTech competition, they may in fact be quite sensitive (machine-safe). The FI manager must use judgment to decide where each item belongs, and an incorrect classification can distort the entire repricing gap analysis, just as washing a wool sweater in hot water can ruin it—or, in the FI context, produce a wildly inaccurate measure of interest rate risk.

Key Insight

The classification of demand deposits and passbook savings as rate-sensitive or rate-insensitive is a judgment call that can dramatically alter the measured repricing gap. The traditional regulatory treatment as insensitive is increasingly questionable in the Internet age, where depositors can move funds to higher-yielding alternatives with a few clicks. The mobility of core deposits has become highly sensitive to relative rates paid by banks versus other nonbank financial institutions such as money market mutual funds.

3.3 The CGAP Effect

Interpretation

The CGAP effect is the relationship between changes in interest rates and changes in net interest income when the repricing gap is expressed cumulatively. When CGAP is positive (RSA > RSL), net interest income is positively related to changes in interest rates: if rates rise, NII increases; if rates fall, NII decreases. This occurs because, when rates rise, interest income rises by more than interest expense (since a larger dollar amount of assets reprices up compared to liabilities). Conversely, when rates fall, interest income falls by more than interest expense, reducing NII. Table 8–4 in the original text summarizes these relationships: a positive CGAP with rising rates leads to rising NII; a positive CGAP with falling rates leads to falling NII; a negative CGAP with rising rates leads to falling NII; and a negative CGAP with falling rates leads to rising NII.

The magnitude of the CGAP effect is directly proportional to the absolute size of the CGAP. The larger the absolute value of CGAP, the larger the expected change in NII for any given change in interest rates. This means an FI that wants to increase its net interest income in anticipation of rising rates would want to position its balance sheet with a positive CGAP—holding more rate-sensitive assets than rate-sensitive liabilities over the planning horizon. Conversely, an FI expecting falling rates would prefer a negative CGAP. The strategic implications are clear: the repricing model not only measures risk but also provides a roadmap for positioning the balance sheet to profit from anticipated rate movements.

The one-year CGAP is by far the most commonly used planning horizon, but the choice of horizon is critical. An excessively long repricing period (say, five years) overstates rate sensitivity because it lumps together assets and liabilities that reprice at very different times within the period. An excessively short repricing period (say, one month) understates rate sensitivity because it ignores the risk posed by instruments repricing shortly after the cutoff. The most informative analysis uses multiple buckets, from one-day out to five-plus years. A bank might have a zero one-year CGAP but be heavily exposed to rate movements occurring within that year because of mismatches within the bucket—for example, $50 million more RSAs than RSLs repricing in the first six months and $50 million more RSLs than RSAs repricing in the last six months. The zero aggregate CGAP masks a genuine intra-year exposure. This is why regulators and sophisticated bank managers examine the entire term structure of repricing gaps, not just a single number.

CGAP Effect Relationships (Equal Rate Changes on RSAs and RSLs)
\[ \text{CGAP} > 0,\ \Delta R > 0 \Rightarrow \Delta NII > 0 \]
\[ \text{CGAP} > 0,\ \Delta R < 0 \Rightarrow \Delta NII < 0 \]
\[ \text{CGAP} < 0,\ \Delta R > 0 \Rightarrow \Delta NII < 0 \]
\[ \text{CGAP} < 0,\ \Delta R < 0 \Rightarrow \Delta NII > 0 \]

Example

Using the FI from Table 8–2 with CGAP = $15 million, suppose interest rates rise uniformly by 1% on both RSAs and RSLs. The expected annual change in net interest income is:

ΔNII = (RSA × ΔR) − (RSL × ΔR) = CGAP × ΔR

= ($155M × 0.01) − ($140M × 0.01) = ($15M) × 0.01

= $1.55M − $1.40M = $150,000

Now suppose rates fall uniformly by 1%. The CGAP predicts that NII will fall:

ΔNII = [$155M × (−0.01)] − [$140M × (−0.01)] = $15M × (−0.01)

= −$1.55M − (−$1.40M) = −$150,000

This demonstrates the CGAP effect in both directions. If the FI manager expected rates to rise, the positive CGAP of $15 million positions the bank to gain $150,000 in annual NII for every 1 percentage point rise in rates. If the manager expected rates to fall, a negative CGAP would be more desirable, positioning the bank to gain when rates decline because interest expense decreases by more than interest income.

Analogy

The CGAP effect is like a thermostat set to respond to temperature changes. A positive CGAP is like a thermostat programmed to turn on the heater when the temperature drops—it responds in the opposite direction to the temperature change in a way that either helps or hurts, depending on your perspective. More precisely, a positive CGAP is like a company that sells umbrellas: when it rains (rates rise), the company's revenue increases (NII rises); when it's sunny (rates fall), revenue drops. A negative CGAP is like a company that sells sunscreen: when it's sunny (rates fall), revenue rises; when it rains (rates rise), revenue falls. The manager chooses which "product" to sell based on the weather forecast—whether rates are expected to rise or fall.

Key Insight

The CGAP effect assumes equal changes in rates on RSAs and RSLs, which is rarely the case in practice. The spread between asset yields and liability costs can widen or narrow as rates move, creating a separate effect—the spread effect—that can reinforce or offset the CGAP effect. An FI manager who ignores the spread effect can be seriously misled about the true direction and magnitude of changes in net interest income.

3.4 The Spread Effect

Interpretation

The spread effect recognizes that the rates on RSAs and RSLs do not always change by equal amounts. In practice, the rates on assets and liabilities are determined in different markets with different competitive dynamics, and they respond to general interest rate movements at different speeds and to different degrees. For example, a bank's prime lending rate (an asset rate) may rise more quickly and by a larger amount than the bank's CD rates (liability rates) during a period of monetary tightening. Conversely, during a period of easing, the prime rate may fall more slowly than CD rates. These differing movements cause the spread between asset yields and liability costs to change—hence the term "spread effect."

The general rule of the spread effect is: regardless of the direction of the change in interest rates, a positive relationship exists between changes in the spread and changes in net interest income. If the spread increases (assets reprice up more than liabilities or down less than liabilities), NII increases. If the spread decreases, NII decreases. This effect operates independently of the CGAP effect. When both effects push in the same direction, the direction of the change in NII is unambiguous. For example, with a negative CGAP and rising rates (which reduces NII) combined with a decreasing spread (which also reduces NII), the manager can be certain NII will fall. But when the effects push in opposite directions, the outcome depends on the relative magnitudes of the CGAP and the spread change. An FI with a negative CGAP might still experience an increase in NII when rates rise, if the spread widens sufficiently to overcome the CGAP effect.

Figure 8–2 in the original text illustrates this phenomenon: it plots three-month CD rates (liability rates) and prime lending rates (asset rates) from 1990 to 2021. Although the two rates generally move in the same direction, they do so with different magnitudes and timing, reflecting the different market forces affecting each rate. In March 2020, for example, the Fed slashed the federal funds rate to 0–0.25% in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, and CD rates plunged. However, the prime rate adjusted somewhat differently. The imperfect correlation between asset and liability rates is the fundamental source of the spread effect.

Combined CGAP and Spread Effects on NII
\[ \Delta NII = (RSA \times \Delta R_{RSA}) - (RSL \times \Delta R_{RSL}) \]
\[ \Delta NII = \Delta \text{Interest Revenue} - \Delta \text{Interest Expense} \]

Example

Example 8–2 (Pure Spread Effect): Assume RSAs equal RSLs at $155 million each (CGAP = 0). Rates rise by 1.2% on RSAs and by 1.0% on RSLs, so the spread increases by 0.2%.

ΔNII = ($155M × 1.2%) − ($155M × 1.0%) = $155M × (1.2% − 1.0%) = $310,000

Despite having a zero CGAP, the FI gains $310,000 because asset yields increased more than liability costs.

Example 8–3 (Combined CGAP and Spread Effects): For the FI from Table 8–2 (CGAP = $15M), suppose rates fall by 1.0% on RSAs and by 1.2% on RSLs. The spread increases by 0.2% because liability costs fall more than asset yields.

ΔNII = [$155M × (−0.01)] − [$140M × (−0.012)]

= −$1.55M − (−$1.68M) = +$130,000

Even though the CGAP effect (positive CGAP with falling rates) puts negative pressure on NII (using equal rate changes would produce −$150,000), the spread effect is so large that NII actually increases by $130,000. This dramatic example illustrates why managers cannot rely solely on CGAP—they must also consider expected spread movements.

Analogy

Think of a married couple trying to save money for a vacation. The husband's income (asset yields) and the wife's income (liability costs) both matter. If the husband gets a raise while the wife's income stays the same, the family's savings grow (spread widens, NII rises). If both lose income but the wife loses more, the savings may still grow despite the overall downturn—just as in Example 8–3 where NII rose despite falling rates because liability costs fell faster than asset yields. The CGAP effect is like the relative levels of the two incomes (whether the husband earns more or less than the wife); the spread effect is like the changes in those incomes. Both must be considered to predict the family's saving rate, just as both effects must be combined to predict the change in NII.

Key Insight

The spread effect can reinforce or completely reverse the CGAP effect. When the spread between asset yields and liability costs widens, NII increases even if the CGAP effect alone would suggest otherwise. Table 8–5 shows that in four of the eight possible combinations (rows 2, 3, 5, and 8), the direction of the NII change cannot be predicted without knowing the relative magnitudes of the CGAP and spread changes. This uncertainty is not merely academic—it is a fundamental limitation of the repricing model's predictive power.

3.5 The Gap Ratio and Real-World Applications

Interpretation

The gap ratio expresses the repricing gap as a percentage of total assets: Gap Ratio = CGAP / A. This ratio is valuable because it provides a standardized measure of interest rate risk exposure that can be compared across FIs of different sizes. A $15 million CGAP might be immaterial for a $100 billion mega-bank but extremely significant for a $270 million community bank. Expressing the gap relative to assets gives regulators and managers a sense of the scale of the exposure. The ratio tells us two things simultaneously: the direction of exposure (positive or negative sign) and the magnitude of exposure (the percentage of assets that are effectively mismatched).

Regulators use the gap ratio to evaluate bank interest rate risk. An alternative version of the gap ratio—rate-sensitive assets divided by rate-sensitive liabilities (RSA/RSL)—provides similar information in a different form. A ratio greater than 1 indicates more RSAs than RSLs (positive gap), meaning the FI gains when rates rise. A ratio less than 1 indicates more RSLs than RSAs (negative gap), meaning the FI gains when rates fall. In the Table 8–2 example, this alternative ratio would be $155M/$140M = 1.107, meaning the FI has $1.107 of RSAs for every $1.00 of RSLs in the one-year-and-less bucket.

The real-world application of gap analysis is visible in the interest rate sensitivity reports that publicly traded banks file with regulators. Flushing Financial Corporation's 2021 report showed cumulative three-month, one-year, three-year, and five-year repricing gaps expressed as percentages of total assets. At the end of 2020, Flushing Financial had a negative cumulative three-month gap ratio of −8.03% and a negative one-year ratio of −6.79%, indicating that its management expected interest rates to fall (or remain low) and positioned the balance sheet to benefit from falling rates. However, its three-year ratio was positive at +13.72% and its five-year ratio was +29.73%, indicating that management expected rates to rise over those longer horizons. This contrast illustrates how a bank can simultaneously express different rate expectations for different planning horizons. The choice of horizon is not neutral—it reflects the FI's strategic view of the future rate environment.

Gap Ratio
\[ \text{Gap Ratio} = \frac{CGAP}{A} = \frac{\$15\text{M}}{\$270\text{M}} = 0.056 = 5.6\% \]

Example

Consider Flushing Financial Corporation, a real-world depository institution. In its 2021 interest rate sensitivity report, Flushing Financial showed the following cumulative gap ratios: for the three-month horizon, −8.03% of total assets; for the one-year horizon, −6.79%; for the three-year horizon, +13.72%; for the five-year horizon, +29.73%; and for the ten-year horizon, +14.55%. This pattern reveals that the bank expected short-term rates to fall or stay low (benefiting from its negative short-term gaps) but expected rates to rise over multi-year horizons (benefiting from its positive longer-term gaps). If rates fell in 2021, Flushing Financial's net interest income would increase due to the repricing of a large amount of short-term liabilities at lower rates relative to assets. If rates rose over a five-year horizon, the bank would benefit from the repricing of its longer-term assets at higher rates. This kind of granular gap analysis is precisely what regulators and bank management use to evaluate the institution's interest rate risk posture.

Analogy

The gap ratio is like a blood pressure reading for an FI. Just as a doctor measures blood pressure to assess cardiovascular health, regulators use the gap ratio to assess interest rate risk health. A blood pressure reading consists of two numbers—systolic and diastolic—and both matter. Similarly, the gap ratio has a direction (positive or negative) and a magnitude (percentage of assets). Just as high blood pressure is dangerous because it indicates excessive strain on the cardiovascular system, a large positive or negative gap ratio indicates excessive strain on the FI's earnings from interest rate movements. And just as a single blood pressure reading can be misleading without additional context, a single gap ratio at one horizon can mislead without examining the full term structure of gaps.

Key Insight

An FI can simultaneously be positioned to benefit from falling short-term rates and rising long-term rates, as Flushing Financial's gap report demonstrates. The repricing gap is not a single number but a term structure of gaps across different maturity buckets. Managers and regulators must examine the entire profile—not just the one-year CGAP. The gap ratio serves as a standardized summary measure, but it should be computed for multiple horizons to capture the FI's full interest rate risk exposure.

Key Takeaways

  • The repricing gap model measures interest rate risk by comparing rate-sensitive assets (RSAs) to rate-sensitive liabilities (RSLs) within specified maturity buckets; the difference is the gap.
  • A positive gap (RSA > RSL) exposes the FI to reinvestment risk and means NII rises with rising rates; a negative gap (RSA < RSL) exposes the FI to refinancing risk and means NII falls with rising rates.
  • The cumulative gap (CGAP) sums gaps across buckets, with the one-year CGAP being the most commonly used measure; ΔNII = CGAP × ΔR.
  • The spread effect captures the reality that rates on RSAs and RSLs change by different amounts; it can reinforce or offset the CGAP effect, making the direction of NII changes unpredictable in some scenarios.
  • The gap ratio (CGAP / Total Assets) standardizes the gap measure for comparison across FIs and is used by regulators; real-world gap reports reveal an institution's rate expectations across multiple horizons simultaneously.
  • Classification of demand deposits and passbook savings as rate-sensitive or rate-insensitive remains a critical judgment call that can significantly alter the calculated gap.

4. Weaknesses of the Repricing Model

4.1 Market Value Effects

Interpretation

The most significant weakness of the repricing model is that it completely ignores the market value effects of interest rate changes. The model operates within a book value accounting framework, meaning that assets and liabilities are reported at their historic values or costs rather than their current market values. When interest rates change, the present values of all future cash flows on an FI's assets and liabilities change as well—these are the market value effects. A 30-year fixed-rate mortgage purchased when rates were 13% will have a dramatically different market value when rates fall to 7%, even though its book value remains unchanged. The repricing model would classify this mortgage as rate-insensitive (since its interest rate does not change within the one-year horizon) and would completely miss the substantial capital gain or loss embedded in the instrument.

The income effect—which the repricing model captures—is only one dimension of interest rate risk. The capital value effect can be far larger and more consequential. Consider a long-term bond with a 10-year duration. A 1% rise in interest rates will reduce the bond's market value by approximately 10%. For an FI with a substantial bond portfolio, this capital loss could dwarf any income effect captured by the repricing model. The FI's net worth—the market value of its assets minus the market value of its liabilities—can be severely eroded even when net interest income appears well protected. In the worst cases, duration mismatches combined with large market value losses can render an FI insolvent, even if its income statement shows positive net interest income.

The repricing model thus provides what might be called a "dashboard warning light" attitude to risk: it alerts managers to short-term income exposure but says nothing about the condition of the engine itself—the FI's net worth. It is precisely this limitation that motivated the development of the maturity model (Appendix 8A) and, more importantly, the duration gap model (Chapter 9), both of which incorporate market value accounting and the timing of cash flows. The Bank for International Settlements has advocated for market value–based models in evaluating bank interest rate risk, and large banks have adopted duration gap analysis as their primary measure. The repricing model remains useful as a simple, intuitive first screen, but it is not a substitute for full market value analysis.

Example

Consider a bank that made a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage loan of $1 million when interest rates were 13% and still holds that loan on its books when market rates have fallen to 7%. Under the repricing model, this mortgage is classified as rate-insensitive because its coupon rate does not change. The model would say: "No interest rate risk from this asset over the next year." Yet the market value of that mortgage has risen substantially as rates fell—the present value of its future cash flows at a 7% discount rate is far higher than at a 13% discount rate. If the bank needed to sell the mortgage to raise liquidity, it would realize a significant capital gain. Conversely, if rates had risen from 13% to 18%, the market value of the mortgage would have fallen, inflicting a capital loss.

Another example: A bank with $100 million in long-term fixed-rate assets and $80 million in short-term liabilities might show a positive repricing gap (RSAs > RSLs) if some of those assets have floating rates. The repricing model might indicate that the bank benefits from rising rates. However, the market values of its fixed-rate assets could fall sharply as rates rise, while the market values of its liabilities (being short-term) decline only slightly. The bank's net worth could be decimated even as its net interest income rises. This divergence between the income effect and the capital value effect is the fundamental blind spot of the repricing model.

Analogy

Think of a homeowner checking their bank account balance each month to gauge their financial health. The bank balance (net interest income) is important, but it says nothing about the value of the house itself (net worth). If the housing market crashes, the homeowner may have a comfortable monthly cash flow but a severely depleted net worth. The repricing model is like checking only monthly cash flow while ignoring the house's value—it gives an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of financial health. The duration model, by contrast, is like a full financial statement that considers both monthly income and the market value of assets and liabilities.

Key Insight

The repricing model's reliance on book value accounting ignores the fact that virtually all assets and liabilities change in market value when interest rates change. An FI can show a well-hedged repricing gap while simultaneously facing catastrophic net worth erosion from interest rate movements. This is the most important limitation of the model and the primary reason regulators and large banks have moved toward market value–based duration analysis.

4.2 Overaggregation

Interpretation

The repricing model groups assets and liabilities into relatively wide maturity buckets—ranging from one day to over five years—and assumes that all instruments within a bucket reprice at the same time. This overaggregation problem masks the distribution of repricing times within each bucket. Two FIs could have identical balances in every bucket yet have completely different interest rate risk exposures because the assets and liabilities within a bucket reprice at different times.

Consider the three-month to six-month bucket. An FI might have $50 million more in RSAs than RSLs repricing in months 3–4 and $50 million more in RSLs than RSAs repricing in months 5–6. The interest rate sensitivity report would show a zero gap for the entire three-to-six-month bucket [+50 + (−50) = 0], leading the FI to believe it has no exposure. In reality, the FI is significantly mismatched within the bucket: when rates change in months 3–4, the income effect will differ from the effect in months 5–6. A rate change that occurs early in the bucket's period will affect the FI differently than a rate change occurring late in the period, and the zero aggregate gap completely obscures this $50 million internal mismatch.

The solution to overaggregation is to use narrower maturity buckets. The shorter the range over which bucket gaps are calculated, the smaller the overaggregation problem. In the limit, an FI could compute its repricing gap for every individual day into the future. Indeed, many large FIs maintain internal information systems that report repricing gaps on any given day in the future (e.g., 252 days from now, 1,329 days from now). This gives the FI manager a much more precise picture of when mismatches will actually occur, allowing for more accurate hedging decisions. Regulators, however, typically require reporting only over relatively wide maturity buckets, so the public gap reports may not reveal intra-bucket risk. An FI with a zero reported gap for every bucket might still be substantially exposed to interest rate risk—the model's overaggregation literally hides the risk.

Example

Figure 8–3 from the text illustrates the overaggregation problem concretely. Suppose an FI has the following within the three-month to six-month bucket: between month 3 and month 4, RSAs exceed RSLs by $50 million; between month 5 and month 6, RSLs exceed RSAs by $50 million. The net gap for the bucket is zero [+$50M + (−$50M) = $0]. The FI reports zero repricing risk for this bucket. But this is deeply misleading. If interest rates rise early in the period (say, in month 3), the FI benefits because it has more assets repricing at the higher rates. If rates rise late in the period (say, in month 5), the FI is hurt because it has more liabilities repricing at the higher rates. The direction of the rate change timing matters enormously, and the aggregate zero gap captures none of this nuance.

Now consider a second scenario: an FI with $50 million more RSAs than RSLs distributed evenly across months 3 through 6. The gap for the bucket is $50 million, and the FI appears exposed. But if the liabilities reprice at the very beginning of the bucket (month 3) while the assets reprice evenly throughout, the effective exposure is quite different from what the aggregate number suggests. The repricing model's inability to capture such intra-bucket timing differences is a fundamental flaw that can lead to substantial mis-measurement of interest rate risk.

Analogy

Overaggregation is like averaging the temperatures of two cities to decide how to dress. If it's 100°F in Phoenix and 0°F in Minneapolis, the average is 50°F—suggesting you should wear a light jacket. But that average is useless: if you're in Phoenix you'll be roasting, and if you're in Minneapolis you'll be freezing. Similarly, a zero repricing gap that aggregates a +$50 million mismatch at the start of a bucket and a −$50 million mismatch at the end tells you nothing about the actual risk you face. The distribution matters, not just the average.

Key Insight

Wide maturity buckets hide intra-bucket mismatches. A zero repricing gap in a bucket can coexist with substantial interest rate risk if assets and liabilities within that bucket reprice at different times. Shorter buckets—ideally daily—provide a more accurate picture of an FI's true exposure. The overaggregation problem is a key reason why the repricing model is considered a relatively crude tool, useful for quick approximations but inadequate for comprehensive interest rate risk measurement.

4.3 The Problem of Runoffs

Interpretation

The repricing model assumes that rate-insensitive assets and liabilities remain on the FI's books unchanged throughout the repricing horizon. In reality, virtually all financial instruments generate periodic cash flows—called runoffs—that become available for reinvestment at current market rates. Even a fixed-rate 30-year mortgage pays principal and interest each month. The principal portion of those payments is a cash flow that runs off from the asset and must be reinvested. If rates have changed, the reinvested funds will earn a different yield. Thus, even "rate-insensitive" assets with fixed coupons contain an embedded rate-sensitive component: their periodic cash flows are exposed to changes in interest rates.

The runoff problem is closely related to prepayment risk. Mortgage holders can refinance their loans when rates fall, repaying the original mortgage early and forcing the FI to reinvest the proceeds at lower rates. Similarly, borrowers may sell their homes and pay off mortgages early for reasons unrelated to interest rates. These prepayments are a form of runoff that is virtually impossible to predict with certainty. When an FI manager classifies a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage as rate-insensitive for the next year, the classification implicitly assumes that the mortgage will not be prepaid. In reality, some portion of every mortgage portfolio will prepay each year, and that portion is highly sensitive to interest rate levels—when rates fall, prepayments spike as borrowers refinance.

An FI manager can address these issues by adjusting the repricing model to incorporate expected runoffs. For each asset and liability item, the manager can estimate the dollar cash flow that will run off within the next year and add that amount to the value of rate-sensitive assets or liabilities. For example, if a bank holds $100 million in 30-year mortgages but expects $5 million in principal payments (runoffs) and $2 million in prepayments over the next year, then $7 million of those mortgages should be treated as rate-sensitive. This adjustment makes the repricing model more accurate, but it requires sophisticated cash flow modeling and assumptions about prepayment behavior—complexities that undermine the model's initial simplicity.

Adjusted Rate-Sensitive Assets with Runoffs
\[ RSA_{adjusted} = RSA + \text{Expected Runoff Cash Flows from Rate-Insensitive Assets} \]
\[ RSL_{adjusted} = RSL + \text{Expected Runoff Cash Flows from Rate-Insensitive Liabilities} \]

Example

Consider a bank with $100 million in 30-year fixed-rate mortgages earning 6% and $100 million in one-year CDs costing 3%. The repricing model classifies the mortgages as rate-insensitive (since their rates won't change for a year) and the CDs as rate-sensitive. The CGAP appears to be −$100 million (0 − $100M). But suppose the bank's mortgage portfolio is expected to generate $8 million in principal payments (runoffs) over the next year—some from borrowers refinancing, some from home sales, some from regular amortization. These $8 million in cash flows must be reinvested at whatever market rates prevail. If rates rise to 7%, the reinvested funds earn 7%; if rates fall to 5%, they earn 5%. The $8 million runoff is thus effectively rate-sensitive, even though the original $100 million mortgage portfolio was classified as not rate-sensitive.

Similarly, suppose the bank's demand deposit accounts (classified as rate-insensitive) are expected to be drawn down by $5 million over the year as depositors move funds to higher-yielding alternatives. If rates rise, this runoff accelerates, forcing the bank to replace those funds with higher-cost CDs or other rate-sensitive liabilities. The adjusted repricing gap would be:

CGAP_adjusted = ($0 + $8M) − ($100M + $5M) = $8M − $105M = −$97M

The failure to incorporate these runoffs overstates the FI's negative gap (making it appear more exposed than it actually is) or, depending on the direction of the runoffs, understates the true exposure. The inclusion of runoffs changes both the size and potentially the direction of the measured gap.

Analogy

Think of a bathtub with a partially open drain. The faucet (rate-sensitive assets) adds water at a rate that adjusts when you turn the handle (interest rates change). The water already in the tub (rate-insensitive assets) stays put—or does it? If the drain is open, water constantly leaks out (runoff) and must be replaced. The drain rate depends on how far the plug is pulled (prepayment rates), which itself depends on whether the water is hot or cold (interest rate levels). A model that ignores the drain would overstate the bathtub's ability to retain water, just as the repricing model overstates the stability of rate-insensitive assets when it ignores runoffs and prepayments. The tub's true water level depends on both the faucet and the drain, just as an FI's true interest rate risk depends on both explicit repricings and embedded runoffs.

Key Insight

All assets and liabilities—even those with fixed interest rates—generate rate-sensitive cash flows through amortization, prepayments, and deposit runoffs. Ignoring these runoffs distorts the repricing gap and can lead to serious miscalculation of interest rate risk exposure. The prepayment component is particularly problematic since it varies with the level of interest rates itself: when rates fall, prepayments accelerate, increasing the rate-sensitive portion of the mortgage portfolio precisely when reinvestment rates are lowest.

4.4 Cash Flows from Off-Balance-Sheet Activities

Interpretation

The repricing model considers only on-balance-sheet assets and liabilities. In doing so, it ignores the substantial cash flows generated by off-balance-sheet instruments such as interest rate futures contracts, options, swaps, and forward rate agreements. These instruments are widely used by FIs to hedge interest rate risk, and their cash flows can significantly offset—or amplify—the interest rate risk embedded in the balance sheet. The exclusion of off-balance-sheet activities is the fourth major weakness of the repricing model.

Consider the example of an FI that has hedged its on-balance-sheet interest rate risk using an interest rate futures contract. As interest rates change, the futures contract is "marked to market," producing a daily cash flow for the FI—positive when the futures position gains in value, negative when it loses value. These cash flows are designed to offset the income effects of interest rate changes on the FI's balance sheet. If rates rise and the FI's negative gap causes net interest income to fall, the futures hedge generates daily gains that compensate for the income loss. However, the simple repricing model, which looks only at balance sheet positions, completely misses these offsetting hedge cash flows. It would measure a large negative interest rate risk exposure even though the FI's combined on- and off-balance-sheet risk is effectively neutralized.

The failure to capture off-balance-sheet cash flows means the repricing model can dramatically overstate the risk of FIs that actively hedge their balance sheets. It also means the model cannot capture the risk of FIs that use off-balance-sheet instruments to increase their risk exposure—for example, by taking speculative positions in futures or options. In principle, off-balance-sheet cash flows could and should be included in the repricing model, but doing so requires a substantial expansion of the analysis: the FI must track not only the current market value of each off-balance-sheet position but also how those values will change as interest rates move, which is effectively a duration or sensitivity analysis. Once again, this defeats the model's simplicity. The BIS and national regulators are increasingly aware of this limitation, and modern risk management systems incorporate off-balance-sheet positions into a more comprehensive interest rate risk measure such as the duration gap model or value-at-risk (VaR) analysis.

Example

A bank, expecting interest rates to rise, decides to protect its net interest income by selling interest rate futures contracts. The bank has a negative repricing gap of $50 million. If rates rise by 1%, the repricing model predicts that NII will fall by $500,000. However, the bank's futures position gains value when rates rise, producing daily cash inflows that exactly offset the loss in net interest income. From a purely balance-sheet view, the repricing model signals a large negative exposure; from a combined balance-sheet-and-hedge view, the FI has zero net exposure. Conversely, consider a bank that buys interest rate futures for speculative purposes. The futures position will experience daily cash flows that fluctuate with rates, but the repricing model captures none of this. If rates move against the speculative position, the bank's total risk exposure could be far larger than its balance-sheet repricing gap indicates.

Analogy

Imagine you are trying to measure the temperature risk to a greenhouse full of plants. The greenhouse has a heating system (on-balance-sheet assets) and cooling vents (on-balance-sheet liabilities). But it also has a backup generator (off-balance-sheet futures contract) that kicks in when the temperature drops too far. If you measure only the heating and vent systems, you might conclude that the greenhouse will freeze when temperatures fall. But the backup generator provides exactly the heat needed to keep the plants alive. Conversely, if the greenhouse has a faulty backup generator that actually blows cold air when temperatures fall, you would underestimate the risk. Any temperature risk assessment that ignores the backup generator is incomplete—just as the repricing model's exclusion of off-balance-sheet cash flows makes its measures of interest rate risk incomplete.

Key Insight

Off-balance-sheet instruments generate real cash flows that can hedge or amplify an FI's interest rate risk, and the repricing model's complete exclusion of these instruments produces an incomplete and potentially misleading picture of total risk exposure. Modern FIs that aggressively use derivatives require more comprehensive risk measurement approaches—such as duration gap or simulation-based models—that incorporate off-balance-sheet positions
