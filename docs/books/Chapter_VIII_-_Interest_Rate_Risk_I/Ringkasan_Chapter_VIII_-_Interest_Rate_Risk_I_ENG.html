1. Introduction: Interest Rate Risk and the Financial Institution

Financial institutions (FIs) perform an asset-transformation function: they accept liabilities from savers and transform them into assets held against borrowers. In performing this function, FIs frequently mismatch the maturities of their assets and liabilities. This maturity mismatch exposes them to interest rate risk: the risk that changes in the level of interest rates will adversely affect the institution's profitability and, in more comprehensive frameworks, its net worth. The measurement of this exposure is the central subject of this chapter and the one that follows.

The degree of interest rate risk faced by an FI is not constant over time; it depends critically on the stance and credibility of central bank monetary policy. During 2008–2015, in response to the severe financial crisis and the slowly recovering economy, the U.S. Federal Reserve dropped its target federal funds rate to a range between zero and one-quarter of 1 percent and lowered its discount window rate to 0.5 percent (through 2010) and 0.75 percent (through 2015). The Fed announced it would keep rates at these low levels for several years. Because the path of rates was stable and publicly assured, interest rate risk over this period was relatively low. However, as the economy recovers and inflationary pressure arises, interest rate risk again becomes a significant risk that FIs must actively manage. The critical managerial question is not whether rates will move, but when and by how much.

This chapter and the next present three models used to measure an FI's interest rate risk exposure: (1) the repricing (funding gap) model, covered here; (2) the maturity model, a market value–based approach presented in Appendix 8A; and (3) the duration model, presented in the subsequent chapter. The repricing model is the simplest of the three and remains the primary measure for smaller depository institutions (DIs)—the vast majority of U.S. institutions—because of its information value and its simplicity in pointing to an FI's profit exposure to rate changes. The maturity model, while rarely used anymore, was a first attempt to capture the impact of rate changes on the overall market value of an FI's assets, liabilities, and net worth. The duration gap model is the most comprehensive: it considers market values, the maturity distribution of assets and liabilities, the degree of leverage on the balance sheet, and the timing of cash flow arrivals. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS)—the organization of the world's major central banks—has suggested a standardized, market-value-based approach for regulators, and larger banks (which hold the vast majority of total banking industry assets) have adopted duration-based and other market value models as their primary risk measures.

The BIS requires DIs to maintain interest rate risk measurement systems that assess the effects of interest rate changes on both earnings and economic value. These systems must provide meaningful measures of current exposure levels and be capable of identifying excessive exposures. The repricing model satisfies the earnings dimension; the market value models address the economic value dimension. Understanding both is therefore essential for a complete picture of interest rate risk.

Analogy

An FI with mismatched maturities resembles a homeowner who borrows short-term (for example, via a variable-rate credit line) to finance a long-term investment (a rental property yielding fixed rent). If the short-term borrowing cost rises while the rental income stays fixed, the homeowner's monthly profit shrinks or turns negative. The FI faces the same structural tension: the cost of its funding and the return on its assets respond to interest rates at different speeds and to different degrees.

Key Insight

Interest rate risk is a policy- and environment-dependent risk, not a fixed attribute of the balance sheet. The same balance sheet carries low risk when the central bank has credibly committed to stable rates and high risk when rate changes are anticipated or uncertain. Risk measurement must therefore always be paired with a view of the likely path and volatility of interest rates.

Key Takeaways

  • FIs incur interest rate risk by mismatching the maturities of assets and liabilities in their asset-transformation function.
  • Central bank monetary policy—especially the Federal Reserve's—most directly underlies the level and movement of interest rates affecting FI funding costs and asset returns.
  • Three measurement models exist: the repricing model (income/book value focus), the maturity model (market value focus), and the duration model (comprehensive market value focus).
  • Small DIs predominantly use the repricing model; large banks and regulators favor market value–based (duration) approaches.
  • The BIS requires measurement systems that capture effects on both earnings and economic value.

2. The Level and Movement of Interest Rates

While many factors influence the level and movement of interest rates, the central bank's monetary policy strategy most directly underlies them, and these rates in turn determine an FI's cost of funds and return on assets. In the United States, the central bank is the Federal Reserve (the Fed). Through daily open market operations—buying and selling Treasury bonds and Treasury bills—the Fed seeks to influence the money supply, inflation, and the level of interest rates, particularly short-term rates. Changes in short-term rates usually feed through to the whole term structure of interest rates, which is why Fed actions ultimately affect rates across all maturities.

The transmission mechanism operates through economic decisions. When the Fed finds it necessary to slow the economy, it tightens monetary policy by taking actions that raise interest rates; the normal result is a decrease in business and household spending, especially spending financed by credit or borrowing. Conversely, when spending declines to the point that the Fed finds it necessary to stimulate the economy, it allows interest rates to fall (expansionary monetary policy); the drop in rates promotes borrowing and spending. A historic illustration: in December 2008, as the U.S. economy fell into its deepest downturn since the Great Depression, the Fed unexpectedly announced it would drop its target fed funds rate to a range of zero to 0.25 percent and lower its discount window rate to 0.5 percent—the lowest level since the 1940s. Even into 2015, the Fed expected to keep fed funds rates below 1 percent. The first increase came only in December 2015—the first in ten years—followed by six more increases by June 2018. In March 2020, in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the Fed again slashed the federal funds rate to a target range of 0 to 0.25 percent.

Beyond the low rates set by the Federal Reserve, the Bank of Japan, the European Central Bank, and several smaller European central banks ventured into the once uncharted territory of negative interest rates. When central banks set negative rates, the lender pays the borrower: instead of being paid for depositing funds with the central bank, a commercial bank now pays the central bank to deposit funds. This environment further complicates the measurement and management of interest rate risk.

A second structural force is financial market integration. The increased integration of financial markets over recent decades has raised the speed with which interest rate changes and associated volatility are transmitted among countries. This makes control of U.S. interest rates by the Federal Reserve more difficult and less certain than before. Investors worldwide carefully evaluate statements by the Chair of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors before Congress; even hints of changes in U.S. interest rates may have major effects on world interest rates, foreign exchange rates, and stock prices. The globalization of financial market flows has made the measurement and management of interest rate risk a prominent concern for modern FI managers.

Example

Policy timeline from the source: December 2008 → fed funds target cut to 0–0.25 percent, discount rate to 0.5 percent (lowest since the 1940s); low-rate guidance maintained through 2015; December 2015 → first rate increase in ten years; six further increases by June 2018; March 2020 → pandemic-driven cut back to 0–0.25 percent. Interpretation: during the announced low-rate period, interest rate risk was low because the rate path was stable and predictable; risk re-emerged as recovery and inflation raised the prospect of increases. Practical lesson: FI managers must monitor not only current rates but the credibility and signals of the central bank's future path.

Analogy

The central bank acts like a thermostat operator for the economy's temperature (spending and inflation). Raising rates is like turning down the heat to cool an overheating room; lowering rates is like turning the heat up in a cold room. Financial market integration means the thermostat is now connected to heating systems in every other house on the street—adjusting it produces effects that leak across borders and are harder to control precisely.

Key Insight

Fed actions target short-term rates, but the effects propagate through the entire term structure. Moreover, because financial markets are globally integrated, even hints of U.S. rate changes move world interest rates, exchange rates, and equity prices. Interest rate risk management is therefore inseparable from monitoring central bank communication and international capital flows.

Key Takeaways

  • The Fed influences short-term rates through daily open market operations in Treasury securities; changes feed through to the whole term structure.
  • Tightening policy raises rates to slow spending; expansionary policy lowers rates to stimulate borrowing and spending.
  • Negative central bank rates reverse the normal lender-pays-borrower relationship for central bank deposits.
  • Financial market integration transmits rate changes and volatility across countries faster, making domestic rate control less certain.
  • The timing of expected rate changes—not just their direction—is the critical concern for interest rate risk management.

3. The Repricing Model: Conceptual Framework

The repricing model (also called the funding gap model) is a simple model used by small—and therefore most—FIs in the United States. It is essentially a book value accounting cash flow analysis of the repricing gap between the interest income earned on an FI's assets and the interest expense paid on its liabilities over a particular period. The performance variable on which the model focuses is net interest income (NII): the difference between an FI's interest income and its interest expense. This book value orientation contrasts sharply with the market value–based maturity and duration models.

Under the repricing gap approach, commercial banks report quarterly, on their call reports, interest rate sensitivity reports showing the repricing gaps for assets and liabilities across standardized maturity buckets:

  1. One day.
  2. More than 1 day to 3 months.
  3. More than 3 months to 6 months.
  4. More than 6 months to 12 months.
  5. More than 1 year to 5 years.
  6. More than 5 years.

A bank reports the gap in each bucket by calculating the rate sensitivity of each asset (RSA) and each liability (RSL) on its balance sheet. Rate sensitivity means that the asset or liability is repriced at or near current market interest rates within a certain time horizon (maturity bucket). Repricing can occur in two ways: (1) through a rollover—an asset or liability is paid off at or prior to maturity and the funds are used to issue a new instrument at current market rates; or (2) because the instrument is variable-rate—for example, a variable-rate mortgage whose rate resets every quarter based on movements in a prime rate. Note carefully that rate sensitivity is defined relative to the chosen horizon: a 30-year floating-rate mortgage that resets every nine months is a rate-sensitive asset under a one-year horizon even though its original maturity is 30 years.

The model's advantage is its simplicity and information value: it directly indicates the FI's net interest income (profit) exposure to interest rate changes. Its corresponding limitation—developed fully in Section 10—is that it captures only the income effect of rate changes, not the market value (capital loss) effect, because in the book value accounting world of the repricing model, assets and liabilities are reported at historic cost. Interest rate changes therefore affect only current interest income or expense on the income statement, not the market values on the balance sheet.

Core Definitions
\[ \text{Repricing gap} = RSA_i - RSL_i \]

Meaning of symbols: \(RSA_i\) is the dollar volume of rate-sensitive assets in maturity bucket \(i\)—assets whose interest rates will be repriced or changed over that future period. \(RSL_i\) is the dollar volume of rate-sensitive liabilities in the same bucket—liabilities whose rates will be repriced or changed over that period. The repricing gap is the difference between the two.

Interpretation: a positive gap (\(RSA_i > RSL_i\)) means more assets than liabilities reprice in the bucket; a negative gap (\(RSA_i < RSL_i\)) means the reverse. A zero gap means equal dollar volumes reprice. Practical use: the gap is the model's summary statistic for profit exposure in each bucket; it is reported quarterly in regulatory interest rate sensitivity reports. Assumption: classification depends entirely on the chosen repricing horizon—an instrument's rate sensitivity is not an intrinsic property but a property of the instrument relative to the bucket.

Example

Model: Table 8–1 of the source presents a simple interest rate sensitivity report with six buckets (equity assumed zero): one day, assets $20m, liabilities $30m, gap −$10m; 1 day–3 months, $30m/$40m, gap −$10m; 3–6 months, $70m/$85m, gap −$15m; 6–12 months, $90m/$70m, gap +$20m; 1–5 years, $40m/$30m, gap +$10m; over 5 years, $10m/$5m, gap +$5m; totals $260m/$260m. Interpretation: the FI is liability-sensitive in the short buckets and asset-sensitive in the longer buckets. Practical lesson: the bucket structure reveals where on the timeline the exposure sits, not merely its overall size.

Analogy

Think of the maturity buckets as compartments in a train, and each asset and liability as a passenger assigned to the compartment where its rate next changes. The gap in each compartment tells you whether more "income passengers" or "expense passengers" will have their fares adjusted first when the pricing environment shifts.

Key Insight

The repricing model measures profit (income statement) exposure, not value (balance sheet) exposure. Because it operates in a book value world, a 30-year bond purchased ten years ago at a 13 percent coupon is reported at the same book value whether current rates are 13 percent or 7 percent. The model deliberately trades comprehensiveness for simplicity and transparency.

Key Takeaways

  • The repricing (funding gap) model is a book value cash flow analysis focused on net interest income (NII) = interest income − interest expense.
  • Banks report gaps quarterly in six standardized maturity buckets on call reports.
  • Rate sensitivity = the instrument's rate is repriced at or near market rates within the bucket, via rollover or variable-rate reset.
  • Rate sensitivity is horizon-dependent: a 30-year mortgage resetting every nine months is rate-sensitive over a one-year horizon.
  • The model's simplicity is both its advantage (transparency, ease of use) and the root of its limitations.

4. The Repricing Gap Equation

The repricing model measures the change in an FI's net interest income for interest rate changes in different maturity buckets. The central equation links the dollar gap in a bucket to the resulting profit impact:

Repricing Gap Impact on Net Interest Income
\[ \Delta NII_i = (GAP_i)\,\Delta R_i = (RSA_i - RSL_i)\,\Delta R_i \]

Meaning of symbols:

  • \(\Delta NII_i\) = the change in net interest income generated in maturity bucket \(i\).
  • \(GAP_i\) = the dollar size of the gap between the book value of rate-sensitive assets and rate-sensitive liabilities in bucket \(i\), i.e., \(RSA_i - RSL_i\).
  • \(RSA_i\) = rate-sensitive assets in bucket \(i\).
  • \(RSL_i\) = rate-sensitive liabilities in bucket \(i\).
  • \(\Delta R_i\) = the change in the level of interest rates impacting assets and liabilities in the \(i\)th bucket.

Mathematical relationship: the change in profit is the product of the gap and the rate change. The relationship is linear in both the gap and the rate change: doubling either doubles the profit impact. The sign of the gap determines the direction of the exposure.

Interpretation: if \(GAP_i < 0\) (more rate-sensitive liabilities than assets), a rise in rates increases interest expense by more than interest income, so \(\Delta NII_i < 0\). This is refinancing risk: the risk that the cost of rolling over or reborrowing funds will rise above the returns being earned on asset investments. Conversely, if \(GAP_i > 0\), a fall in rates reduces interest income by more than interest expense. This is reinvestment risk: the risk that the returns on funds to be reinvested will fall below the cost of the funds.

Practical use: the equation converts a balance sheet classification exercise into a direct profit forecast for any assumed rate change. A manager can stress-test NII by plugging in alternative \(\Delta R_i\) scenarios. Key assumption: the equation as stated assumes the rate change on RSAs equals the rate change on RSLs in the bucket (equal rate changes); the case of unequal changes is treated separately via the spread effect in Section 8.

Example

Model: the FI in Table 8–1 has a negative $10 million one-day gap. Assets and liabilities repricing each day are typically interbank borrowings on the federal funds or repurchase agreement market. If short-term rates (fed funds and/or repo rates) rise 1 percent, the annualized change in future NII is \(\Delta NII_i = (-\$10\text{ million}) \times 0.01 = -\$100{,}000\). Interpretation: the negative gap and associated refinancing risk produce a $100,000 loss in net interest income. Practical lesson: a negative gap converts rising rates directly into profit erosion, with the loss proportional to both gap size and rate change.

Analogy

Imagine you owe a friend $10 million at a floating rate but have lent only $10 million... actually, suppose you owe $30 million floating but are owed only $20 million floating. Every 1 percent rise in rates costs you $100,000 per year on the unmatched $10 million—exactly the FI's refinancing risk. The gap is the unmatched amount; the rate change is the price of being unmatched.

Key Insight

The gap equation quantifies exposure but does not by itself constitute a forecast. It answers: if rates change by \(\Delta R_i\), then NII changes by \(GAP_i \times \Delta R_i\). Whether rates will actually change—and by how much—is a separate judgment about monetary policy and market conditions. The equation is conditional, not predictive.

Key Takeaways

  • \(\Delta NII_i = (RSA_i - RSL_i) \times \Delta R_i\) converts gap and rate change into profit impact.
  • Negative gap → refinancing risk (rising rates lower NII); positive gap → reinvestment risk (falling rates lower NII).
  • The relationship is linear: exposure scales proportionally with both gap size and rate change.
  • The basic equation assumes equal rate changes on RSAs and RSLs; unequal changes require the spread effect.
  • Only rate-sensitive items enter the calculation; rate-insensitive items' income and expense are unaffected by the rate change over the horizon.

5. Constructing the Gap: Rate-Sensitive Assets and Liabilities

To compute the cumulative one-year gap from an actual balance sheet, the FI manager asks a single classification question for every item: "Will or can this asset or liability have its interest rate changed within the next year?" If yes, it is rate-sensitive; if no, it is not. The classification uses remaining maturities (or next repricing dates), not original maturities. The source's simplified balance sheet (Table 8–2, $270 million total assets) illustrates the procedure.

One-year rate-sensitive assets (RSAs), totaling $155 million:

  1. Short-term consumer loans: $50 million—repriced at the end of the year, just making the one-year cutoff.
  2. Three-month T-bills: $30 million—repriced on maturity (rollover) every three months.
  3. Six-month T-notes: $35 million—repriced on maturity (rollover) every six months.
  4. 30-year floating-rate mortgages: $40 million—rate reset every nine months. These long-term assets are rate-sensitive under the one-year horizon because their rates change within it.

The remaining $115 million of assets—long-term consumer loans, 3-year Treasury bonds, and 10-year fixed-rate mortgages—generate interest income over the next year, but the size of that income will not change if rates change, because their rates are fixed over the horizon. They are therefore rate-insensitive. (The source assumes these assets are noncallable over the year and that no prepayments or runoffs occur on the mortgages within the year.)

One-year rate-sensitive liabilities (RSLs), totaling $140 million:

  1. Three-month CDs: $40 million—mature in three months, repriced on rollover.
  2. Three-month bankers acceptances: $20 million—mature in three months, repriced on rollover.
  3. Six-month commercial paper: $60 million—mature and repriced every six months.
  4. One-year time deposits: $20 million—repriced right at the end of the one-year gap horizon.

The remaining $130 million is rate-insensitive: the $20 million of equity capital and $40 million of demand deposits pay no interest (noninterest-paying); the $30 million of passbook savings and $40 million of two-year time deposits generate interest expense over the year, but the level of that expense will not change if the general level of rates changes.

The demand deposits controversy. Demand deposits (and transaction accounts generally) are excluded from RSLs in the standard treatment, but strong arguments exist on both sides. Against inclusion: the explicit interest rate on demand deposits is zero by regulation; even on transaction accounts paying explicit interest (such as NOW accounts), the rates paid do not fluctuate directly with the general level of rates, particularly when rates are rising; and many demand deposits act as core deposits—long-term sources of funds for the FI. For inclusion: even without explicit interest, demand deposits pay implicit interest, because FIs do not charge fees that fully cover the cost of checking services. Moreover, if rates rise, individuals draw down (run off) all or part of their demand deposits, forcing the bank to replace them with higher-yielding, interest-bearing, rate-sensitive funds. This is most likely when rates on alternative instruments are high, since the opportunity cost of holding funds in demand deposit accounts is then larger. In such an environment, FIs would include all or a portion of demand deposits as rate-sensitive liabilities. Similar arguments apply to retail passbook savings accounts: although Federal Reserve Regulation Q ceilings on maximum rates for these accounts were abolished in March 1986, banks still adjust these rates only infrequently; yet savers tend to withdraw funds when rates rise, forcing banks into more expensive fund substitutions. The Federal Reserve's repricing report has traditionally viewed transaction accounts and passbook savings as rate-insensitive, but with the growth of the Internet and competition from FinTech institutions, the mobility of these funds is highly sensitive to relative rates paid by banks versus nonbank FIs such as money market mutual funds.

Model: RSAs = $50m + $30m + $35m + $40m = $155m; RSLs = $40m + $20m + $60m + $20m = $140m. Interpretation: the classification hinges on the next repricing date, not original maturity—the 30-year floating-rate mortgages are RSAs because they reset within nine months, while the one-year consumer loans just make the cutoff. Analogy

Classifying the balance sheet is like sorting your monthly bills into "price can change this month" versus "price locked this month." A one-year gym contract signed last month is locked; a streaming subscription that resets quarterly is not. Your exposure to a general price increase depends on how much of your spending reprices soon—exactly the logic of the RSA/RSL sort.

Key Insight

The demand deposit classification is not a technicality—it can materially change measured exposure. Excluding them treats stable, low-cost funding as immune to rate rises; including them (or a portion) recognizes that deposit runoffs in high-rate environments force costly funding substitution. The correct treatment depends on depositor behavior, which in turn depends on the rate environment and competitive alternatives.

Key Takeaways

  • The classification test is: can the item's rate change within the horizon? Remaining maturity/repricing date governs, not original maturity.
  • In the source example: RSAs = $155m (including 9-month-reset floating mortgages); RSLs = $140m; rate-insensitive items include equity, demand deposits, passbook savings, and longer fixed-rate instruments.
  • Arguments against counting demand deposits as RSLs: zero explicit rate, rates insensitive to market levels, core-deposit stability.
  • Arguments for inclusion: implicit interest, and rate-driven runoffs forcing replacement with rate-sensitive funds.
  • FinTech competition and internet banking have increased the rate sensitivity of transaction and savings balances.

6. The Cumulative Gap and the Gap Ratio

Because exposures in individual buckets can offset, the FI manager can estimate cumulative gaps (CGAP) over various repricing categories. A common cumulative gap of interest is the one-year repricing gap, the sum of the gaps in all buckets up to and including one year. From Table 8–1: CGAP = (−$10m) + (−$10m) + (−$15m) + $20m = −$15 million. From the balance sheet in Table 8–2, the same measure is computed directly as total one-year RSAs minus total one-year RSLs.

If \(\Delta R_i\) is the average interest rate change affecting assets and liabilities that can be repriced within a year, the cumulative effect on net interest income is \(\Delta NII = CGAP \times \Delta R_i\). Note that a change in the dollar value and mix of rate-sensitive assets and liabilities (a change in CGAP itself) also affects the FI's net income—the gap is not static but evolves with balance sheet composition.

FIs commonly express interest rate sensitivity as a percentage of assets, the gap ratio. This normalization serves two purposes: (1) it indicates the direction of the interest rate risk exposure (positive or negative CGAP), and (2) it indicates the scale of that exposure relative to the institution's asset size, making comparisons across institutions of different sizes meaningful. This version of the gap ratio is used by regulators to measure interest rate risk. An alternative version divides RSAs by RSLs: a ratio greater than 1 indicates more rate-sensitive assets than liabilities (analogous to a positive gap), so NII rises when rates rise; a ratio below 1 indicates the opposite. In the source's example the alternative ratio is 1.107, meaning the FI holds $1.107 of RSAs for every $1.00 of RSLs in the one-year-and-less buckets.

The choice of time horizon is critical. An excessively long repricing period includes many securities repriced at different times within the period, overstating the balance sheet's rate sensitivity and leaving the FI exposed to rate changes occurring within the period. An excessively short period omits consideration of the exposure of assets and liabilities repriced immediately after the period ends, understating rate sensitivity. The shorter the buckets, the smaller the within-bucket mismatch problem—but the more aggregation issues arise at bucket boundaries.

Cumulative Gap and Gap Ratio
\[ CGAP = RSA - RSL \qquad\qquad \text{Gap ratio} = \frac{CGAP}{A} \]

Meaning of symbols: \(CGAP\) is the cumulative one-year repricing gap—the dollar difference between one-year rate-sensitive assets (\(RSA\)) and one-year rate-sensitive liabilities (\(RSL\)). \(A\) is total assets. The gap ratio expresses the gap as a fraction of total assets.

Mathematical relationship: the ratio rescales the dollar gap so that exposures can be compared across institutions: a $15 million gap is large for a $100 million bank and small for a $2 trillion bank. Interpretation: in the source example, Gap ratio = $15m / $270m = 0.056 = 5.6 percent: the FI has 5.6 percent more RSAs than RSLs in one-year-and-less buckets as a percentage of total assets. Practical use: regulators use this ratio to compare interest rate risk across institutions; managers use it to communicate exposure magnitude and direction in a single number. Assumption: the ratio inherits all classification assumptions of the underlying gap (horizon choice, deposit treatment, runoff assumptions).

Example

Model: CGAP = $155m − $140m = $15m; Gap ratio = $15m/$270m = 5.6%; alternative ratio = $155m/$140m = 1.107. Interpretation: a positive, modestly sized asset-sensitive position: NII rises if rates rise, and the exposure equals 5.6 percent of assets. Practical lesson: always report gap direction and scale; a dollar gap without the asset base is uninterpretable across institutions.

Analogy

A raw gap is like stating someone's debt as "$50,000"—meaningless without knowing their income. The gap ratio is the debt-to-income ratio of interest rate risk: it tells you whether the exposure is heavy or light relative to the size of the institution carrying it.

Key Insight

The one-year CGAP can conceal offsetting exposures within the year. A bank with $50 million more RSAs than RSLs repricing in the first six months and $50 million more RSLs than RSAs in the last six months shows a zero one-year gap [+50 + (−50) = 0] yet remains fully exposed to intra-year rate changes. The horizon length is a modeling choice that shapes the measured risk itself.

Key Takeaways

  • CGAP aggregates bucket gaps over a horizon (commonly one year): CGAP = RSA − RSL.
  • The gap ratio (CGAP/A) conveys exposure direction and scale; regulators use it to compare institutions.
  • The alternative ratio RSA/RSL > 1 (or < 1) signals asset (liability) sensitivity; 1.107 in the example.
  • Horizon choice is critical: too long overstates sensitivity (mismatches hidden within buckets); too short understates it (post-horizon repricing ignored).
  • Changes in the dollar value and mix of RSAs and RSLs change CGAP and therefore NII over time.

7. CGAP Effects: Equal Changes in Rates

The CGAP effect describes the relation between changes in interest rates and changes in net interest income when the rate change on RSAs equals the rate change on RSLs—that is, when the interest rate spread between RSAs and RSLs is unchanged. The logic follows directly from the gap equation. When CGAP is positive (more RSAs than RSLs) and rates rise, interest income increases more than interest expense, so NII rises. When CGAP is positive and rates fall, interest income falls more than interest expense, so NII falls. Symmetrically, when CGAP is negative and rates rise, NII falls; when CGAP is negative and rates fall, NII rises. In general: when CGAP is positive, the change in NII is positively related to the change in interest rates; when CGAP is negative, the change in NII is negatively related to the change in interest rates.

The size of the effect matters as much as its direction: the larger the absolute value of CGAP, the larger the expected change in NII—the larger the increase or decrease in interest income relative to interest expense. This yields a clear managerial prescription. An FI would want its CGAP to be positive when interest rates are expected to rise, and negative when interest rates are expected to fall. Positioning the gap in anticipation of rate movements is the model's primary practical application.

The full mapping of the equal-rate-change case is: (1) CGAP > 0, rates rise → NII rises; (2) CGAP > 0, rates fall → NII falls; (3) CGAP < 0, rates rise → NII falls; (4) CGAP < 0, rates fall → NII rises. These four rows summarize the model's core behavioral prediction and are the baseline against which spread effects are compared.

CGAP Effect under Equal Rate Changes
\[ \Delta NII = (RSA \times \Delta R) - (RSL \times \Delta R) = CGAP \times \Delta R \]

Meaning of symbols: \(RSA\) and \(RSL\) are the one-year rate-sensitive asset and liability totals; \(\Delta R\) is the common rate change applied to both; \(CGAP = RSA - RSL\). Mathematical relationship: because the same \(\Delta R\) multiplies both terms, the expression factors into \(CGAP \times \Delta R\)—the gap alone drives the outcome. Interpretation: the sign of \(\Delta NII\) equals the sign of \(CGAP \times \Delta R\). Practical use: given a measured CGAP, the manager can immediately compute the NII impact of any hypothesized equal rate change. Assumption: rates on RSAs and RSLs change by identical amounts (constant spread); balance sheet composition is unchanged.

Example

Model (Example 8–1 of the source): for the FI with RSA = $155m, RSL = $140m, CGAP = $15m: if rates rise 1 percent on both, \(\Delta NII = (\$155m \times 0.01) - (\$140m \times 0.01) = \$1.55m - \$1.40m = \$150{,}000\). If rates fall 1 percent on both, \(\Delta NII = \$15m \times (-0.01) = -\$150{,}000\). Interpretation: with a positive gap, rising rates add $150,000 to annual NII and falling rates subtract the same. Practical lesson: the symmetric exposure means the FI "wins" from rate increases and "loses" from decreases, in equal dollar measure, as long as the spread is constant.

Analogy

Think of a seesaw with income on one side and expense on the other. A positive CGAP puts more rate-sensitive weight on the income side: any rate movement tilts the seesaw toward income first. Rising rates lift your profit; falling rates drop it. The gap is the imbalance of weight; the rate change is the push.

Key Insight

The CGAP effect is a statement about direction and proportion, not about causation of rate movements. It says: given a rate change, here is the profit consequence under the equal-spread assumption. It is a conditional exposure mapping—managers use it to position the balance sheet, but the accuracy of the resulting profit forecast depends entirely on the rate forecast being right.

Key Takeaways

  • With equal rate changes, \(\Delta NII = CGAP \times \Delta R\): sign and size follow the gap.
  • Positive CGAP: NII moves with rates (rises when rates rise, falls when rates fall).
  • Negative CGAP: NII moves against rates (falls when rates rise, rises when rates fall).
  • Larger |CGAP| means larger NII sensitivity.
  • Managerial rule: hold a positive CGAP when rates are expected to rise; a negative CGAP when rates are expected to fall.

8. Spread Effects: Unequal Changes in Rates

The equal-rate-change assumption is a simplification. In practice, rate changes on RSAs generally differ from rate changes on RSLs: the spread between interest rates on assets and liabilities changes along with the levels of those rates. Evidence: although three-month CD rates (liability side) and prime lending rates (asset side) generally move in the same direction over 1990–2021, they are not perfectly correlated. When the Fed slashed the federal funds rate to 0–0.25 percent in March 2020 to counter the COVID-19 pandemic, the three-month CD rate plunged. Because asset and liability rates respond differently, the FI faces a spread effect in addition to the CGAP effect.

The spread effect is the effect that a change in the spread between rates on RSAs and RSLs has on net interest income as interest rates change. Its logic: if the spread between the rate on RSAs and the rate on RSLs increases, then when interest rates rise (fall), interest income increases (decreases) by more (less) than interest expense, and NII increases. If the spread decreases, then when rates rise (fall), interest income increases (decreases) less (more) than interest expense, and NII decreases. In general, regardless of the direction of the change in interest rates, a positive relation exists between changes in the spread and changes in NII: whenever the spread increases, NII increases; whenever the spread decreases, NII decreases.

The CGAP and spread effects can reinforce or offset each other. When they work in the same direction, the manager can accurately predict the direction of the change in NII. When they work in opposite directions, the net change in NII cannot be predicted without knowing the size of the CGAP and the expected change in the spread. For example, with a negative CGAP and rising rates, the CGAP effect predicts falling NII; if the spread simultaneously widens, the spread effect predicts rising NII, and the net outcome depends on relative magnitudes.

Combined CGAP and Spread Effects
\[ \Delta NII = (RSA \times \Delta R_{RSA}) - (RSL \times \Delta R_{RSL}) = \Delta\text{Interest revenue} - \Delta\text{Interest expense} \]

Meaning of symbols: \(\Delta R_{RSA}\) is the rate change on rate-sensitive assets; \(\Delta R_{RSL}\) is the (possibly different) rate change on rate-sensitive liabilities. The change in NII is the change in interest revenue minus the change in interest expense. Mathematical relationship: when \(\Delta R_{RSA} = \Delta R_{RSL}\), the expression collapses to the pure CGAP effect; when they differ, the difference \((\Delta R_{RSA} - \Delta R_{RSL})\) is the spread change, which contributes independently of the gap. Interpretation: NII responds to two distinct forces—volume mismatch (CGAP) and pricing mismatch (spread). Practical use: realistic NII forecasting requires assumptions about both the level change and the relative change of asset and liability rates. Assumption: the balance sheet composition is held fixed over the analysis period.

Example — Spread effect alone (Example 8–2)

Model: suppose RSA = RSL = $155 million, rates rise 1.2 percent on RSAs and 1.0 percent on RSLs (spread widens by 0.2 percent). Then \(\Delta NII = (\$155m \times 1.2\%) - (\$155m \times 1.0\%) = \$155m \times (1.2\% - 1.0\%) = \$310{,}000\). Interpretation: with no volume mismatch, the widening spread alone raises NII by $310,000. Practical lesson: pricing dynamics can move profit even when the gap is zero.

Example — Combined effects (Example 8–3)

Model: for the FI with RSA = $155m, RSL = $140m, rates fall 1 percent on RSAs and 1.2 percent on RSLs: \(\Delta NII = (\$155m \times (-0.01)) - (\$140m \times (-0.012)) = -\$1.55m - (-\$1.68m) = \$130{,}000\). Interpretation: the CGAP effect (RSA > RSL with falling rates) pushes NII down—by $150,000 under equal changes—yet the spread widens enough that NII actually rises by $130,000. Practical lesson: when CGAP and spread effects oppose each other, only their relative magnitudes determine the outcome; direction cannot be inferred from the gap alone.

Analogy

CGAP is like the quantity of fuel you buy and burn each week; the spread is like the price gap between what you sell energy for and what you pay for it. Your profit can change because quantities are mismatched, because prices move differently, or both—and when one force pushes up while the other pushes down, only the arithmetic of magnitudes reveals the net result.

Key Insight

A positive relation exists between spread changes and NII changes regardless of the direction of interest rates. This means an FI can profit from falling rates if its funding costs fall faster than its asset yields—demonstrating that gap management alone is an incomplete profit strategy. Spread behavior must be forecast alongside rate levels.

Key Takeaways

  • Asset and liability rates are not perfectly correlated; the spread between RSA and RSL rates changes as rates move.
  • Spread effect: spread widens → NII rises; spread narrows → NII falls, irrespective of the direction of rate changes.
  • When CGAP and spread effects align, NII direction is predictable; when they conflict, only relative magnitudes decide.
  • Full forecasting requires \(\Delta NII = (RSA \times \Delta R_{RSA}) - (RSL \times \Delta R_{RSL})\) with separate rate assumptions.
  • Empirically, CD rates and prime rates co-move but imperfectly—e.g., the March 2020 fed funds cut to 0–0.25 percent drove the three-month CD rate sharply down.

9. Practical Application and Balance Sheet Management

The repricing gap is the historically dominant measure of interest rate risk and remains the main measure used by small community banks and thrifts. Its practical virtues are conceptual accessibility and direct usability: an FI can use the gap to structure its assets and liabilities—or to take positions off the balance sheet—so as to profit from a projected interest rate change. The workflow is: (1) classify every balance sheet item by next repricing date within the chosen horizon; (2) compute the gap (or CGAP) and gap ratio; (3) form a rate expectation; (4) reposition the gap—positive if rates are expected to rise, negative if expected to fall—or hedge it; (5) translate the gap and expected rate/spread changes into a forecast \(\Delta NII\).

Real disclosures show the model in action. Flushing Financial Corporation (2021 interest rate sensitivity report) reported a cumulative three-month repricing gap to total assets of −8.03 percent and a cumulative one-year gap ratio of −6.79 percent—more RSLs than RSAs. If interest rates fell in 2021, the institution was positioned to see NII increase, because its large volume of RSLs (relative to RSAs) would reprice at lower rates. Management thus set the balance sheet in expectation of rate decreases over the next year. Simultaneously, Flushing reported a cumulative three-year gap ratio of +13.72 percent and a five-year ratio of +29.73 percent—a position set up in expectation of rate increases over the five-year horizon. The example illustrates that gap positioning can differ by horizon within a single institution.

Wells Fargo & Company (Form 10-Q, June 2021) frames the broader sources of interest rate risk created in its role as financial intermediary: (1) assets and liabilities may mature or reprice at different times (if assets reprice faster than liabilities and rates generally rise, earnings initially increase); (2) assets and liabilities may reprice at the same time but by different amounts; (3) short- and long-term market rates may change by different amounts (yield curve shape affects new loan yields and funding costs differently); (4) remaining maturities may shorten or lengthen as rates change (sharply higher long-term mortgage rates can slow the paydown of mortgage-backed securities, affecting portfolio income); and (5) rates directly or indirectly affect loan demand, collateral values, credit losses, mortgage origination volume, and the fair value of MSRs and other instruments. Wells Fargo assesses risk by comparing outcomes under net interest income simulations across many rate scenarios differing in direction, magnitude, speed, and projected yield curve shape—requiring assumptions about loan originations, prepayment speeds, deposit flows and mix, and pricing strategies. Its recent simulations indicated it would benefit from higher rates because its assets would reprice faster and to a greater degree than its liabilities.

These disclosures show a progression: the simple gap identifies direction and rough magnitude of exposure; simulation-based approaches extend the same income-focused logic with richer behavioral assumptions. Both remain earnings-oriented; the market value dimension requires the duration-based models of the next chapter.

Example

Model: Flushing Financial's gap ratios: −8.03% (3-month), −6.79% (1-year), +13.72% (3-year), +29.73% (5-year). Interpretation: liability-sensitive short, asset-sensitive long—deliberately positioned for falling rates in the near term and rising rates over five years. Practical lesson: gap management is horizon-specific; a single institution can rationally hold opposite exposures at different points on the curve, and the gap report makes this strategy transparent to regulators and investors.

Analogy

Positioning the gap is like a farmer choosing what to plant based on the weather forecast. If rain (rate increases) is expected, plant the crop that thrives in rain (positive gap); if drought is expected, plant accordingly (negative gap). The farmer cannot control the weather—only the exposure to it. Flushing planted one crop for the next season and a different one for the five-year horizon.

Key Insight

Gap positioning is an expression of a rate expectation, not a guarantee of profit. Flushing's negative one-year gap pays off only if rates actually fall. A wrong rate forecast converts a "well-positioned" balance sheet into a loss-making one—the model measures exposure; it does not eliminate forecasting risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Practical workflow: classify by repricing date → compute CGAP and gap ratio → form rate/spread expectations → reposition or hedge → forecast ΔNII.
  • Flushing Financial: −8.03% (3-month) and −6.79% (1-year) gap ratios (positioned for falling rates); +13.72% (3-year) and +29.73% (5-year) (positioned for rising rates).
  • Wells Fargo identifies five risk channels: repricing timing differences, repricing magnitude differences, yield curve effects, maturity/prepayment changes, and rate effects on demand, collateral, credit losses, volume, and fair values.
  • Large FIs supplement gap analysis with NII simulations across multiple rate scenarios and behavioral assumptions.
  • The gap can be restructured on the balance sheet or offset with off-balance-sheet instruments.

10. Weaknesses of the Repricing Model

Despite its use by the vast majority of U.S. depository institutions, the repricing model has four major shortcomings that have led regulators to call for more comprehensive models (such as the duration gap model): (1) it ignores market value effects of interest rate changes; (2) it is overaggregative; (3) it fails to deal with rate-insensitive asset and liability runoffs and prepayments; and (4) it ignores cash flows from off-balance-sheet activities.

Weakness 1: Market value effects. Interest rate changes have a market value effect in addition to an income effect: the present values of the cash flows on assets and liabilities change as rates change, beyond the immediate interest received or paid. In fact, the present values (and, where relevant, market prices) of virtually all assets and liabilities on an FI's balance sheet change when rates change. The repricing model ignores this effect, implicitly assuming book value accounting: asset and liability values are reported at historic cost. A 30-year bond purchased ten years ago when rates were 13 percent is reported at the same book value whether current rates are 13 percent or 7 percent, even though its market value would differ substantially. Under market value accounting, gains and losses would appear on the balance sheet as rates change. Consequently, the repricing gap is only a partial measure of the true interest rate risk exposure of an FI—it captures the income effect but omits the capital loss effect measured by the maturity and duration models.

Weakness 2: Overaggregation. Defining buckets over ranges of maturities discards information about the distribution of assets and liabilities within each bucket. Dollar values of RSAs and RSLs in a bucket may be equal, yet liabilities may be repriced toward the end of the bucket's range while assets are repriced toward the beginning—so a rate change affects cash flows in ways the bucket gap cannot capture. The source's illustration (Figure 8–3, three-month to six-month bucket): $50 million more RSAs than RSLs reprice between months 3 and 4, while $50 million more RSLs than RSAs reprice between months 5 and 6. The report shows a zero gap [+50 + (−50) = 0], but the assets and liabilities are mismatched within the bucket. The shorter the bucket range, the smaller the problem; one-day buckets would give a far more accurate picture. Reportedly, many large FIs maintain internal systems indicating repricing gaps on any given future day (252 days out, 1,329 days out, etc.)—suggesting that although regulators examine only relatively wide bucket ranges, managers can implement daily internal gap reporting.

Weakness 3: Runoffs and prepayments. The simple model assumes, for example, that all consumer loans mature in one year and all conventional mortgages in 30 years. In reality, the FI continuously originates and retires loans as it creates and retires deposits. Some "30-year" mortgages are in their 29th year; others will be prepaid early as holders refinance or sell their houses—so proceeds are reinvested at current market rates within the year. Moreover, even rate-insensitive assets and liabilities pay some principal and/or interest back to the FI in any given year. The FI therefore receives a runoff cash flow—periodic cash flow of interest and principal amortization payments on long-term assets, such as conventional mortgages—that can be reinvested at market rates. This runoff component of a rate-insensitive item is itself rate-sensitive. The manager can address this within the repricing framework by identifying, for each asset and liability, the estimated dollar cash flow that will run off within the next year and adding these amounts to rate-sensitive assets and liabilities.

Weakness 4: Off-balance-sheet cash flows. The RSAs and RSLs in the model generally include only on-balance-sheet items, yet rate changes affect the cash flows of many off-balance-sheet instruments. For example, an FI that hedged its interest rate risk with an interest rate futures contract receives or pays daily cash flows through the marking-to-market process as rates change—flows that may offset on-balance-sheet gap exposure. The simple repricing model ignores these offsetting cash flows, although they could and should be included.

Runoff Adjustment Concept
\[ RSA^{*} = RSA + \text{(expected one-year runoffs on assets)} \qquad RSL^{*} = RSL + \text{(expected one-year runoffs on liabilities)} \]

Meaning of symbols: \(RSA^{*}\) and \(RSL^{*}\) are runoff-adjusted rate-sensitive totals; the runoff amounts are the estimated dollar cash flows of principal and interest amortization (and prepayments) expected within the year on items otherwise classified as rate-insensitive. Mathematical relationship: because runoffs are reinvested (or refinanced) at current market rates, they behave like rate-sensitive volumes and belong in the gap. Interpretation: ignoring runoffs understates the true repricing exposure; including them changes both the gap's size and possibly its sign. Practical use: the source explicitly recommends identifying estimated one-year runoffs per item and adding them to RSAs and RSLs. Assumption: runoff estimates require behavioral forecasts (prepayment speeds, amortization schedules), which introduce their own uncertainty.

Example

Model (overaggregation): in the 3–6 month bucket, +$50m of net RSAs reprice in months 3–4 and −$50m of net RSAs (net RSLs) reprice in months 5–6; the reported bucket gap is zero. Interpretation: a rate change in month 4 hits the unmatched assets while the matching liabilities are still locked in—the zero gap conceals real exposure. Practical lesson: bucket-level gaps are averages that hide timing mismatches; finer buckets (down to daily internal reporting at large FIs) reduce but do not eliminate the problem.

Analogy

The four weaknesses are like four blind spots in a car's mirror system: the book value convention is a mirror that shows where objects were, not where they are now (market values); overaggregation is a wide-angle mirror that blurs everything inside the frame; ignoring runoffs is forgetting that passengers get in and out during the trip; ignoring off-balance-sheet flows is driving while forgetting the trailer attached behind you also reacts to the road.

Key Insight

The repricing model measures only the income effect of rate changes; the capital (market value) effect is entirely absent. Because the present values of virtually all balance sheet items change when rates change, the repricing gap is a partial measure of true interest rate exposure. This is precisely why regulators and large banks have moved toward duration-based, market value models—which additionally capture leverage and cash flow timing—while the repricing model survives as the workhorse of smaller institutions.

Key Takeaways

  • Weakness 1 — Market value effects ignored: book value accounting omits capital gains/losses on assets and liabilities as rates change; the gap is only a partial exposure measure.
  • Weakness 2 — Overaggregation: within-bucket timing mismatches are hidden (the +50/−50 zero-gap example); shorter buckets and daily internal gap systems mitigate it.
  • Weakness 3 — Runoffs and prepayments: amortization and prepayment cash flows from "rate-insensitive" items are themselves rate-sensitive and should be added to RSAs/RSLs.
  • Weakness 4 — Off-balance-sheet flows ignored: marking-to-market cash flows on hedges such as futures can offset on-balance-sheet gaps but are excluded from the simple model.
  • These weaknesses motivated the BIS-endorsed shift toward market value–based (duration gap) measurement.

11. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

The repricing model is simple, and its simplicity invites specific, recurring interpretation errors. The following cautions are all grounded in the source's own analysis and should be treated as a checklist whenever the model is applied.

1. Confusing income exposure with value exposure. The model measures the change in net interest income only. It does not measure changes in the market value of assets, liabilities, or net worth. Concluding that "a zero gap means no interest rate risk" is incorrect: the capital loss effect operates on virtually all balance sheet items even when no rates reprice within the horizon.

2. Misreading the gap as a causal or predictive statement. The equation \(\Delta NII = CGAP \times \Delta R\) is conditional: it maps an assumed rate change into a profit change. It does not predict rate movements, and a favorable gap position becomes a loss position if the rate forecast is wrong. Gap positioning expresses an expectation; it does not create certainty.

3. Ignoring the spread effect. Applying \(\Delta NII = CGAP \times \Delta R\) assumes equal rate changes on RSAs and RSLs. Because asset and liability rates are imperfectly correlated (e.g., prime rates versus CD rates), the spread effect can reinforce or offset the CGAP effect. When the two conflict, NII direction cannot be determined from the gap alone—only from the relative magnitudes of gap and spread changes (as in the source's Example 8–3, where a $150,000 CGAP-effect loss was overwhelmed by a spread-effect gain, yielding +$130,000).

4. Mishandling demand deposits and passbook savings. Mechanically classifying all demand deposits as rate-insensitive ignores implicit interest and rate-driven runoffs; mechanically classifying them as rate-sensitive ignores their core-deposit character and regulated zero explicit rates. The classification is behavioral and environment-dependent, and competition from money market funds and FinTech has increased the sensitivity of these balances.

5. Trusting bucket-level gaps (overaggregation). A zero bucket gap can conceal large within-bucket mismatches (+$50m early, −$50m late). The reported gap is an average over the bucket; exposure to intra-period rate changes remains.

6. Ignoring runoffs and prepayments. Treating long-term amortizing assets as fully rate-insensitive understates the gap: their periodic principal and interest payments—and prepayments—are reinvested at current market rates and are therefore rate-sensitive. Estimated one-year runoffs should be added to RSAs and RSLs.

7. Ignoring off-balance-sheet positions. Hedges such as interest rate futures generate daily marking-to-market cash flows that may offset on-balance-sheet gaps. A gap computed from balance sheet items alone can misstate the FI's net exposure.

8. Ignoring units and scale. A dollar gap is uninterpretable without the asset base. Always report the gap ratio (CGAP/A) alongside the dollar gap, and remember the ratio inherits every classification assumption of the underlying gap.

9. Forgetting that the gap itself moves. Changes in the dollar value and mix of rate-sensitive assets and liabilities change CGAP over time; a gap measured today is not a permanent feature of the balance sheet.

Example

Model: the source's combined-effect calculation: CGAP = +$15m, rates fall 1% on RSAs and 1.2% on RSLs → \(\Delta NII = -\$1.55m - (-\$1.68m) = +\$130{,}000\). Interpretation: the naive CGAP-only forecast (−$150,000) has the wrong sign. Practical lesson: always ask whether the equal-spread assumption holds before translating a gap into a profit forecast; the spread effect can reverse the conclusion entirely.

Analogy

Using the repricing model without these cautions is like navigating with a speedometer alone: it tells you your current speed (income exposure) but not your direction of travel (market values), the terrain ahead (bucket timing), the fuel remaining (runoffs), or the trailer behind you (off-balance-sheet items). A complete journey requires the full dashboard—duration and market value models included.

Key Insight

Every number the repricing model produces is assumption-dependent. The gap depends on the horizon, the deposit classification, runoff estimates, and the treatment of off-balance-sheet items; the profit forecast depends additionally on the equal-spread assumption and the rate scenario. The model is a transparent, useful first measure of earnings exposure—but a partial one, and regulators and large institutions supplement it with market value–based duration measures precisely because of what it leaves out.

Key Takeaways

  • Zero gap ≠ zero risk: market value effects exist outside the model's book value framework.
  • The gap equation is conditional exposure mapping, not a rate forecast; wrong rate expectations convert positioning into losses.
  • Check the equal-spread assumption: CGAP and spread effects can offset, and only relative magnitudes determine NII direction.
  • Demand deposit and passbook classification is behavioral; implicit interest and runoffs argue for partial rate sensitivity.
  • Adjust for within-bucket timing (overaggregation), runoffs/prepayments, and off-balance-sheet cash flows before trusting the gap.
  • Report the gap ratio (CGAP/A) for scale; remember CGAP itself changes with balance sheet mix over time.
  • The model endures among small DIs for its simplicity, but comprehensive risk measurement requires the duration gap framework of the next chapter.
