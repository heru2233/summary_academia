Introduction

The study Enterprise risk management, ESG disclosure and firm performance: evidence from the manufacturing sector in Vietnam investigates how enterprise risk management (ERM) influences firm value and profitability, and whether environmental, social, and governance (ESG) disclosure moderates these relationships. The research is motivated by contradictory findings in the existing literature regarding the impact of ERM on corporate performance, especially in emerging economies where ERM adoption is uneven and institutional contexts differ markedly from developed markets. By focusing on Vietnam’s manufacturing sector—a cornerstone of the national economy contributing roughly 34–35 % of GDP and highly exposed to supply‑chain disruptions, energy volatility, and environmental pressures—the authors aim to fill several gaps: (1) the lack of consistent evidence on ERM‑performance links in emerging markets; (2) the scarcity of sector‑specific studies in manufacturing; (3) the limited exploration of the interplay between ERM and ESG; and (4) the reliance on outdated ERM frameworks rather than the latest COSO 2017 guidelines. The study poses two research questions: (RQ1) How does ERM, measured according to the COSO 2017 framework, affect firm value and profitability in Vietnam’s manufacturing sector? (RQ2) Does ESG performance moderate the relationship between ERM and corporate performance, thereby enhancing ERM’s effectiveness? To answer these questions, the authors construct a COSO 2017‑based ERM index and an ESG disclosure index derived from the S&P Global Corporate Sustainability Assessment (CSA), and employ a two‑stage regression with a control function approach to address endogeneity.

Literature Review

The literature review begins by defining ERM according to COSO (2004) as a process that identifies potential events and provides reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of entity objectives. Recent scholarship stresses that ERM should be viewed not only as a threat‑mitigation tool but also as a strategic mechanism for identifying opportunities that enhance long‑term competitiveness (Lundqvist, 2015; Ogutu et al., 2018). This perspective shifts risk management from a purely defensive posture to a proactive, value‑creating function that fosters innovation and strategic resilience.

ESG disclosure, formalized by the UN Global Compact in 2004, requires firms to report on environmental performance, social responsibility, and governance quality. Investors use ESG information to assess non‑financial risks, evaluate long‑term viability, and identify firms with strong sustainability practices. Prior work shows that robust ESG reporting enhances legitimacy, stakeholder trust, and access to capital (Friede et al., 2015; Aouadi & Marsat, 2018).

The review highlights the complementary nature of ERM and ESG. ERM offers a structured approach to risk oversight, while ESG introduces transparency and accountability that reduce information asymmetry. When integrated, the two frameworks can reinforce each other, improving governance, risk anticipation, and stakeholder engagement (Callahan & Soileau, 2017; Chairani & Siregar, 2021). However, the magnitude of these benefits varies across institutional settings, and emerging markets such as Vietnam remain understudied.

Theoretical foundations are drawn from agency theory, resource‑based view (RBV), and organizational inertia theory. Agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976) posits that ERM reduces conflicts of interest between principals (owners) and agents (managers) by increasing transparency and oversight, thereby enhancing firm performance. Conversely, RBV argues that implementing ERM consumes valuable resources—systems, personnel, training, compliance—that could otherwise be deployed for revenue‑generating activities, potentially harming short‑term profitability (Barney, 1991). Organizational inertia theory suggests that formalized ERM frameworks may reduce flexibility, hinder innovation, and delay decision‑making, negatively affecting operational performance (Hannan & Freeman, 1984).

In the Vietnamese context, ERM adoption is uneven, often driven by compliance rather than strategic integration. Corporate governance structures are still evolving, information disclosure practices are inconsistent, and investor protection is relatively weak. The manufacturing sector’s deep exposure to supply‑chain disruptions, volatile energy costs, environmental challenges, and increasing ESG‑related pressures from export markets heightens operational risks, making ERM a potentially valuable yet costly endeavor. These considerations lead to the development of two hypotheses: (H1) ERM has a positive impact on firm performance; (H2) ESG strengthens the positive relationship between ERM and firm performance.

Methodology

Sample selection The sample comprises 200 listed manufacturing firms from the Ho Chi Minh Stock Exchange (HOSE) and Hanoi Stock Exchange (HNX) observed over the period 2017–2022, yielding 1,200 firm‑year observations. Firms with incomplete data, those that went public after 2018, and outliers beyond the 5th or 95th percentiles were excluded to ensure consistency and mitigate distortions from listing age and extreme financial values.

Variables measurement Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) index – Constructed from 16 binary criteria derived from the COSO 2017 framework. The criteria capture two dimensions: (1) ERM environment (risk strategy, communication, Chief Risk Officer appointment, risk committee, risk governance structure, risk appetite, risk‑aware culture) and (2) ERM implementation (risk identification, assessment, prioritization, mitigation, monitoring, reporting). Each criterion equals 1 if disclosed in the annual report (per Circular 155/2015/TT‑BTC) and 0 otherwise. The ERM index is the average score across the 16 criteria, yielding a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 1. Independent coding by two trained raters, with discrepancies resolved through discussion, enhances reliability.

ESG disclosure index – Based on the S&P Global Corporate Sustainability Assessment (CSA) framework, 23 binary indicators are constructed: 8 environmental, 6 social, and 9 governance criteria. Each indicator equals 1 if disclosed in the annual or sustainability report and 0 otherwise. The ESG index is the average of these 23 items, also ranging from 0 to 1. Double‑check coding ensures consistency and minimizes subjectivity.

Control variables – Leverage (total debt/total equity), firm size (natural log of sales), firm age (natural log of years since establishment), and liquidity (current assets/current liabilities) are included to capture financial and structural characteristics that may affect performance.

Dependent variables – Firm performance is measured by (1) Return on Assets (ROA) = net profit/total assets, reflecting accounting‑based profitability; and (2) Tobin’s Q = (market value of equity + book value of debt)/book value of total assets, reflecting market‑based firm value.

Econometric model – The baseline model examines the direct impact of ERM on performance:

\[ \text{Performance}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{ERM}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{Lev}_{it} + \beta_3 \ln(\text{Age})_{it} + \beta_4 \ln(\text{Sales})_{it} + \beta_5 \text{Liquid}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

The moderating model adds ESG disclosure and the interaction term (ERM × ESG):

\[ \text{Performance}_{it} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{ERM}_{it} + \beta_2 \text{ESG}_{it} + \beta_3 (\text{ERM}_{it}\times\text{ESG}_{it}) + \beta_4 \text{Lev}_{it} + \beta_5 \ln(\text{Age})_{it} + \beta_6 \ln(\text{Sales})_{it} + \beta_7 \text{Liquid}_{it} + \varepsilon_{it} \]

To address potential endogeneity—where firms with certain governance structures or industry norms may simultaneously adopt higher ERM and experience better performance—the authors employ a control function approach. Instruments for ERM include environmental uncertainty (measured as the coefficient of variation of sales, capital expenditures, and net income over a five‑year rolling window), board monitoring (ratio of board members to ln(total assets)), and industry‑average ERM (peer effect). The control function proceeds in two stages: (1) regress ERM on the instruments and controls to obtain residuals; (2) include the residuals and their interaction with ESG in the second‑stage performance regression, thereby purging endogeneity from both ERM and the ERM × ESG term.

Findings

Descriptive statistics Supplementary Table 3 summarizes the sample. The mean ROA is 4.6 %, indicating modest average profitability. Tobin’s Q averages 0.91 (range 0.4–1.9), suggesting that, on average, the market values firms below their book value, with considerable dispersion reflecting heterogeneous market perceptions. The ERM index averages 0.145 (14.5 %), revealing limited adoption of formal ERM practices among the sampled manufacturers. The ESG index averages 0.206 (20.6 %), pointing to moderate engagement with sustainability disclosure. Control variables display wide variation: leverage ranges from low to high, firm size (ln sales) spans approximately 10–14, firm age (ln age) from roughly 2 to 4, and liquidity varies between 0.5 and 3.0. Correlation analysis (Supplementary Table 4) shows all pairwise correlations below 0.5, alleviating multicollinearity concerns.

Regression results – Tobin’s Q Supplementary Table 5 presents the outcomes for market‑based firm value. In Model 1 (direct effect), ERM exhibits a positive and statistically significant coefficient of 0.718 (p < 0.01), indicating that stronger ERM frameworks are associated with higher Tobin’s Q. When ESG and the interaction term are introduced in Model 2, the ERM coefficient diminishes to 0.121 (p < 0.10), while ESG shows a strong positive effect (1.961, p < 0.01) and the interaction term is positive and significant (0.352, p < 0.10), suggesting that ESG amplifies the value‑enhancing impact of ERM. The first‑stage regression (Model 3) confirms the relevance of the instruments: board monitoring negatively affects ERM adoption (−0.226, p < 0.05), whereas industry‑average ERM exerts a strong positive influence (0.813, p < 0.01). Model 4, which incorporates the control function residuals, yields the final estimates: ERM retains a positive and significant effect on Tobin’s Q (0.557, p < 0.01); the ESG × ERM interaction is significantly positive (1.230, p < 0.01); and ESG itself remains positively associated with Tobin’s Q (1.602, p < 0.01). All control variables behave as expected: firm size (ln sales) has a negative coefficient (−0.035), leverage a positive coefficient (0.022), firm age a positive coefficient (0.048), and liquidity a small positive coefficient (0.009).

Regression results – ROA Supplementary Table 6 reports the findings for accounting‑based profitability. Model 1 shows a negative and significant direct effect of ERM on ROA (−0.162, p < 0.01), implying that, on average, stronger ERM implementation is linked to lower short‑term profitability. In Model 2, after adding ESG and the interaction, the ERM coefficient remains negative but smaller in magnitude (−0.095, p < 0.10), ESG exhibits a positive direct effect (0.412, p < 0.01), and the interaction term is positive and significant at the 10 % level (0.198, p < 0.10). The control‑function adjusted Model 4 confirms these patterns: ERM’s negative impact on ROA persists (−0.110, p < 0.05); the ESG × ERM interaction remains positive and significant (0.185, p < 0.10); and ESG retains a beneficial direct effect on ROA (0.380, p < 0.01). Control variables display similar signs as in the Tobin’s Q regressions.

Interpretation The results reveal a clear temporal distinction. ERM enhances market‑based firm value (Tobin’s Q) but depresses short‑term accounting profitability (ROA). ESG disclosure serves as a moderator that strengthens the positive influence of ERM on Tobin’s Q and mitigates its adverse effect on ROA. Specifically, a one‑unit increase in the ERM index raises Tobin’s Q by approximately 0.56 points, while the same increase reduces ROA by about 0.11 points. However, when ESG disclosure is high, the net effect of ERM on ROA becomes less negative (the interaction term adds roughly +0.19 to ROA for each unit of ERM × ESG), and the positive effect on Tobin’s Q is amplified (the interaction contributes about +1.23 to Tobin’s Q per unit of ERM × ESG). These findings support the view that ERM should be regarded as a long‑term strategic investment rather than a short‑term profitability tool, especially when complemented by robust ESG practices that improve transparency, stakeholder trust, and alignment with sustainability objectives.

Conclusion

The study concludes that enterprise risk management, when measured according to the COSO 2017 framework, exerts a dual‑faceted impact on firm performance in Vietnam’s manufacturing sector. On the one hand, ERM significantly boosts market‑based valuation (Tobin’s Q), signaling to investors that the firm possesses superior governance, transparency, and long‑term resilience. On the other hand, ERM imposes short‑term costs that reduce accounting profitability (ROA), likely reflecting expenditures on risk‑management systems, personnel, training, and procedural formalization that temporarily divert resources from profit‑generating activities.

Crucially, ESG disclosure moderates both relationships. Firms with stronger ESG reporting experience a magnified positive effect of ERM on Tobin’s Q and a dampened negative effect on ROA. This synergy arises because ESG enhances transparency, reduces information asymmetry, strengthens stakeholder relations, and aligns risk‑management activities with broader sustainability goals, thereby allowing the firm to translate risk governance into tangible financial and non‑financial benefits.

Theoretically, the findings refine agency theory by showing that governance improvements via ERM may yield asymmetric effects across performance metrics and time horizons: market participants promptly recognize the reduction in agency costs, whereas operational efficiency gains may materialize only after a lag. The results also extend the resource‑based view by positioning ERM and ESG as complementary strategic capabilities that, when combined, generate inimitability and long‑term competitive advantage.

From a practical standpoint, managers and boards should treat ERM as a strategic, long‑term investment, anticipating possible short‑term profitability pressures during implementation. Integrating ESG into ERM frameworks can alleviate these pressures, enhance legitimacy, and create additional value. Policymakers are encouraged to promote disclosure standards aligned with COSO 2017 and internationally recognized ESG reporting frameworks to improve market discipline and transparency. Investors may leverage ERM‑ESG information as a gauge of risk‑management maturity and sustainability performance when assessing firms in emerging markets.

The study acknowledges limitations: ERM and ESG measures rely on publicly disclosed narrative information, which may not fully capture internal practices; the sample is confined to manufacturing firms, limiting cross‑sector generalizability; and the analysis spans a six‑year period, potentially missing longer‑term dynamics. Future research could broaden the sample to multiple sectors and countries, employ alternative measurement techniques such as machine‑learning‑based textual analytics, and explore additional performance dimensions like risk‑adjusted returns, innovation outcomes, and long‑term volatility reduction.
