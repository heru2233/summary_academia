1. Overview: The Case for Incremental Innovation

Radiology stands at a curious crossroads. On one hand, the field has been built on a pantheon of singular geniuses—Thomas Edison (fluoroscopy), Egas Moniz (angiography), Godfrey Hounsfield (CT), Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield (MRI), and Charles Dotter (angioplasty). On the other hand, the popular narrative of disruptive innovation—particularly the doomsday prediction that computer algorithms will displace radiologists—creates a paralyzing anxiety that can blind practices to the more manageable, near-term opportunities staring them in the face. The central thesis of Rubin and Abramson's work is elegantly simple yet frequently overlooked: while inspiration may arise from a single mind, implementation requires organization. Innovation is as much (or more) dependent on organizational characteristics as it is on individual conception. The authors argue that radiology practices of ALL sizes can and must engage in incremental innovation—the process of making improvements or additions while maintaining the core product or service model—as a strategic tool for preservation and growth. This article is fundamentally a practical playbook. It doesn't offer utopian visions of a transformed radiology business, but rather a grounded framework for practices to enhance performance in the near-term without completely disrupting their underlying operations. The three pillars—culture, structure, and process—form an integrated system that, when adequately addressed, creates an environment where value-maximizing strategies are identified, filtered, and implemented.
Think of disruptive innovation as a complete kitchen renovation—tearing down walls, installing new plumbing, rewiring the electricity. It's expensive, risky, and takes months. Incremental innovation, by contrast, is like upgrading your chef's knife, reorganizing the pantry, or installing a new spice rack. You're still running the same kitchen, but you're getting better, faster, and more efficient at producing outstanding meals. Both approaches can transform your restaurant, but the incremental path is accessible to every operation, regardless of size or budget.
Key point: Radiologists must not be "blinded by distant headlights" (the threat of disruptive AI and machine learning) "at the expense of missed near-term opportunities." Innovation is a tool for BOTH great leaps AND incremental steps. The incremental steps are within your reach today.

2. Defining Innovation: Beyond the Buzzword

Innovation is a term with a rich typology, but in popular usage it has been reduced to something narrow: the creation and application of technology tools or new products for competitive strategy. The authors deliberately expand this definition because they want radiologists to understand that innovation encompasses a much broader range of activities—new operating procedures, service enhancements, business strategies, partnerships, and even small workflow adjustments. The formal definition proposed is: "Innovation is the development and implementation of creative ideas for advancing and evolving the mission of an organization." Equivalently: "Innovation is the purposeful creation of value-enhancing change." Three critical boundaries emerge from this definition: 1. **Innovation ≠ all change.** Not every change qualifies as innovative. Change is innovative only when it involves testing and translating ideas into value-generating solutions. 2. **Innovation ≠ creativity for its own sake.** Advancing creative ideas without implementation or value does NOT count. The mobile app that beautifully displays imaging studies on smartphones is exciting in its newness, but unless it solves a real problem and creates value, it is not innovation. 3. **Innovation requires discipline.** True organizational innovation demands: adherence to organizational goals, careful attention to capabilities and priorities, informed decision-making, determined execution, and thoughtful communication. The article distinguishes two primary types of innovation: - **Incremental/sustaining innovation**—improvements or additions while maintaining the core business model - **Disruptive/radical innovation**—breakthroughs that fundamentally alter the business model and market While radical innovation tends to favor large organizations with broad expertise, incremental innovation is intrinsically accessible to radiology departments of even modest size. This is the democratization of innovation—you don't need to be Google or Apple to be a successful innovator in your practice.

CONCEPTUAL FORMULAE

Innovation = Creative Ideas × Implementation × Value Creation

Incremental Innovation = Improvements + Additions (while maintaining core service model)

Disruptive Innovation = Breakthroughs (that fundamentally alter business model and market)

Organizational Innovation = Creativity + Discipline (goal adherence + capability assessment + informed decisions + execution + communication)

Competence-Enhancing Innovation = New knowledge building upon prior expertise (augments value of experienced workers)

Competence-Destroying Innovation = Prior knowledge rendered obsolete (replaces existing competencies)

Example: The Hybrid Reality

Consider a radiology practice that implements a structured reporting system to replace free-text prose dictation. For one senior radiologist who has refined his dictation style over 25 years, this is a competence-destroying innovation—it reduces the value of his rhetorical skill. For a young radiologist who trained exclusively with structured reporting templates, this is a competence-enhancing innovation—it builds on what she learned in residency.

Interpretation: The same initiative can be perceived differently by different individuals. Leaders must identify these perceptions and manage transitions accordingly, offering retraining or alternative roles to those whose competencies are threatened.

If innovation were a garden, incremental innovation is tending to the plants you already have—watering, pruning, fertilizing—to get a better harvest this season. Disruptive innovation is planting an entirely new crop variety, possibly in a different field, hoping to create a new market for your produce. Most small farms can do the former; very few can afford the latter. But a farmer who ONLY tends to existing plants and never experiments with improved varieties may eventually become obsolete.
The critical boundary condition: All innovation involves change, but not all change is innovative. The difference is value creation. A practice that adopts a new billing software just to "keep up with the times" without improving revenue cycle, reducing errors, or enhancing the user experience is engaging in change, not innovation. Innovation must solve a problem.

3. Why Innovate? The Ambidextrous Organization

O'Reilly and Tushman's concept of the "ambidextrous organization" provides the strategic rationale for incremental innovation. An ambidextrous organization is one that successfully balances two fundamentally different activities: 1. **Exploitation**—applying existing competencies for ongoing value creation (doing what you already do, but better) 2. **Exploration**—acquiring new competencies and experimenting to create new products and processes (doing new things) In the corporate world, these are often segregated into discrete organizational units. Radiology practices may be too small for formal segregation, but leaders must still foster conditions that allow the organization to advance simultaneously along BOTH dimensions. Supporting exploitation ensures today's revenue. Supporting exploration ensures tomorrow's survival. The authors identify five logical targets for radiology practice exploration: 1. Adapting to changing market conditions 2. Avoiding obsolescence through adoption of new technologies 3. Designing to enhance clinical quality and patient experience 4. Aligning to satisfy parent organizational goals 5. Building resilience for whatever disruptions the future may bring The urgency of this balancing act is underscored by the current landscape: cloud-based image sharing, molecular diagnostics, precision health, population health management, large-scale multisource analytics, and new payment methodologies are all potential innovation targets on radiology's horizon. Meanwhile, deep learning-powered algorithms that can distinguish ocelots from margays in billions of online images foreshadow what may be possible in image interpretation. Incremental innovation is the vehicle to navigate these changes without a full structural overhaul.
A successful professional sports team must do two things simultaneously: it must execute its current game plan effectively (exploitation), while also developing new plays, training young players, and studying upcoming opponents (exploration). Teams that only execute their current plays without innovation become predictable and lose. Teams that constantly change plays without mastering fundamentals never win. The championship teams balance both—they exploit proven strengths while continuously exploring new strategic options. Radiology practice is no different.

Vignette: Why Innovate—The CT Colonography Decision

Consider a group deciding whether to offer CT colonography as a new service. The exploitation dimension argues for optimizing their existing CT protocol efficiency and referring physician relationships. The exploration dimension argues for building CT colonography capability line-item by line-item: reading literature, attending conferences, performing a site visit to observe an established service, studying approaches from outpatient surgery centers and the hospitality industry regarding patient-friendly implementation.

The "directed learning" (specific focus on CT colonography) and "undirected learning" (broader study of innovation adoption in other industries) together create the capacity to make an informed deployment decision.

Incremental innovation sustains a practice over months-to-years; radical innovation may be necessary over a longer horizon. But you cannot skip to radical innovation without first mastering incremental innovation. The practice that cannot successfully implement a new structured reporting template will not survive an AI-driven practice transformation. Incremental innovation builds the organizational muscle and cultural DNA for all future change.

4. Culture: Creating a Fertile Environment for Innovation

Culture is defined as "an organization's collective set of values, principles, and beliefs that affect decisions, behaviors, and interpersonal and group interactions." The opening vignette of this section is deliberately provocative and disturbing—the department chair's suggestion to change trauma CT reading workflow is met with hostility, the young body radiologist is dismissed, and the scientist with a spinal fracture algorithm is literally pelted with paper balls. This vignette crystallizes every cultural pathology that kills innovation: - **Fear of change** ("Are you nuts? We are the experts") - **Territorial protectiveness** ("We're too busy... Our reputation rests on doing things our way") - **Status hierarchy** (senior staff silences junior input) - **Dismissal of external knowledge** (the scientist is told to "stay in the lab") - **Reactive, not proactive posture** (fixing a revenue problem after the fact, not preventing it) The authors identify five pillars of an innovation-positive culture:
The culture section can be distilled into five actionable imperatives: 1. Build "absorptive capacity"—the ability to find and exploit external knowledge 2. Be radically inclusive—solicit ideas from every level and stakeholder 3. Understand and manage the fear of change (competence-destroying innovations) 4. Reframe threats as opportunities (positive perceptual bias) 5. Tolerate risk and uncertainty (embrace lean start-up prototyping)
### 4.1 Absorptive Capacity: The Art of Importing External Knowledge
Wayne Gretzky's famous quote—"skate to where the puck is going, not where it has been"—captures the essence of absorptive capacity. Success in any field is achieved not by predicting the future with certainty, but by recognizing where opportunity exists and positioning yourself to move toward it. Absorptive capacity is the "ability and conviction of an organization to seek and exploit outside knowledge in expanding the scope of its innovation activity." Four characteristics maximize organizational absorptive capacity: 1. **Absorptive capacity of individuals**—every radiologist's training is a "catalytic environment" with steep learning curves and novel problem-solving; this primes lifelong learning 2. **Robust communication systems**—formalized mechanisms for knowledge transfer across teams 3. **Diversity of knowledge and experience**—breadth of exposure creates more "hooks" for assimilating new information 4. **Active engagement in research and development**—even in nonacademic practices, formalizing the study of clinical procedures and business processes enhances assimilation capacity The NIH ("Not Invented Here") syndrome is the cultural villain here: a bias favoring internal ideas over external ones, even when external solutions are demonstrably superior. This syndrome flourishes in organizations with strong identity and is particularly destructive because it systematically blocks the external knowledge flow that is essential for innovation.

ABSORPTIVE CAPACITY MODEL

Organizational Absorptive Capacity = Individual Absorptive Capacity (aggregated) + Assimilation Systems + Dissemination Systems + Exploitation Systems

Individual Absorptive Capacity = Prior Knowledge Base × Learning Activities × Transfer-ability of Skills

Learning Types:

  • Directed Learning = learning focused on a strategic initiative (e.g., reading about CT colonography, attending a conference)
  • Undirected Learning = learning to broaden knowledge from nontraditional sources (e.g., studying hospitality industry customer service)

NIH Syndrome = Evaluation Bias favoring internal ideas over external ideas (often strengthened by strong organizational identity)

Example: Medical Knowledge Transfer

Scenario: A practice wants to build a new CT colonography service.

Directed Learning:

  • Read medical literature on CT colonography accuracy and reimbursement
  • Attend a radiology conference session on CT colonography techniques
  • Perform a site visit to observe an established CT colonography service

Undirected Learning:

  • Study how outpatient surgery centers successfully introduced new technologies
  • Study how hospitality industry enhances customer service (to inform patient preparation)
  • Analyze how other specialists introduce screening services to healthy populations

Interpretation: The combination of domain-specific and cross-industry learning creates a richer, more robust knowledge base from which to design and implement the new service. You don't just replicate what other radiology groups did; you borrow ideas from other industries that solve analogous problems.

Absorptive capacity is like having a large set of keys on your keychain. Each key represents prior knowledge or experience. When you encounter a new lock (a new problem or opportunity), the more keys you have, the more likely one of them fits or can be adapted to fit. Training, diverse experiences, and cross-industry learning are how you add more keys to your organizational keychain. Without enough keys, you stand in front of the locked door unable to enter, and you may end up blaming the door (the NIH syndrome).
A critical nuance: Organizational absorptive capacity is NOT merely the sum of its individuals' absorptive capacities. It requires systems that promote assimilation, dissemination, and exploitation of outside knowledge. Solitary genius is not the answer; organized learning is. Additionally, research efforts in academic departments only contribute to absorptive capacity when they are directed toward tangible practice value AND communicated effectively. Non-academic practices that formalize quality improvement studies can outperform academic departments that don't connect research to operations.
### 4.2 Inclusiveness, Openness, and Partnership: The Multi-Stakeholder Ecosystem
Radiology departments are not monolithic entities composed solely of radiologists. They are ecosystems that include physician extenders, nurses, technologists, receptionists, schedulers, billing and coding personnel, IT staff, and support staff. Each of these constituents has a unique perspective and can contribute creatively to advancing the practice mission. The authors emphasize that inclusiveness serves two distinct purposes: 1. **Maximize ideation supply**—more diverse perspectives = more and better creative ideas 2. **Build trust**—people who feel valued and connected to the innovation process are more likely to support its implementation The egalitarian imperative is practical, not idealistic: "Encouraging input to maximize creative potential requires a suspension of hierarchies and a tolerance for dissenting opinions." Meetings where senior physicians cluster around a center table while junior members and staff sit at the periphery are NOT designed for innovation—they are designed for compliance. The Duke Radiology Revenue Integrity Committee example is instructive. It included radiologists, chief technologists, nurses, business managers, and health system administrators from multiple departments (clinic management, revenue management, compliance, IT). This multidisciplinary structure allowed them to identify billing denials arising from clinic practices and align stakeholders around improved patient service—achieving a >50% reduction in billing denials. The model was so successful, it was emulated by clinical departments throughout the health system. External partnerships matter too. If radiology practices are embedded in larger health care delivery systems, they must actively cultivate system-wide partnerships, seeking to understand how their innovation efforts are affected by other stakeholders. The radioactive seed localization example is perfect: despite evidence showing patient-preference and superior outcomes over wire localization, physician preference for the old method hindered adoption. Only by proactively involving breast surgeons as partners could a practice hope to implement this obviously superior technology.

INCLUSION QUICK-DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS

Self-Assessment Instrument:

  • Do we meet regularly with technologists and nurses—both at manager AND rank-and-file level?
  • Do we seek input from support staff in IT, finance, housekeeping, scheduling?
  • Do we engage patients and families to understand how they experience the department?
  • Do we partner with other clinical units to facilitate THEIR workflows?
  • Are junior physicians and non-physician staff present and heard at meetings?

Partnership Building = Build trust by supporting broader goals + Participate in non-radiology improvement efforts + Illuminate cultural nuances

Example: Billing Denial Reduction at Duke Radiology

Problem: CT imaging requests lacking appropriate supporting diagnosis, or specifying inappropriate body regions or contrast material usage, resulted in billing denials and time-consuming rework.

Approach: Formation of a Radiology Revenue Integrity Committee with multidisciplinary composition: radiologists, chief technologists, nurses, professional practice business managers, health system administration, clinic management, revenue management, compliance, and IT.

Actions: 1. Crossed organizational barriers to uncover clinic practices causing denials 2. Engaged referring physicians and their clinic managers in a collaborative process 3. Collectively aligned around improved patient service as the shared goal

Results: >50% reduction in billing denials, plus a new model for collaboration replicated across the health system.

Interpretation: The multi-stakeholder committee format was itself the innovation. It created new communication pathways and aligned incentives across an entire ecosystem. The revenue improvement was the value created by this inclusive structure.

Imagine trying to fix a leaky roof by only consulting the architect who designed it, never talking to the plumber who notices condensation on the pipes, the electrician who sees water damage near the wiring, or the homeowner who sees the stain on the ceiling. Each sees a different piece of the problem. Innovation culture ensures that everyone with a bead on any part of the problem—regardless of title or seniority—gets to speak before you decide on the repair. Because the best roof solutions often come from the person who's been staring at the leak from below.
The case for inclusiveness is not about "being nice"; it is about "getting better ideas." The reception staff sees problems in the waiting room that the Chief of Radiology rarely visits first-hand. Technologists experience the workflow friction of every protocol change. Referring physicians and their clinic managers are a rich source of insights about what makes imaging services valuable—or not. Innovation cultures treat information as widely distributed, not hierarchically concentrated.
### 4.3 Attitudes Toward Change: Navigating Competence-Enhancing vs. Competence-Destroying Innovation
Physicians take great pride in their professional relevance. Fear that innovation will lead to personal disruption or obsolescence is a deeply human response—and it lies at the heart of much resistance to change. The article gives vivid examples: - Elderly radiologists who "refined their diction to ensure effective transcription of their prose" resist transitioning to structured reporting - Fluoroscopy experts facing the transition to digital imaging - Traditional angiography experts holding off the transition to CT angiography Leaders must bring "emotional intelligence to the complexities of human adaptation." They must understand whether a proposed innovation is: - **Competence-enhancing**—builds on existing knowledge and experience; augments the value of experienced workers - **Competence-destroying**—renders current knowledge and skills obsolete; threatens the value of expertise The authors also warn of the "competency trap"—a self-reinforcing cycle where over-reliance on established competencies (which were previously valuable but are now increasingly obsolete) diminishes efforts to develop new competencies. In radiology, this manifests as "professional inertia": relentlessly insisting on familiar, efficiently-delivered imaging services at the expense of exploring novel methods. The "Lovin Radiology" vignette is a cautionary tale. A group that prided itself on personalized relationship building with referring physicians rejects a merger opportunity that would consolidate market position. The senior members were "trapped" by their commitment to a service approach they believed was exclusive. Junior partners saw the writing on the wall, left, joined the competitor, and took over the hospital contract. The lesson: a competency that was once a strength can become a trap when it prevents adaptation to new market realities. The question leadership must ask: "Are we protecting a unique and durable competitive advantage, or are we rationalizing inertia?"

COMPETENCE-INNOVATION MATRIX

Competence-Enhancing Innovation:

  • Builds on existing knowledge and skills
  • Increases the value of experienced workers
  • Lower resistance expected, smoother adoption
  • Example: Building a 3T MRI service when you already have 1.5T experience

Competence-Destroying Innovation:

  • Renders prior knowledge/skills obsolete
  • Threatens professional identity and economic security
  • High resistance expected, requires transition planning
  • Example: Replacing myelography with spinal MRI; replacing intra-arterial diagnostic angiography with CT angiography

Competency Trap = Over-reliance on established competencies (previously valuable, now obsolete) → diminished development of new competencies → organizational decline

Mitigation Options for Competence-Destroying Innovation:

  • Support retraining of affected workforce
  • Reassign employees to roles where existing competencies remain valuable
  • Provide pathways for transition to other productive organizational roles

Example: The Pain Management Service

Context: A radiology department plans to build a pain management service for patients with skeletal neoplasia using high-frequency focused ultrasound ablation. The innovation makes traditional opioid-based pain management approaches less central.

Risk Analysis:

Oncologists and radiation oncologists may see this as a competence-destroying innovation—their traditional pain management competencies become secondary to an interventional radiology approach. Resistance is predictable if they are not engaged as partners.

Mitigation: 1. Include oncologists and radiation oncologists in the planning team 2. Frame the innovation as a collaborative enhancement, not a replacement 3. Acknowledge and value their existing expertise 4. Create new protocols that leverage their knowledge in the new model 5. Plan for shared ownership of outcomes

Interpretation: The radiology department might be fully aligned internally around the new service, but external stakeholders (referring physicians, other specialties) are essential to successful implementation. Anticipating their resistance is not optional—it is a gating criterion in the innovation funnel.

A skilled watchmaker who has spent 40 years crafting mechanical timepieces faces the arrival of quartz technology. The competence-destroying innovation is quartz—it renders his entire deep expertise technically unnecessary. The wise watchmaker doesn't just fight the change; he becomes the expert in high-end hybrid movements, or pivots to restoring antique mechanical watches for a premium market, or trains the next generation of calibration specialists. The unwise watchmaker persists in steam-powered watchmaking and goes bankrupt. Organizations are not so different: acknowledge the threat, and seek to control your own transformation rather than being destroyed by it.
A few critical nuances: 1. "Competence-destroying" is a perceptual category. The same innovation may enhance some people's competencies while destroying others'. The radiologist who resists structured reporting is not irrational; they are protecting meaningful professional capital. 2. The competency trap is insidious because it rewards current excellence at the expense of future adaptability. The best organizations are those that can intentionally "destroy" their own competencies before a competitor does it to them. 3. When weighing proposed innovations, the "threat of disruption risk destabilizing the organization" must be an explicit consideration in deciding direction.
### 4.4 Perceptual Bias and Risk Tolerance
Organizations develop perceptual habits—ways of interpreting threats and opportunities. The authors argue that successful innovation cultures actively cultivate a "positive perceptual bias": weaknesses or threats are embraced as opportunities to build strengths, rather than obstacles to success. This optimism is not naive; it is a strategic orientation that facilitates identifying and internalizing marketplace trends. Example: A practice facing a contract negotiation that specifies new service and quality metrics could see this as a burden. A positively-biased practice recasts it as an opportunity to build an internal quality measurement and improvement infrastructure—something that helps the pending negotiation but also becomes a competitive advantage for future opportunities. Closely connected is tolerance for uncertainty and risk. Innovation is inherently an iterative process, and "no one can expect to derive the optimal solution the first time, every time." The authors reference the lean start-up philosophy: 1. Create a minimum viable solution 2. Measure its effectiveness 3. Use resulting performance data to inform the next set of ideas Small pilot initiatives with careful collection of pre- and post-intervention metrics are the recommended approach. This allows testing new ideas with minimal resource expenditure and practice disruption. Rational risk-taking is structured and measured, not impulsive.

LEAN START-UP CYCLE FOR RADIOLOGY

Step 1: Create minimum viable solution (smallest testable version)

Step 2: Measure effectiveness (pre/post intervention metrics)

Step 3: Learn from performance data

Step 4: Iterate → return to Step 1 with improved understanding

Risk Management Principles:

  • Risk must be rational and informed
  • Risk must be tolerated by both the team AND sponsors
  • Pilots should involve minimal resource expenditure and practice disruption

Example: Reframing a Contract Threat

Threat: Our health system contract renewal now requires us to report on 12 new quality and patient experience metrics—a significant administrative burden.

Negative Framing: "This is an unpalatable requirement imposed by the health system. It will stretch our already thin administrative staff and distract us from clinical work."

Positive Framing: "This is an opportunity to build an internal quality measurement infrastructure. It will help us negotiate better in the current contract cycle and provide a competitive advantage when we pursue new opportunities, because we will already have the data systems, expertise, and workflow that demonstrate quality. Very few other practices in our market will have this capability."

Outcome: The practice builds the quality infrastructure, demonstrates early wins to the health system, and positions itself as a more valuable partner for future strategic initiatives.

This is like a marathon runner who faces a sudden headwind. A negative framing says, "This wind will slow me down; it's not fair." A positive framing says, "This headwind will make the race harder, but it will also make my competitors work harder; if I train specifically for wind resistance, I can gain a relative advantage." The runner purchases wind-resistant gear and practices pacing into the wind. When the race-day conditions arrive, the same adversity that breaks unprepared runners becomes his competitive advantage.
The organizational optimism described here is not about positivity-for-positivity's sake. It is a strategic orientation: when threats are viewed through the lens of opportunity, the organization is more likely to invest effort in identifying, internalizing, and responding to marketplace trends early, when those trends can still be shaped. Reactivity is expensive. Proactivity is a competitive advantage.

5. Structure: Organizing for Innovation

The section opens with a "choose your own adventure" vignette comparing two radiology practices in "Mostlyhealthton, U.S.A.": - **Inertia Radiology**: 30-person group, all radiologists compensated purely on clinical productivity, quarterly meetings at the steakhouse rarely attended by more than 40%, majority approval for small policy changes and two-thirds supermajority for any major capital expenditure. The group is aligned with "Creaky Hospital." - **Evolution Radiology**: 30-person group run by an elected executive committee (5 partners + CFO + business manager). Executive committee has final approval for budgets and capital expenditures. One partner chairs a "New Initiatives Committee" that oversees the development and assessment of new initiatives. The practice designates 1% of gross revenues as an annual R&D budget managed by the committee. Supports both project-specific and strategic professional development. The group is aligned with "Shiny Healthcare System." When Creaky Hospital is sold to a national healthcare system—which then closes inpatient units, builds outpatient facilities, and announces an open RFP for imaging services—it's immediately obvious which practice is better prepared to develop and exploit the competencies needed to win the contract. Structural enablers of innovation include: dedicated resources, flexible team structures, strong centralized governance, robust communication systems, and incentive alignment. Structural preparation is the "capital investment" that allows cultural willingness to become operational capacity.
Culture is the desire to cook; structure is the kitchen setup that determines whether you can actually produce a great meal. You can have a brilliant chef (culture) with a terrible kitchen—no prep space, dull knives, broken oven—and the results will be mediocre. You can also have a beautiful kitchen with a disengaged chef, and the results will also be mediocre. The best restaurants have both: passionate chefs AND a kitchen where the tools and layout facilitate their craft.
### 5.1 Creating and Dedicating Resources
"Practices should consider investing in innovation activity the same way companies invest in research and development to maintain their competitive advantage." This means: - A line-item expense in the annual budget for innovation management - Dedicated support personnel, space, funding, and TIME - A formal "prospector strategy"—an entrepreneurial orientation directed toward growth, particularly through expansion into new markets or stimulation of new opportunities - Regular strategic and scenario planning to identify new opportunities - Candid, rigorous evaluation of internal performance, marketplace advances, and practices adopted from competitors and other industries Beyond ensuring funding, dedicated budgeting for innovation communicates something crucial to the organization: "innovation is a strategic priority." When the CFO asks why money is being spent on a project with no immediate revenue, the existence of a formal budget line makes justification routine rather than exceptional. For individual professional development, the practice should support both project-specific and general strategic topics. This could mean funding attendance at conferences, coursework, or even formal training in market research, forecasting, and team coordination.
