Summary: Theory, Models and Implementation in Financial Management

Authors: J. Morris McInnes (MIT) and Willard J. Carleton (University of North Carolina)
Source: Management Science, Vol. 28, No. 9, pp. 957-978, September 1982

Core Argument

This article investigates why there is a wide gap between normative corporate finance theory and the practice of financial modeling in firms. The authors combine a literature review of normative theory with empirical evidence from field research at 18 manufacturing companies. The main finding: although computer-based models are gaining acceptance, the logic programmed into these models is far from the prescriptions of normative finance theory.

Why This Article Was Written

Every issue of Interfaces contains concern about the limited conversion of theory into practice. This article is of the same genre: a survey of the gap between theory and practice in corporate financial models.

The focus is on strategic financial decisions, not operational ones. That is, decisions that set the broad direction of a firm's financing, rate and mix of investment, and production levels within the context of the trade-off between profitability and risk.

A Financial Management Framework

Much of the finance literature has historically treated the managerial process as a disjointed set of activities. On one side there is the problem of financing the firm; on the other, methodology for appraising investment proposals. The two are rarely connected.

Relevant financial theory originates from classical economic theory of the firm. Vickers (1968) proposed that achieving an optimal equilibrium requires a simultaneous solution of production, investment, and financing plans. In this paradigm, financial markets are seen as the sole and sufficient evaluator of firm decisions.

Three Core Components

The emerging managerial finance theory needs a framework to model three components:

  1. Demand for funds -- ongoing estimates of investment opportunities and expected benefits, including justification for funds already committed.
  2. Supply of funds -- ongoing estimates of fund availability from various sources and their costs, including release of funds from existing investments.
  3. Choice criterion -- generally maximizing shareholder utility from the expected stream of dividends.

Additionally, two different directions of literature development have emerged:

  • Positive economics approach -- seeks to simplify assumptions so that investment and financing decisions can be separated, investments are additive, a one-period horizon suffices, and uncertainty can be described in mean-variance terms. This produces powerful models but oversimplifies the problem.
  • Normative approach -- more directly supportive of corporate modeling and attempts to address real complexities such as taxation, accounting reporting, working capital, bankruptcy risk, and dividend policies. This is the approach the article focuses on.

Common Characteristics of Normative Models

All models in the normative approach share common traits:

  1. Based on cash-flow representation of the firm with discounting calculations to determine value at a specific point in the planning horizon.
  2. Myopic -- all information is assumed available at the decision point, without allowing state descriptions to change over time.
  3. Assume homogeneous preferences and complete markets, thus not recognizing differences between owner preferences and management predilections.
  4. Sufficiently large or complex to require computer-based implementation.

The authors argue for a much broader theory based on dynamic systems conceptualization. Because a central feature of financial management practice is the continual formation and revision of expectations, financial managers must concern themselves with the physical phenomena causing cash flows and economic value creation, both externally and within the firm.

Modeling Support for Corporate Planning

During the 1970s there was a sharp increase in adoption of computer-based models by business organizations. Grinyer and Wooller linked this growth to increasing formalization of corporate planning due to adverse changes in the business environment.

However, early literature tended to conclude that modeling was failing. Hall (1973) found that top managers did not find strategic planning models useful. He identified five reasons for failure:

  1. Erroneous perception by modelers that corporate planning is a centralized, normative, and essentially static process.
  2. Inadequate attention to interactions between decision makers and models.
  3. Tendency to create assertive rather than supportive models.
  4. Oversimplified view of causal relationships.
  5. Isolation of modelers from managers directly involved in planning.

Lorange and Rockart (1977) added that hardware and software limitations were also barriers, though these constraints were being rapidly overcome. They emphasized the need to differentiate models based on the administrative ordering of the decision-making process.

Classification of Planning Models

Various attempts have been made to classify planning models. Common distinctions include:

DimensionDistinction
Descriptive vs. OptimizingMost models in practice are descriptive simulations
Deterministic vs. ProbabilisticPractice strongly favors deterministic models
Simple vs. ComplexLacks clear foundation for distinction
Bottom-up vs. Top-downBottom-up consolidates from below; top-down decomposes from above

For strategic planning, Kingston (1973) proposed models should be top-down and focus on high aggregation levels. Swanson et al. (1973) added that strategic models should focus on the few salient issues central to top management's perception.

Carleton et al. (1973) pointed out that the usual financial model (budget compiler) is ill-suited for articulating a robust corporate financial strategy. They proposed a top-down modeling structure capturing cash flows, main accounting flows and balance-sheet ratios, focused on maximizing the expected value of cash flows accruing to ownership capital.

Evidence from Field Research

Methodology

The research covered 18 manufacturing companies with revenues ranging from $5 million to over $10 billion. Data was collected through interviews with managers in various positions: line, planning, modeling, and data processing. Twenty models or modeling systems were observed.

General Findings

The principal focus of observed models was to provide enterprise simulation. Key findings:

  • About half the models worked on annual planning horizons, the rest up to ten years.
  • Very few models used decision-science methodologies. Only one contained probabilistic formulations, three contained optimizing routines.
  • All models used accounting logic; two-thirds included cash flows; one-third included discounting routines.
  • Overall, models appeared relatively unsophisticated from a decision-science perspective and somewhat removed from normative finance theory prescriptions.

However, models helped address planning complexity, freeing managerial resources to focus on external and internal uncertainty.

Base Case Construction

The core of financial modeling is constructing a "base case" -- a "most likely" projection of the firm's financial future assuming continuity of existing businesses without significant disruption. Two approaches were observed:

  1. Interactive and dispersed -- various inputs debated across the organization to build consensus, with the model providing computational support.
  2. Centralized -- the base case generated from regression equations applied to "base year" performance.

Problems that emerged: future cost predictions using learning curves are fairly accurate, but the level of investment required to achieve projected cost declines is unclear. Additionally, line managers often assume efficiency gains will directly improve margins, ignoring competitive effects on prices.

Strategic Investment Programs

Strategic investments must be analyzed individually and in their interactions with the base case and other programs. Several companies developed specialized models for this, with forms varying according to each firm's competitive expertise.

An important bias exists in comparing the base case with new strategic investments:

  • The base case is viewed with greater confidence because it is already running, so it is perceived as less risky.
  • Measuring the base case with accounting rates of return creates an inflated perception compared to economic returns.

Both biases can lead to overinvestment in existing operations and underinvestment in new strategies.

Management of Uncertainty

Models were primarily developed to address complexity, not uncertainty. Sensitivity analysis was the main method used to handle uncertainty in input assumptions. Only one company attempted probabilistic formulations, which were considered impractical due to variable interdependencies and massive data processing requirements.

Interestingly, managers tend to distill continuous uncertainty into discrete, vivid scenarios. This helps achieve collective commitment to action. Contingency planning was done in two ways:

  1. Defining several scenarios and choosing one as the base case, with the rest becoming contingency plans.
  2. Creating three projections: base case, pessimistic case, and optimistic case.

Decentralization of Investment Decisions

There is tension between top management's need to control major investment decisions and their limited information. Mueller (1972) conceptualizes the firm as a venture capital market, where effectiveness diminishes as size increases because entrepreneurial information flow to the top gets blocked.

In one company, a modeling system capable of supporting centralized analysis was instead used in a delegated mode. Top management chose this approach to maintain mutual trust, acknowledge competence of lower-level managers, and build psychological commitment of the line organization to strategic investments.

A key insight: top management does not see information as a given, fixed resource, but as a variable quantity shaped by the roles, attitudes, and managerial style of information sharing within the organization.

Integration of Investment and Financing Decisions

This is where the gap between theory and practice is widest. The most fundamental proposition of normative theory is that investment and financing should be treated jointly and simultaneously through constrained optimization based on shareholder utility. However, only three of the twenty models had optimizing routines, and all three were used only for project selection from evaluated sets, not for overall investment and financing decisions.

The reasons are varied:

  • Reluctance to address integration directly -- most models separate financing decisions from investment and operating decisions.
  • Lack of relevant information -- historical data is typically averages in accounting, not economic, terms. It is difficult to extract meaningful input for strategic optimization.
  • Some companies feel constraints are not on capital but on other resources like skilled people, making formal optimization seem unnecessary.
  • Management resistance -- reluctance to address goals and goal-weightings explicitly.

There is also a political dimension: line managers tend to view optimizing models as assertive rather than supportive, which could shift decision-making influence closer to those who design and use the models.

Conclusions

Several key conclusions from this article:

  1. Computer-based modeling is gaining acceptance and its growth will continue.
  2. A real and substantial gap exists between normative finance theory prescriptions and the logic of models used in practice.
  3. Modeling must be viewed together with the managerial organization -- the formal logic in models is only partial evidence of the overall logic underlying financial strategy formulation.

Areas requiring further attention:

  1. Information extraction from monitoring systems and planning data in a form amenable to analysis conforming to normative finance theory prescriptions. This is not simply a data aggregation issue, but involves several different logical structures impacting planning, monitoring, and reporting activities.
  2. Treatment of risk and uncertainty -- the theoretical formulation does not come close to fully describing how strategic uncertainty is experienced and distributed within management.
  3. Optimization -- the political dimensions of management need further research to be integrated into optimizing prescriptions.

Normative finance theory provides a rational design framework, but is predominantly static and disconnected from organizational context. Organization theory is beginning to provide process insights. Advances in data processing offer a powerful enabling technology that could bridge the structural perspective of finance theory with the process perspective of organization theory.

Closing this gap is an exciting challenge for management scientists: intellectually demanding, integrative of relevant theory, and pragmatic in its orientation toward solving real problems in society.
