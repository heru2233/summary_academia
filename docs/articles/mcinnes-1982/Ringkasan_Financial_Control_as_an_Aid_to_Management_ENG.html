1. Research Question and Purpose

The paper, published in Management Science in 1982, addresses a persistent concern in management science: the limited conversion of academic finance theory into corporate financial modeling practice. The authors, J. Morris McInnes and Willard J. Carleton, examine the correspondence—or lack of correspondence—between the precepts of normative finance theory and the actual design and use of computer-based financial models in large business organizations.

Normative finance theory may be defined as the body of theory that prescribes how financial decisions should be made to achieve an optimal outcome, typically defined as maximization of shareholder utility. This is contrasted with positive theory, which describes how financial markets and decision makers actually behave. The central research question is not simply whether models are used, but whether their information and logic structures reflect the prescriptions of normative finance.

Three more specific questions follow. First, what are the central propositions of a normative theory of corporate financial management? Second, what are the characteristics of financial modeling practice, as observed in field research? Third, why does a gap between theory and practice exist? The paper identifies implementation difficulties in three interdependent areas: the construction of relevant information, the treatment of strategic uncertainty, and the modeling of multiple and conflicting goals. It also recognizes political dimensions that make explicit objective functions difficult to address.

The purpose is therefore diagnostic and corrective: to identify the differences between theory and practice, to explain their causes, and to suggest directions for future theoretical and empirical research.

Example

The authors note that prior literature frequently expressed disappointment that strategic planning models were not enhancing managerial effectiveness. Hall's study of 17 companies found that top managers did not find strategic planning models useful. The diagnosis included flawed assumptions about planning as a centralized, static, normative process and inadequate attention to interactions between managers and models. This example sets the stage for the paper's own attempt to understand implementation failure.

Analogy

The relationship between normative finance theory and financial modeling practice can be compared to a navigational chart versus the actual experience of sailing. The chart provides a logically consistent representation of where a ship should go, based on principles of navigation. The captain, however, must deal with weather, crew disagreements, and the slow accumulation of local knowledge. The chart remains useful, but it is incomplete and may be ignored in practice if it cannot accommodate the realities of the voyage.

Key Insight

The gap between theory and practice is not a trivial implementation detail. It stems from deep differences between the abstraction of normative models and the information, uncertainty, and political realities of organizational decision making. The paper argues that normative finance theory provides a powerful logic, but one that is incomplete without behavioral and political dimensions.

Key Takeaways

  • The research question is why corporate financial modeling practice diverges from normative finance theory.
  • Three implementation areas explain the gap: information construction, strategic uncertainty, and multiple conflicting goals.
  • The purpose is both theoretical and practical: improve the design and use of financial models in organizations.

2. Focus and Working Definitions

The paper focuses on strategic financial decisions rather than operational decisions. Following Anthony's typology of managerial decision processes, strategic decisions set the broad direction of a firm's financing, rate and mix of investment, and production levels. They involve a trade-off between profitability and risk.

The authors define key terms carefully to avoid ambiguity. The firm, company, and enterprise are used interchangeably to mean a set of business activities relating to a specific corporate entity, distinguished by legal framework from the parties and institutions in the environment with which it transacts. The environment is defined by exclusion: everything not included in the firm is part of its environment.

The organization is a narrower concept: a subset of the firm's employees acting as an information-processing and decision-making system. Importantly, the organization is not synonymous with the firm. The organization is described as a property of the firm-environment system because its information-processing and influence systems must include the relevant parts of the environment.

The boundary between firm and environment is not always clear. This apparently abstract point has practical consequences. For example, whether a firm's pension plan assets and liabilities should be considered part of the firm's financial system is a real boundary-definition problem with major policy implications.

Example

The pension plan question illustrates the boundary problem. If pension assets and liabilities are considered part of the firm, financial models must include them; if they are considered external, models could materially misstate the firm's financial condition. The choice is not merely technical but reflects a policy decision about the scope of the enterprise.

Analogy

Defining the boundary of the firm is like drawing a national border on a map. Rivers and mountains may seem natural boundaries, but political decisions determine where the line falls. Once drawn, the border affects trade, taxation, and defense. Similarly, the boundary of the firm affects which cash flows, risks, and obligations are inside the financial planning model.

Key Insight

The organization is not the firm. The managerial organization is an information-processing and decision-making subsystem that connects the firm to its environment. This distinction matters because financial models are built by organizations, not by firms abstractly, and therefore must reflect organizational realities.

Key Takeaways

  • The paper is concerned with strategic, not operational, financial decisions.
  • Firm, environment, and organization are defined distinctly.
  • The boundary between firm and environment is a practical modeling problem, not just a theoretical one.

3. A Financial Management Framework

The financial management literature historically treated the managerial process as a set of disjointed activities. One branch focused on financing the firm, assuming that investments were somehow given and the only problem was choosing a financing plan at minimum expected cost. Another branch focused on investment appraisal methods, largely computational in character and disconnected from the organizational process of generating and appraising opportunities. The paper argues that this functional separation is incomplete, because strategic planning is necessarily integrative.

The classical economic theory of the firm treated capital as simply one factor input among many. Vickers argued that capital deserves special status and that optimal equilibrium requires simultaneous solution of production, investment, and financing plans. The availability of finance must be treated as a constraint on the firm.

From this perspective, normative financial theory initially proposed that financial markets are the only and sufficient evaluator of the firm's decisions. But this assumption is questionable: some empirical work suggests that firms do not always know of a set of investment opportunities requiring more capital than they can finance. In other words, the demand side of the financial management problem deserves more attention.

The emerging managerial theory of finance requires a framework with three principal components:

  • Demand for funds: the organization must provide ongoing estimates of investment opportunities and expected benefits, including justification for continuing existing investments.
  • Supply of funds: the organization must provide ongoing estimates of funds available from each source and their expected cost functions, including funds released from existing operations.
  • Choice criterion: a criterion to bring demand and supply into a consistent, optimal relationship, generally derived from a model of shareholder utility.

The framework must also recognize interdependencies. Demand-side interdependencies include technological interactions among projects and interactions of projected financial flows when projects are combined into a portfolio. Supply-side interdependencies exist because the availability and cost of funds from any source depend on the mix and utilization of other sources. Finally, demand and supply are reciprocally interdependent because the supply and cost of funds may depend on investment opportunities, and the attractiveness of investment opportunities may depend on the availability and cost of funds.

The choice criterion aims to provide a unique, optimal solution that can be continuously derived in real time. This criterion is generally justified as maximization of utility to shareholders of an expected stream of dividends through time. However, the interpretation of shareholder needs cannot be viewed as wholly exogenous to the managed system.

Net Present Value Criterion in Normative Finance
\[ NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

This equation is a standard representation of the discounting calculation that normative finance theory uses to measure economic value at a defined point in the planning horizon. It should be read as a stylized expression of the framework summarized by the authors, in which the firm is represented by its cash flows and value is derived by discounting expected cash flows.

Symbols:

  • NPV = net present value at the beginning of the planning horizon (or, equivalently, at some defined decision point).
  • CFt = expected net cash flow at time period t, usually including both investment outflows and operating inflows.
  • r = discount rate representing the opportunity cost of capital, or the required rate of return.
  • T = planning horizon, the number of periods over which cash flows are projected.
  • t = time index, from 0 to T.

Mathematical relationship: The equation sums the present values of all expected cash flows. Future cash flows are divided by \((1+r)^t\) to reflect the time value of money. A larger discount rate reduces the present value of future cash flows; a larger cash flow in any period increases the present value.

Interpretation: An investment with a positive net present value is expected to increase the economic value of the firm. In normative finance theory, the firm should undertake positive-NPV projects subject to financing and other constraints. In practice, the formula is used in capital appraisal to compare investment opportunities on a common economic basis.

Important caveat: The NPV equation by itself does not address interdependencies among projects, financing constraints, uncertainty, or multiple goals. It is a building block, not a complete decision rule. The authors emphasize that strategic planning requires constrained optimization over a system of interrelated variables, not simply the ranking of independent projects by their individual NPVs.

Example

In the source's review, Carleton proposes a procedure for partial decoupling of the project-selection model from financing using an information construct called the overall investment opportunities function for the firm. This recognizes that individual projects cannot be evaluated only in isolation: their value may depend on the aggregate rate and mix of investment. The NPV calculation must be embedded in a broader framework that includes the opportunity set and financing conditions.

Analogy

The three component framework can be compared to a market: demand for funds is the buyer's willingness to pay for investment projects, supply of funds is the seller's willingness to provide capital at various costs, and the choice criterion is the price and allocation mechanism that clears the market. A market works efficiently only if all three parts operate together; ignoring one part distorts the whole system.

Key Insight

Investment and financing decisions are not naturally separable. The classical financial literature often assumed separability for analytical convenience, but strategic planning requires integration. The paper argues that the financial management problem must be modeled as a system with reciprocal interdependencies between demand and supply of funds.

Key Takeaways

  • A useful financial management framework has three components: demand for funds, supply of funds, and a choice criterion.
  • Interdependencies arise within demand, within supply, and between demand and supply.
  • NPV is a fundamental economic measure, but it must be used within a constrained optimization logic to capture the complexity of strategic financial decisions.

4. Normative Finance Theory: Main Propositions

The paper identifies two broad directions in the finance literature. The first, in the spirit of positive economics, seeks minimal assumptions about how financial markets function. These assumptions allow investment and financing decisions to be logically separable, investment decisions to be additive, a one-period planning horizon to be sufficient, and uncertainty to be described in mean-variance terms with agreed parameter values. This literature has produced powerful insights, but the extraction of particular issues from the totality of the strategic problem is somewhat arbitrary.

The second direction is more normative and directly supportive of corporate modeling. It addresses real-world complexities such as corporate taxation, accounting-based reporting requirements, working capital and bankruptcy risk, dividend policies, and—more recently—the organizational context of financial decision making through agency theory. Agency theory is the analysis of goal conflicts between principals and agents, especially between shareholders and managers, and the role of contracts and markets in resolving those conflicts.

The normative literature can be grouped into three categories:

  • Extensions of Weingartner's capital budgeting framework to include richer management policy constraints and multiple objectives.
  • Decomposition algorithms to integrate decentralized or loosely coupled financial planning models, including models that explicitly confront goal conflict between corporate and operating management.
  • Corporate planning models that treat the firm's major financial decisions as jointly determined within an optimization framework.

All three themes share common characteristics. First, they use a cash-flow representation of the firm and a discounting calculation to derive value—either net present value at the beginning or terminal value at the end of the planning horizon. Decisions are made by constrained optimization. Compromises are made to align model logic with users' cognitive perceptions and with existing computational methods. Second, the models are myopic: state descriptions and probabilities do not change with time, all information is assumed available at time zero, and the whole decision is treated globally and ab nova. This means flexibility can be treated only rudimentarily, and qualitative differences in risk perception over time are not allowed. Third, most models assume homogeneous preferences and probability beliefs and complete markets, so divergence between owner preferences and management predilections is excluded. Fourth, all models are sufficiently large and complex to require computer-based implementation.

The second and third characteristics are critical. A normative formulation must recognize the organizational constraints on decision making. Moreover, because decision-support computer methodologies are relatively new, especially for strategic decisions, implementation requires close identity between managers' cognitive structures and the logic structures embedded in models. Computational tractability and cognitive mapping may be in tension.

Example

The model reported by Hamilton and Moses is a notable exception to the usual descriptive simulation. It uses a mixed-integer programming formulation with an objective function that maximizes earnings-per-share. The model includes all main financial variables of the corporation—financing, operating, and investment variables—over a multi-year planning period. This example illustrates both the possibility and the rarity of optimization-based corporate models.

Analogy

Normative finance models are like architectural blueprints that assume a perfectly flat building site, standard materials, and no need to consult the people who will use the building. Such blueprints can be elegant and rigorous, but they may not fit the irregularities of the actual site or the preferences of the occupants. The paper argues that strategic financial models need to be more like adaptive building systems that incorporate changing conditions and human behavior.

Key Insight

The main deficiency of existing normative models is not their mathematics but their assumptions. They are myopic, ignore organizational constraints, and assume away conflicts between shareholders and managers. These assumptions are especially damaging for strategic planning, which is necessarily integrative and dynamic.

Key Takeaways

  • Positive finance theory provides separation and additivity assumptions but is incomplete for strategic planning.
  • Normative finance theory directly supports corporate modeling by addressing taxes, accounting constraints, risk, and dividends.
  • Common model weaknesses include myopia, homogeneous preferences, complete markets assumptions, and exclusion of organizational conflict.

5. Modeling Support of Corporate Planning

The paper reviews the literature on corporate modeling support and the growth of computer-based models during the 1970s. The rate of adoption increased sharply, partly due to adverse changes in the business environment and the resulting uncertainties for management. Formalized corporate planning became more common, and computer-based models came to be seen as necessary because of the number of relevant variables, their estimation difficulty, their interactivity, and the passage of time.

However, earlier literature from managerial researchers concluded that modeling was failing to enhance managerial effectiveness. Hall's study identified five organizational factors for failure:

  • Modelers mistakenly assumed corporate planning is a centralized, normative, and static decision-making process.
  • Inadequate attention was given to interactions between decision makers and models in the dynamics of planning.
  • Models tended to be assertive rather than supportive, based only on explicit goals and neglecting implicit organizational goals.
  • Models used oversimplified causal relationships.
  • Modelers were isolated from the managers actually involved in planning.

Lorange and Rockart identified technical limitations of hardware and software, but suggested these constraints were rapidly being overcome. They argued that models should be differentiated according to the administrative ordering of decision-making processes. The three principal cycles in this administrative ordering are objective-setting, multiyear planning, and responsibility budgeting.

Several typologies of planning models have been proposed. A common typology distinguishes between descriptive simulation, which represents what happens given assumptions, and optimizing simulation, which searches for a best solution. In practice, most models are descriptive simulations. Another distinction is between deterministic and probabilistic formulations; deterministic models dominate. A further dimension is simple versus complex, but this dimension lacks a clear foundation. It may refer to the number of equations, the degree of data aggregation, or the degree to which causal relationships are captured. Hayes and Nolan distinguish between bottom-up models, which consolidate plans from lower organizational levels, and top-down models, which decompose corporate objectives and plans into intermediate detail.

Kingston proposes that for strategic planning, a financial model should be top-down in logic and highly aggregated. Swanson and colleagues add that a strategic model should focus on the few salient issues central to top management's perception of investment strategy. Several case studies illustrate this: Univac and Xerox used models for new product planning; Esmark used models to support acquisition and divestment. However, none of these case studies directly addresses the integration of investment and financing strategies in the modeling logic or in the use of model output.

Carleton and colleagues critique the usual budget compiler model—a model that simply generates financial statements from operating budgets—as ill-suited for comprehensive corporate financial strategy. They propose a top-down modeling structure that captures cash flows, accounting flows, and balance-sheet ratios, and focuses on maximization of expected cash flows to ownership capital. They also emphasize viewing the model and managers as an information-assimilating, planning, and decision-making entity. Key inputs such as the cost of capital should be generated by managers rather than developed entirely within the model's logic, and descriptions of business opportunities should come from other organizational systems.

Example

Ishikawa's model for a major insurance company is an exception to the deterministic preference in practice. Managers at that company were uncomfortable with point-estimate output because they were accustomed to thinking in probability distributions. This example shows that the choice of model form should depend on the cognitive habits of the decision makers, not only on technical convenience.

Analogy

Descriptive simulation is like a flight simulator that shows where an aircraft will go given a set of control inputs. Optimizing simulation is like an autopilot that also adjusts controls to reach a destination efficiently. Most corporate models are the first type: they help explore consequences, but they do not automatically find optimal strategies. A good simulator must match the pilot's mental model of the aircraft, or the pilot will not trust it.

Key Insight

The apparent technical unsophistication of most financial models is not simply backwardness. Models are used to reduce the complexity of the internal enterprise so that managers can focus their attention on external uncertainty and goal direction. The choice of model form is often a conscious judgment about efficiency rather than a failure of knowledge.

Key Takeaways

  • Computer-based financial models gained wide acceptance in the 1970s, but early evidence showed limited success.
  • Most corporate models are deterministic, descriptive simulations, not optimization models.
  • The design of models should be connected to the administrative and cognitive realities of organizational planning.

6. Field Research: Methodology and Sample

The empirical component of the paper is a field research study investigating the design and implementation of computer-based financial planning models. The research design sits between broad survey research and a single case study. It included 18 companies in manufacturing industry.

Data were collected through interviews with a range of people in each company: managers in line positions, planning positions, modeling and systems support positions, and data processing positions. Additional data were obtained from documents such as 10-K reports, organization charts, planning and reporting manuals, and model documentation manuals. This triangulation of sources strengthens the reliability of the observations.

The companies were selected on an opportunistic basis, so they do not represent any particular population. Their annual revenues ranged from about $5 million to over $10 billion, but most were large international companies. Two companies had considered modeling but decided not to proceed; two had already failed and abandoned their modeling efforts. A total of 20 models or modeling systems were observed. The number of models exceeds the number of companies because in some organizations separate modeling efforts were sustained at corporate and divisional levels and were treated as distinct, loosely coupled systems.

A model was judged successful if it was used frequently, if resources were committed to maintaining its currency, and if its direct users—usually planning staff rather than line executives—expressed satisfaction with its functionality. A stronger test of success in terms of improved decisions and performance was not considered necessary or feasible in this exploratory research. The authors cite evidence that subjective criteria of success are likely to correlate with more objective criteria.

Six organizations had well-established, successful modeling systems widely and continually used in planning and decision making. General observations refer to the whole sample of 20 models; more definitive descriptions draw principally on these six companies.

Example

The presence of both failed and successful modeling efforts in the same sample is instructive. In two companies, modeling had failed and been abandoned; in six, models were well established and successful. This variation allowed the authors to compare conditions associated with success and failure, although the sample is not statistically representative.

Analogy

This field research approach is like a geologist taking core samples at multiple sites rather than doing a comprehensive survey of an entire terrain. The cores provide deep but local information. They are not a random sample, but they reveal the structure and variability of what lies below the surface.

Key Insight

Success was judged subjectively, by use, maintenance, and user satisfaction, not by objective financial outcomes. This is a deliberate limitation of exploratory research, but it means that the findings should be interpreted as evidence about organizational acceptance and integration, not about the ultimate profitability of model-supported decisions.

Key Takeaways

  • The field research covered 18 manufacturing companies and 20 financial models.
  • Data came from interviews and documents, including multiple perspectives within each firm.
  • The sample was opportunistic and not representative; success criteria were subjective and exploratory.

7. Information Structures and the Base Case

A major challenge in operationalizing a financial planning system is extracting an information structure from the ongoing life of the enterprise that can support normative finance techniques. Central to this challenge is the construction of the base case.

The base case is defined as a "most likely" projection of the firm's financial future. It assumes continuity and efficient performance of existing businesses, no significant external perturbations, and no further strategic initiatives. Its purpose is to provide a financial reference point throughout the planning period against which to appraise new investment proposals and possible disinvestment.

Two general approaches to constructing the base case were observed. In the first, the base case is established through a highly interactive and dispersed process. Many inputs—demand, output prices, and costs—are argued through the organization to build consensus. The modeling system provides calculational support, translating assumptions into financial projections and feeding these back into deliberations until agreement is reached.

In the second approach, the base case is constructed more centrally and relies directly on the modeling system. It is generated by applying regression equations to the financial performance and balance sheet of a "base year." This approach appears more objective but still requires judgment.

Judgment is needed in several places: adjusting regression equations to reflect expected relationships, excluding unusual circumstances that are not expected to persist, and predicting future cost and revenue functions in light of incremental changes in competitive position and production methods.

The learning curve example illustrates the difficulties. The learning curve algorithm can project unit product costs with relatively high confidence. But what is less clear is the level and mix of investment—in capital equipment, product design, and administrative support—necessary to achieve the projected decline in unit costs. Moreover, prices and margins are less predictable than production costs. Line managers may assume efficiency gains translate directly into improved margins without considering the effect of competition on prices.

The base case, in a strict formal sense, has no real information content unless it is complemented by a model of the competitive environment. If the internal model lacks a corresponding model of external competitive behavior, the base case is not a sufficient benchmark. The information content is higher in cases of long-term contracts, where price and demand uncertainty are at least partly absorbed by the contract.

Strategic investment programs raise further questions. There must be a definition of a strategic investment program that distinguishes it from base-case investment, so that investments are not double-counted. Strategic investments must be analyzed individually and then in terms of their interactions with the base case, with authorized programs under implementation, and with other proposals under consideration. Computationally, exhaustive analysis of all combinations becomes intractable as the number of programs increases. Human judgment must reduce the possibilities to a manageable set.

Several companies had developed specialized models for strategic investment analysis. Their form varied with the company's competitive strategy: new product development, finding natural resources, or acquisition and divestment. These specialized models were often more analytically sophisticated than general financial planning models and frequently used cash flows and discounting. The base case, by contrast, was expressed in accounting terms only.

There is often a different conceptualization of the enterprise in the base case and in strategic investment models. The base case conceptualizes the enterprise in terms of products and production-distribution systems. Strategy models flow from markets and market needs, through required capabilities, to investment. Thus, the information in the financial planning system is not strictly hierarchical, causing difficult problems of comparison and consolidation.

The reporting system exacerbates the problem. Strategic programs are eventually subsumed into operations well before the end of the planning horizon on which they were justified. The specific outcomes of these investments are lost within general results. Returns to new investment are lost in averages, making it difficult to learn about rates of return as a function of the rate and mix of investment.

Sequential attention to the base case and to new programs creates subtle biases. Because the base is already in place, its projected results tend to be viewed with greater confidence than projections of new strategic investments, so the base may be viewed as less risky. Additionally, the base case is measured by an accounting rate of return, which is likely to be inflated relative to its economic return if the growth rate of the base case is less than its internal rate of return. Comparing this inflated accounting return with expected internal rates of return on strategic projects biases the comparison in favor of the base case. Both biases can cause overinvestment in established operations and underinvestment in new strategies.

Example

The learning curve algorithm is used by several companies to project future costs. It predicts unit product costs with high confidence. But managers need to know how much investment is required to achieve that cost decline. If they assume efficiency gains automatically produce more profits, they may under-invest in the capital equipment and product design needed to realize the gains, or they may overestimate the profitability of the base case because they ignore competitors' price responses.

Analogy

The base case is like a "keep everything as it is" budget for a household: it assumes no major changes, no unexpected expenses, and steady income. It is a useful reference point for deciding whether to take a new job or buy a house, but it is not a prediction of the future. Comparing a new opportunity to an unrealistically smooth continuation can make the new opportunity look riskier or less profitable than it actually is.

Key Insight

Sequential separation and accounting measurement can create a systematic bias in resource allocation. The base case is not seen as competing directly for funding with strategic programs, and its accounting return may be inflated relative to its economic return. This can lead to overinvestment in the existing business and underinvestment in new strategies.

Key Takeaways

  • The base case is a reference projection, not a prediction with full informational content.
  • Two methods exist for building base cases: interactive consensus building and centralized regression on a base year.
  • Strategic investment information is often structurally different from base-case information, making consolidation difficult.
  • Accounting rates of return can be misleading when compared with internal rates of return on new investments.

8. Management of Uncertainty

The field research showed that models are primarily designed to handle complexity, not uncertainty. By formally capturing understanding about the workings of the enterprise, models reduce one source of uncertainty and free managers to concentrate on uncertainty about input assumptions and the desirability of projected outcomes.

Formal treatment of uncertainty was limited. Five organizations had developed sensitivity analysis subroutines that allow a planner to obtain the effect on specified output parameters of varying input values in defined intervals. Several other organizations used sensitivity analysis informally, relying on the curiosity and intuition of users. Only one company had attempted to incorporate probability distributions for main input variables into an enterprise simulation model, using a Monte Carlo subroutine to explore the interaction between different levels of investment in inventory and sales levels.

The reasons for avoiding probabilistic formulations are both technical and organizational. Technically, the probability distributions of variables in an enterprise model are largely interdependent, both within a single period and across time periods. A valid representation would require massive matrices of data and long processing times, which was not considered feasible by the researched companies.

More fundamentally, probabilistic modeling was not perceived as relevant to the organizational process of managing uncertainty. That process is concerned with communication, clarity of direction, and commitment to selected programs of action. Managers, as individuals, may recognize changing circumstances as a continuous process, but in communicating with one another they tend to distill continuity into discrete sets of circumstances. This crystallizes the future into vivid scenarios, making the rationale for action clearer and achieving greater commitment. Furthermore, managers must externally subscribe to the view that results are a joint function of the environment and the quality of managerial effort. They cannot simply accept that results follow a random walk.

Because plans in a large organization require extensive analysis, computation, communication, and coordination, contingency planning is based on a relatively small number of defined scenarios. Two distinct forms of contingency planning were observed.

  • In one company, several possible future scenarios were defined during base-case construction, each centered on discrete events or state variables. One scenario was chosen as the base case. The other scenarios were retained as contingency plans, with monitoring of key variables to sense when conditions might be switching. No formal probability assessments were attached to the scenarios, because if a contingency occurred, the organization would need to respond effectively. The authors speculate that intuitive probability assessments likely influenced how many resources were committed to further investment versus held in reserve.
