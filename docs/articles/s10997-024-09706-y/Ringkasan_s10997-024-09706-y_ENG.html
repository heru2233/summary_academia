1. Introduction and Research Context

Interpretation

This study investigates how corporate governance mechanisms and ownership structures influence the capital structure decisions of firms listed on the Saudi Stock Exchange (Tadawul). Capital structure refers to the mix of debt and equity a firm uses to finance its operations and growth. The study is grounded in agency theory, which examines conflicts of interest between managers, shareholders, and debt holders. Using a sample of 420 firm-year observations from 70 non-financial Saudi firms over the period 2016–2021, the research employs Generalized Least Squares (GLS) and Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) regression techniques to analyze relationships. The study fills a critical gap in the literature by focusing on an emerging market context—Saudi Arabia—where governance regulations have recently been updated to align with global standards. The findings provide practical guidance for policymakers, regulators, firm managers, and investors seeking to optimize capital structure decisions.

Key Insight

Research Gap: While extensive research exists on corporate governance and capital structure in developed markets, very few studies examine this relationship in emerging economies, and none prior to this study have comprehensively analyzed the combined effects of governance mechanisms and ownership structure on capital structure in the Saudi context. The study addresses this gap with fresh data from 2016–2021, a period following significant regulatory reforms in Saudi Arabia.

Analogy

Think of a firm's capital structure like a person's diet. Just as a balanced diet includes the right mix of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats for optimal health, a firm's capital structure requires the right balance of debt and equity for financial health. Corporate governance mechanisms act like a nutritionist—they monitor and guide decisions to ensure the firm doesn't take on too much debt (like too much sugar) or too little (like avoiding essential nutrients). Ownership structure is like the person's lifestyle—concentrated ownership (like a disciplined routine) may lead to different choices than dispersed ownership (like a more relaxed approach).

Core Research Question
How do corporate governance mechanisms and ownership structure affect firm capital structure decisions in Saudi listed firms?

Key Takeaways

  • Capital structure decisions are critical for firm performance and shareholder value, and corporate governance plays a vital role in shaping these decisions.
  • This study is the first to comprehensively examine governance mechanisms and ownership structure together in the Saudi market context.
  • The research covers 420 firm-year observations from 70 non-financial firms across 18 sectors over 2016–2021.
  • Agency theory provides the theoretical foundation, focusing on conflicts between managers, shareholders, and debt holders.
  • Findings have practical implications for policymakers, regulators, managers, and investors in emerging markets.

2. Theoretical Framework: Agency Theory

Interpretation

Agency theory, foundational to this study, explains the relationships between a company's owners (principals) and managers (agents) when making decisions that affect company performance. Conflicts of interest arise because managers may pursue their own interests rather than those of shareholders, or because shareholders and debt holders have different risk preferences. Debt financing serves as a critical governance tool—it forces managers to distribute cash flows to investors rather than waste them on unprofitable projects, thereby reducing agency costs. However, too much debt increases bankruptcy risk and can intensify conflicts between shareholders and lenders. The optimal capital structure balances these competing forces, and effective corporate governance mechanisms help achieve this balance.

Agency Theory Core Proposition
Optimal Capital Structure = Function of Agency Costs + Governance Mechanisms + Ownership Structure

Example

Scenario: A firm has excess free cash flow of $10 million. Without debt, managers might invest in a pet project with a low return (say 2%) rather than paying dividends. With debt, the firm must make interest payments, forcing managers to distribute cash and potentially reducing wasteful investments. This is the "control function of debt" proposed by Jensen (1986). In the study, firms with better governance mechanisms are better positioned to use debt optimally—enough to discipline managers but not so much as to create financial distress.

Analogy

Agency theory is like a parent-child relationship. The shareholders (parents) delegate decision-making to managers (children) but worry they might not act responsibly. Debt is like a strict allowance system—it forces the child to account for spending and limits frivolous purchases. Corporate governance mechanisms (board oversight, independent directors) are like regular check-ins and report cards that ensure the child stays on track. Too much freedom (no debt, weak governance) risks waste; too much control (excessive debt, micromanagement) risks stifling initiative and increasing stress.

Key Insight

Dual Role of Debt: Debt is both a governance tool and a source of risk. It reduces agency costs by disciplining managers, but it also increases financial distress costs and can exacerbate conflicts between shareholders and debt holders. The study uses agency theory to argue that well-designed governance mechanisms help firms navigate this trade-off effectively, leading to an optimal capital structure that maximizes firm value.

Key Takeaways

  • Agency theory addresses conflicts between managers, shareholders, and debt holders that arise from the separation of ownership and control.
  • Debt financing serves as a governance mechanism by forcing managers to distribute cash flows and reducing wasteful investments.
  • However, excessive debt increases bankruptcy risk and stakeholder conflicts, creating a trade-off.
  • Effective corporate governance helps firms achieve an optimal capital structure that balances these costs and benefits.
  • The study applies agency theory to explain how board structure and ownership structure influence leverage decisions in Saudi firms.

3. Board Structure and Capital Structure

3.1 Board Size

Interpretation

Board size refers to the total number of directors serving on a company's board. The study examines how board size influences capital structure decisions. Two competing theoretical perspectives exist. The first argues that larger boards are less effective in decision-making because coordination becomes difficult, leading to weaker governance and higher leverage (as managers take advantage of the chaos). The second argues that larger boards provide better monitoring and oversight, which reduces the need for debt as a disciplinary tool. The study's findings reveal a nuanced relationship: larger boards are associated with more long-term debt but less short-term debt. This suggests that firms with larger boards prefer longer-term financing, possibly because they can negotiate better terms, while they avoid the discipline of short-term debt that requires frequent refinancing.

Board Size Hypothesis (H1)
H1: There is a negative relationship between board size and a firm's capital structure (leverage)

Example

Finding: For a firm with 10 board members compared to one with 6, the study finds that the larger board uses about 28% more long-term debt (significant at 1% level) but about 29% less short-term debt (significant at 1% level). This means larger boards shift the debt mix toward longer maturities. Consider a Saudi manufacturing firm with a 10-member board: it might issue 10-year bonds to finance a new factory, while a firm with a 6-member board might rely more on bank overdrafts and short-term loans. The larger board's preference for long-term debt reflects better negotiation power and a desire to avoid frequent refinancing risks.

Analogy

Board size is like a committee deciding on a family vacation. A small committee (3–4 people) can quickly agree on a destination, budget, and dates—they might prefer flexible, short-term plans (like booking refundable tickets). A large committee (10+ people) struggles to reach consensus, so they plan far in advance, book non-refundable packages, and commit to long-term arrangements. The large committee's long-term focus reduces the need for last-minute decisions but makes it harder to change plans. Similarly, larger boards in the study prefer long-term debt, which provides stability but reduces flexibility.

Key Insight

Maturity Matters: The relationship between board size and capital structure depends critically on the debt maturity. Larger boards use more long-term debt but less short-term debt, suggesting that board size influences not just the total amount of debt but also its composition. This nuanced finding explains why prior studies—which often looked only at total debt—found conflicting results. The study's use of three separate debt measures (total debt, long-term debt, short-term debt) reveals this important distinction.

3.2 Board Independence

Interpretation

Board independence refers to the proportion of independent (non-executive) directors on the board. Independent directors are not part of management and are expected to provide objective oversight. Agency theory suggests two competing views. The first argues that independent directors strengthen monitoring, which reduces the need for debt as a disciplinary tool—leading to lower leverage. The second argues that independent directors help firms access capital markets more easily by improving transparency and credibility, potentially leading to higher leverage. The study finds strong support for the first view: board independence is negatively associated with all three debt measures (total debt, long-term debt, and short-term debt). Firms with more independent directors use less debt overall, consistent with the argument that independent oversight substitutes for debt as a governance mechanism.

Board Independence Hypothesis (H2)
H2: There is a negative relationship between board independence and a firm's capital structure

Example

Finding: A 10% increase in the proportion of independent directors is associated with a 3% decrease in total debt ratio, a 1.7% decrease in long-term debt, and a 1.1% decrease in short-term debt. For example, if a firm moves from 40% independent directors to 50% (a 10% increase), its total debt ratio declines from 45% to about 42%. This suggests that independent directors restrain managers from taking on excessive debt. The effect is strongest for total debt and long-term debt, indicating that independent directors are particularly cautious about long-term financial commitments that could increase bankruptcy risk.

Analogy

Independent directors are like financial advisors who are not emotionally invested in your spending decisions. When you have a financial advisor who is independent (not earning commissions on products they recommend), they tend to be more conservative and advise you to avoid excessive debt. They might say, "You don't need that big mortgage—save more and invest cautiously." In contrast, a manager without strong independent oversight might be like someone making financial decisions alone—more likely to take on debt to fund ambitious projects. Independent directors act as a brake on leverage, ensuring the firm doesn't overextend itself.

Key Insight

Substitution Effect: The negative relationship between board independence and leverage supports the substitution hypothesis—independent directors serve as an alternative governance mechanism that reduces the need for debt to discipline managers. When independent directors are actively monitoring, the disciplinary role of debt becomes less necessary. This is a crucial finding for policymakers: strengthening board independence can reduce excessive risk-taking through debt while still maintaining effective governance.

3.3 CEO Duality

Interpretation

CEO duality occurs when the same person holds both the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) and Chairperson of the Board positions. Agency theory generally argues that separating these roles enhances governance effectiveness because it prevents the concentration of power and ensures independent oversight. When one person holds both roles, the board's monitoring capacity is weakened, potentially leading to higher agency costs. The study finds that CEO duality is negatively associated with long-term debt (significant at the 5% level), meaning firms with CEO duality use less long-term debt. This supports the agency theory argument that lenders perceive firms with CEO duality as riskier and therefore limit their lending, or that managers with dual roles prefer less debt to maintain flexibility and avoid the disciplinary constraints of debt.

CEO Duality Hypothesis (H3)
H3: CEO duality is negatively related to a firm's capital structure

Example

Finding: Firms with CEO duality have about 3.2% less long-term debt compared to firms where the CEO and Chair roles are separate. For instance, consider two Saudi firms of similar size and profitability: Firm A has CEO duality (the CEO is also the Chair), while Firm B separates the roles. Firm A's long-term debt ratio might be 20%, while Firm B's is 23.2%. This suggests that lenders are more cautious about lending long-term to firms where power is concentrated in one individual. The result is specific to long-term debt—no significant effect is found for short-term debt or total debt, indicating that the impact of CEO duality is most pronounced for longer-term financing commitments.

Analogy

CEO duality is like a student who is also the teacher's assistant grading their own exams. The concentration of power makes it harder to maintain objectivity and accountability. Lenders (like parents or school administrators) might be reluctant to give such a student significant long-term responsibilities (like managing a large budget) because they worry about oversight. In contrast, when the CEO and Chair roles are separate (like a student and a teacher), there is a system of checks and balances that gives lenders more confidence to extend long-term credit.

Key Insight

Lender Perception: The negative relationship between CEO duality and long-term debt suggests that lenders perceive unitary leadership as riskier. This is consistent with agency theory: the concentration of decision-making and control in one person weakens monitoring and increases the potential for opportunistic behavior. Lenders respond by limiting long-term exposure, effectively forcing firms with CEO duality to rely more on short-term debt or equity. This finding has implications for corporate governance codes—separating the CEO and Chair roles can improve a firm's access to long-term debt financing.

Key Takeaways

  • Board size has a dual effect: larger boards use more long-term debt but less short-term debt, shifting the maturity structure of debt.
  • Board independence is strongly negatively associated with all forms of debt, supporting the substitution hypothesis that independent directors replace debt as a governance mechanism.
  • CEO duality reduces long-term debt, consistent with lender concerns about concentrated power and weaker oversight.
  • The effects of board structure on capital structure are nuanced and depend on the specific debt measure used (total, long-term, or short-term).
  • These findings support agency theory predictions that effective governance mechanisms reduce the need for debt as a disciplinary tool.

4. Ownership Structure and Capital Structure

4.1 Ownership Concentration

Interpretation

Ownership concentration refers to the proportion of shares held by the largest shareholders (in this study, the top five shareholders). Agency theory offers two perspectives on how ownership concentration affects leverage. The first argues that large shareholders have both the incentive and the power to monitor managers effectively, which can reduce agency problems and potentially reduce the need for debt as a disciplinary tool. The second argues that large shareholders may use debt to expropriate wealth from minority shareholders—by increasing leverage, they can take on more risk and extract private benefits. The study finds that ownership concentration is positively associated with total debt and short-term debt (both significant at the 5% level), supporting the expropriation hypothesis. Large shareholders in Saudi firms appear to use debt as a tool to increase their control and potentially extract benefits at the expense of minority shareholders.

Ownership Concentration Hypothesis (H4)
H4: There is a positive relationship between ownership concentration and a firm's capital structure

Example

Finding: A 10% increase in ownership concentration (the share held by the top five shareholders) is associated with a 0.87% increase in total debt and a 0.53% increase in short-term debt. For example, if the top five shareholders increase their collective stake from 30% to 40%, the firm's total debt ratio rises from 43% to about 43.87%. This effect is relatively modest but statistically significant. In the Saudi context, where institutional ownership averages 26% and government ownership 12%, concentrated owners appear to encourage debt financing to enhance returns and potentially extract private benefits.

Analogy

Ownership concentration is like a group of friends deciding on a restaurant. When one friend has a dominant say (concentrated ownership), they might choose a restaurant that gives them personal benefits (like their favorite cuisine or a place where they get a discount), even if it's not the best choice for everyone. They might also be willing to spend more (take on more debt) because they have the power to steer the decision. In contrast, when decision-making is spread across many friends (dispersed ownership), no single person can push for a choice that benefits only themselves, and the group tends to be more conservative with spending.

Key Insight

Expropriation Risk: The positive relationship between ownership concentration and leverage supports the "expropriation hypothesis"—large shareholders use debt as a tool to extract wealth from minority shareholders. By increasing leverage, they can take on more risk, potentially earn higher returns, and enjoy greater control, while minority shareholders bear the downside risk. This finding is particularly relevant for emerging markets like Saudi Arabia, where minority shareholder protections may be weaker than in developed markets. The study suggests that policymakers should strengthen minority shareholder protections to mitigate this risk.

4.2 Managerial Ownership

Interpretation

Managerial ownership refers to the proportion of shares held by board members and managers. Agency theory predicts that when managers own a significant stake in the firm, their interests become more aligned with those of external shareholders—this is the "incentive alignment" hypothesis. When managers have "skin in the game," they are more likely to make decisions that increase firm value, including using debt optimally. The study finds that managerial ownership is positively associated with total debt and long-term debt (both significant at the 1% level), strongly supporting the incentive alignment hypothesis. Managers with higher ownership stakes use more debt, particularly long-term debt, to increase firm value and enhance their own wealth.

Managerial Ownership Hypothesis (H5)
H5: There is a positive relationship between managerial ownership and a firm's capital structure

Example

Finding: A 10% increase in managerial ownership (from 4% to 14%, for example) is associated with a 3.1% increase in total debt ratio and a 3.22% increase in long-term debt ratio. For instance, if a firm's managers increase their collective stake from 5% to 15%, the total debt ratio rises from 43% to about 46.1%, and long-term debt rises from 23% to about 26.2%. This is a substantial economic effect. The result is consistent with the idea that managers who own more shares are more willing to take on debt to finance growth, because they share in the upside benefits while also bearing the downside risks.

Analogy

Managerial ownership is like a chef who owns a stake in the restaurant. When the chef has ownership, they care more about the restaurant's success—they're willing to invest in better ingredients, hire more staff, and even take out loans to expand, because they benefit directly from the restaurant's profits. In contrast, a chef who is just an employee might be more risk-averse and avoid debt, because they don't share in the upside and worry about job security. Aligning incentives through ownership encourages managers to make bolder, value-enhancing decisions, including using debt strategically.

Key Insight

Incentive Alignment Works: The strong positive relationship between managerial ownership and leverage provides compelling evidence for the incentive alignment hypothesis. When managers have significant ownership stakes, they behave more like owners and are willing to use debt to enhance firm value. This finding is particularly important for emerging markets: encouraging managerial ownership through stock-based compensation can help align manager-shareholder interests and promote optimal capital structure decisions. The effect is strongest for long-term debt, suggesting that managers with ownership stakes focus on long-term value creation through appropriate financing.

Key Takeaways

  • Ownership concentration is positively associated with total debt and short-term debt, supporting the expropriation hypothesis that large shareholders use debt to extract private benefits.
  • Managerial ownership is strongly positively associated with total debt and long-term debt, supporting the incentive alignment hypothesis that managers with ownership stakes make value-enhancing financing decisions.
  • The effects of ownership structure on capital structure are economically significant and robust across different model specifications.
  • These findings highlight the importance of considering ownership structure when designing corporate governance policies.
  • The Saudi context, with its unique ownership characteristics (high institutional and government ownership), provides valuable insights for other emerging markets.

5. Research Design and Methodology

Interpretation

The study employs a rigorous quantitative research design using panel data from 70 Saudi non-financial listed firms over 6 years (2016–2021), yielding 420 firm-year observations. The sample excludes financial firms, insurance companies, and real estate investment trusts (REITs) due to their unique regulatory environments and capital structures. Three dependent variables measure capital structure: total debt ratio (TD), long-term debt ratio (LTD), and short-term debt ratio (STD). Five independent variables capture governance and ownership: board size, board independence, CEO duality, ownership concentration, and managerial ownership. Seven control variables account for firm-specific characteristics: firm size, sales growth, asset tangibility, liquidity, profitability, audit firm size, and firm age. The study uses random-effects GLS regression as the primary method and GMM regression for robustness checks to address potential endogeneity issues.

The research questions are the blueprint—they guide what we build. The sample selection is like choosing the building materials—we need the right quality and quantity. Key Insight

Methodological Rigor: The study's use of three separate debt measures (total, long-term, short-term) is a key strength. Many prior studies examine only total debt, which can mask important differences in how governance affects debt maturity. By disaggregating debt, the study reveals that governance mechanisms influence not just the total amount of debt but also its composition. The use of both GLS and GMM methods addresses potential endogeneity concerns, where causality might run in both directions (e.g., leverage might influence governance choices, or unobserved factors might affect both). The random-effects specification is justified by the Hausman test (p = 0.9963), indicating that firm-specific effects are uncorrelated with the regressors.

Key Takeaways

  • The study uses a balanced panel of 420 firm-year observations from 70 Saudi non-financial firms over 2016–2021.
  • Three dependent variables measure capital structure: total debt, long-term debt, and short-term debt ratios.
  • Five independent variables capture governance mechanisms (board size, independence, CEO duality) and ownership structure (concentration, managerial ownership).
  • Seven control variables account for firm-specific characteristics that influence capital structure.
  • Random-effects GLS regression is the primary method, with GMM regression used for robustness to address endogeneity.
  • The Hausman test supports the random-effects specification over fixed effects.

6. Empirical Results and Findings

Interpretation

The empirical results reveal a complex and nuanced picture of how corporate governance and ownership structure influence capital structure decisions in Saudi listed firms. The models explain between 46.8% and 55.4% of the variation in capital structure (adjusted R²), indicating strong explanatory power. Key findings include: (1) Board size has opposing effects on long-term debt (positive) and short-term debt (negative), showing that larger boards shift toward longer-term financing. (2) Board independence is consistently negative across all debt measures, supporting the substitution hypothesis. (3) CEO duality reduces long-term debt, consistent with lender caution. (4) Ownership concentration increases total and short-term debt, supporting the expropriation hypothesis. (5) Managerial ownership strongly increases total and long-term debt, supporting incentive alignment. Control variables also show expected relationships: larger firms use more long-term debt, profitable firms use less debt, and firms with more tangible assets use more long-term debt but less short-term debt.

Interpreting Coefficients: Consider the effect of managerial ownership on total debt. Key Insight

Asymmetric Effects: One of the most important findings is that the same governance variable can have opposite effects on different types of debt. Board size increases long-term debt but decreases short-term debt. This asymmetry explains why prior studies—which often used only total debt—found inconsistent results. The study's contribution is not just finding that governance matters, but showing precisely how it matters for different components of capital structure. This has practical implications: managers and policymakers need to consider the maturity structure of debt, not just the total amount, when evaluating the impact of governance reforms.

Key Takeaways

  • Ownership concentration increases total and short-term debt, supporting the expropriation hypothesis.

7. Robustness Tests and Validation

Interpretation

To ensure the reliability of the findings, the study conducts several robustness tests. The primary concern in corporate governance research is endogeneity—the possibility that the relationship between governance and capital structure is driven by unobserved factors, or that causality runs in both directions. For example, firms with high leverage might adopt better governance practices to reassure lenders, rather than governance practices affecting leverage. To address this, the study employs the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) regression, which uses lagged values of variables as instruments to control for endogeneity. The GMM results confirm the main findings: board independence remains negatively associated with leverage, managerial ownership remains positively associated, and the other relationships hold. Additional robustness checks include alternative variable definitions (e.g., using different measures of ownership concentration) and sub-sample analyses. The consistency of results across different methods strengthens confidence in the conclusions.

GMM Robustness Model
ΔYit = α + βΔXit + γYi,t-1 + εit

Example

Endogeneity Concern: Suppose a well-governed firm uses less debt (as our findings suggest). But it's also possible that a firm with low debt can afford to invest in better governance because it has more financial flexibility. Or, a firm that wants to take on more debt might improve its governance to convince lenders. The GMM method addresses this by using past values of the variables as instruments. For instance, the board independence in 2016 is used to predict leverage in 2017, but not vice versa. This helps establish the direction of causality. The GMM results confirm that board independence in prior periods predicts lower leverage in subsequent periods, supporting the interpretation that governance affects capital structure, rather than the reverse.

Analogy

Robustness tests are like a pilot's pre-flight checklist. Before takeoff, the pilot checks multiple systems independently—engines, hydraulics, electronics, fuel—to ensure everything is working correctly. Each check is a different test, but they all point to the same conclusion: the plane is safe to fly. Similarly, the study uses multiple methods (GLS, GMM, different variable definitions) to check if the results hold. If the same patterns emerge across different tests, we can be confident the findings are reliable. The GMM method is like the most thorough check—it tests for hidden problems (endogeneity) that simpler checks might miss.

Key Insight

Causal Inference: The robustness tests, particularly the GMM results, strengthen the case for causal interpretation. While no observational study can prove causality definitively, the consistency of results across multiple methods, the use of lagged variables, and the theoretical grounding in agency theory all support the interpretation that governance mechanisms and ownership structure causally influence capital structure decisions. This is important for policymakers: it suggests that reforms to improve governance (e.g., requiring more independent directors) will likely lead to changes in how firms finance themselves.

Key Takeaways

  • GMM regression results confirm the main findings, addressing concerns about endogeneity and reverse causality.
  • Alternative variable definitions and sub-sample analyses produce consistent results, supporting the robustness of the conclusions.
  • The Hausman test supports random-effects specification, and VIF values below 2 indicate no multicollinearity issues.
  • The consistency of results across different methods strengthens confidence in causal interpretation.
  • Robustness testing is essential for establishing the reliability of empirical findings in corporate governance research.

8. Discussion, Implications, and Conclusion

Interpretation

The study's findings have important implications for theory, practice, and policy. From a theoretical perspective, the results support agency theory predictions that governance mechanisms and ownership structure are important determinants of capital structure. The study extends the literature by showing that the effects vary by debt maturity, explaining why prior studies found conflicting results. From a practical perspective, the findings provide guidance for firm managers, investors, and policymakers. For managers, the results highlight the importance of board composition and ownership structure in shaping financing decisions. For investors, the findings suggest that firms with strong governance (independent boards, separate CEO-Chair roles) tend to use less debt, which may indicate lower risk but also lower potential returns. For policymakers, the results suggest that governance reforms (e.g., requiring more independent directors, separating CEO and Chair roles) can influence corporate financing decisions and potentially reduce excessive risk-taking. The study also highlights the need for minority shareholder protection, given the finding that concentrated ownership is associated with higher leverage.

Policy Implications

For Saudi Regulators: The Saudi Corporate Governance Regulations (2017) can be strengthened by: (1) requiring a minimum proportion of independent directors (the current average is 48%, but some firms have as low as 30%); (2) encouraging separation of CEO and Chair roles (34% of sample firms still have duality); (3) implementing measures to protect minority shareholders from expropriation by large shareholders; (4) promoting managerial ownership through stock-based compensation to align incentives. The findings suggest that such reforms would lead to more optimal capital structure decisions and potentially reduce bankruptcy risk.

Example

Practical Application: Consider a Saudi firm with the following characteristics: 8 board members (30% independent), CEO duality, top 5 shareholders hold 40% of shares, and managers hold 2% of shares. Based on the study's findings, this firm likely has: (1) relatively high leverage due to low board independence and high ownership concentration; (2) less long-term debt due to CEO duality; (3) potential for minority shareholder expropriation due to concentrated ownership. If the firm reforms its governance by increasing independent directors to 50%, separating CEO-Chair roles, and increasing managerial ownership to 10%, the model predicts: (1) lower total debt (by about 6% due to more independent directors); (2) more long-term debt (by about 3.2% due to no CEO duality); (3) higher total debt and long-term debt (by about 3.1% and 3.2% due to higher managerial ownership). The net effect depends on the relative magnitudes of these changes, but the firm's capital structure would likely become more balanced and better aligned with shareholder interests.

Key Insight

Integrated Governance Framework: The study's most important contribution is demonstrating that governance mechanisms and ownership structure should be studied together, not separately. Board structure, ownership concentration, and managerial ownership interact in complex ways to influence capital structure. For example, the positive effect of managerial ownership on leverage may be stronger in firms with more independent boards, or the negative effect of board independence on leverage may be weaker in firms with concentrated ownership. Future research should explore these interaction effects. For policymakers, this means that governance reforms should be designed as a package rather than piecemeal—addressing board composition, CEO duality, and ownership structure together to achieve optimal outcomes.

Key Takeaways

  • Board independence is consistently negatively associated with leverage, supporting the substitution hypothesis that independent directors replace debt as a governance mechanism.
  • Board size has opposing effects on long-term debt (positive) and short-term debt (negative), revealing a shift in debt maturity structure.
  • CEO duality reduces long-term debt, consistent with lender perception of higher risk and weaker oversight.
  • Ownership concentration increases total and short-term debt, supporting the expropriation hypothesis and highlighting the need for minority shareholder protection.
  • Managerial ownership strongly increases total and long-term debt, supporting the incentive alignment hypothesis and the value of stock-based compensation.
  • Firm-specific characteristics (size, growth, tangibility, liquidity, profitability, age) are important determinants of capital structure, consistent with prior literature.
  • The study provides guidance for policymakers in developing governance codes, for managers in making financing decisions, and for investors in assessing firm risk and value.
  • Future research should explore interaction effects between governance mechanisms and ownership structure, and examine how these relationships evolve over time as Saudi governance regulations continue to develop.

Overall Summary: Key Takeaways

  • Research Focus: This study examines how corporate governance mechanisms (board size, board independence, CEO duality) and ownership structure (ownership concentration, managerial ownership) affect capital structure decisions of 70 Saudi non-financial listed firms from 2016 to 2021 (420 firm-year observations).
  • Theoretical Foundation: Grounded in agency theory, the study argues that governance mechanisms and ownership structure influence the agency costs that arise from conflicts between managers, shareholders, and debt holders, thereby affecting optimal capital structure.
  • Methodology: The study uses random-effects GLS regression as the primary method and GMM regression for robustness, with three separate debt measures (total debt, long-term debt, short-term debt) to capture the full picture of capital structure.
  • Key Findings — Board Structure: Board independence is negatively associated with all debt measures (supporting the substitution hypothesis). Board size has opposing effects (positive for long-term debt, negative for short-term debt). CEO duality reduces long-term debt.
  • Key Findings — Ownership Structure: Ownership concentration increases total and short-term debt (supporting the expropriation hypothesis). Managerial ownership strongly increases total and long-term debt (supporting the incentive alignment hypothesis).
  • Control Variables: Larger firms, firms with higher growth, and firms with more tangible assets use more long-term debt. More profitable, more liquid, and older firms use less debt overall.
  • Robustness: GMM regression results confirm the main findings, addressing endogeneity concerns. Results are consistent across alternative variable definitions and sub-sample analyses.
  • Policy Implications: The findings support Saudi governance reforms that promote board independence, separate CEO-Chair roles, protect minority shareholders, and encourage managerial ownership through stock-based compensation.
  • Practical Implications: Managers should consider governance structure when making financing decisions. Investors should evaluate governance quality when assessing firm risk and capital structure. Regulators should design governance codes that address board structure, ownership concentration, and managerial incentives together.
  • Contribution: This is the first comprehensive study of governance mechanisms and ownership structure and their combined effects on capital structure in the Saudi context, filling a significant gap in the emerging market literature and providing a foundation for future research.
