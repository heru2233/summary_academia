Summary: Finance Theory and Financial Strategy

Author: Stewart C. Myers (MIT Sloan School of Management)
Source: Interfaces, Vol. 14, No. 1, pp. 126-137, January-February 1984

Core Argument

This article explains why finance theory, despite its major advances, has had little impact on corporate strategic planning. Myers offers three explanations and shows how the gap might be bridged.

The Problem

Strategic planning is fundamentally about how a firm allocates its resources, including capital, across lines of business. Finance theory has made major advances in understanding how capital markets work and how risky assets are valued. Tools derived from finance theory, particularly discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis, are widely used. Yet finance theory has had scant impact on strategic planning.

Myers offers three explanations:

  1. Different languages and cultures between finance theory and traditional strategic planning make the two seem incompatible.
  2. DCF analysis may have been misused, and consequently not accepted, in strategic applications.
  3. DCF analysis may simply be insufficient to capture strategic value, even when properly applied.

The Relevant Finance Theory

Myers summarizes the basic finance concepts that should matter for strategy. The core idea: each investment project is a "mini-firm" that is all-equity financed. If you could trade its stock, you would know its value. Net Present Value (NPV) follows:

\[ PV = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1 + r)^t} \]

Where \(C_t\) is after-tax cash flow and \(r\) is the opportunity cost of capital reflecting project risk.

Key principles from finance theory:

  • Value is additive: Firm value = sum of all project values.
  • Intangible assets and growth opportunities should be valued, not just tangible assets.
  • Cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source. Financing has a secondary impact on value.
  • Focus on cash flows and fundamentals, not accounting figures like EPS or book earnings.

The Gap Between Theory and Strategy

Myers observes that in many firms, strategic analysis actually focuses on things that finance theory considers irrelevant:

  • Impact on EPS and book earnings: Managers worry that strategic decisions will hurt accounting numbers, when in fact if value increases, accounting figures should not matter.
  • Diversification to reduce risk: Managers think diversification reduces firm risk. But capital markets allow investors to diversify cheaply on their own. So the firm does not need to "replace" the diversification investors can do themselves.
  • Conflict between financial and strategic analysis is treated as "a fact of life," rather than as a problem to be solved.

There are also criticisms of the financial approach itself. Critics say financial analysis is too short-sighted and diverts attention from long-run values. Hayes and Garvin even called capital investment "an act of faith." Myers defends finance theory, noting that much of the criticism actually targets the misuse of DCF, not the theory itself.

Two Cultures, One Problem

Myers calls this "two cultures" looking at the same problem. He offers an interesting illustration:

A smart manager knows that in long-run equilibrium, all projects have zero NPV. So if NPV is positive, it must be explained by:

  • A short-run deviation from equilibrium, or
  • A permanent competitive advantage.

If neither explanation applies, a positive NPV is suspect. The same logic works in reverse for negative NPV. Strategic planning should help answer this question, but systematic reconciliation efforts are rare.

Misuse of Finance Theory

Myers identifies several common mistakes in applying DCF:

1. Ranking by IRR Instead of NPV

Projects are often ranked by Internal Rate of Return (IRR) rather than NPV. The problem is IRR is easier to achieve for short, low-investment projects. Long-lived, capital-intensive projects get pushed down the list even when their NPV is substantial. Yet most firms large enough for strategic planning have free access to capital markets.

2. Inconsistent Treatment of Inflation

Many firms use high nominal discount rates but fail to adjust cash flows for inflation. As a result, long-lived projects look less attractive even when their real value is unchanged.

3. Unrealistically High Discount Rates

Discount rates are often inflated for several reasons:

  • Premiums added for risks that investors can easily diversify away.
  • Rates raised to offset managerial optimism bias, but this penalizes long-term projects if the bias does not grow geometrically.
  • Projects risky at start-up but normal afterward often get a risk premium for the entire life, when it should only apply to the start-up period.

4. Organizational Problems

The capital budgeting process is bottom-up. Proposals must survive multiple approval levels. Along the way, political alliances form and cash flow forecasts get bent to meet known standards. The result: projects that make it to the top appear to meet profitability standards, but are not necessarily the best ones.

Finance Theory May Be Incomplete

Myers also acknowledges that even with perfect DCF application, four problems remain difficult:

  1. Estimating cost of capital: Always hard because it is an expected return. But historical capital market data is available and reasonable estimates can be made.
  2. Forecasting cash flows: Impossible to predict actual cash flows accurately, but DCF does not require that. It requires estimates of the mean of possible outcomes.
  3. Cross-project relationships: Estimating how one project affects others simultaneously is difficult, especially with blurred business unit boundaries.
  4. Links between today's investments and tomorrow's opportunities: This is the hardest problem. For example, a firm invests in a negative-NPV project to "open the door" for a valuable second-stage project.

The key insight: the second-stage project is actually an option. The firm is not committed to undertaking it. If the first stage fails, the firm can stop. Conventional DCF cannot properly value options. The first-stage investment purchases an intangible asset: an option on the second stage.

Limits of DCF and Option Value

Myers maps out where DCF works and where it does not:

Asset TypeDCF Effectiveness
Bonds, preferred stocks, fixed incomeHighly effective; this is the industry standard
Safe stocks with regular dividendsEffective and widely used
Firms with significant growth opportunitiesLess effective. Better to use \(P_0 = EPS/r + PVGO\)
Options (calls/puts)Not effective at all. Requires separate option models

The formula \(P_0 = EPS/r + PVGO\) decomposes stock price into two components: the value of assets-in-place and the present value of future growth opportunities. PVGO is actually a portfolio of options, the firm's rights to invest in second-stage, third-stage, and later projects.

For real assets, option pricing theory can be applied. Examples:

  • Oil exploration leases: Option value comes from the lessee's right to delay drilling decisions until reserves and future oil prices are known.
  • Asset abandonment value: A second-hand market provides a put option that increases asset value because owners can "exit" from bad projects.

Lessons for Corporate Strategy

Myers suggests viewing strategy as managing a portfolio of real options. The process involves:

  1. Acquiring options -- either through direct investment (R&D, product design) or as a by-product of capital investment (investing in a stage-1 project even with negative NPV).
  2. Abandoning options that are too expensive or no longer relevant.
  3. Exercising valuable options at the right time.

Myers also cautions that simple financial approaches (e.g., traditional DCF models) are more suited to "cash cows" than to growth businesses full of risk and intangible assets.

Conclusion: Bridging the Gap

Myers closes with two main recommendations:

  1. Apply existing finance theory correctly. Many problems actually stem from DCF misuse, not from theoretical weakness.
  2. Extend the theory. The most promising research line is using option pricing theory to model time-series interactions between investments.

Both sides -- finance and strategy -- need to make a conscious effort to reconcile their analyses. Although complete reconciliation will rarely be possible, the attempt should uncover hidden assumptions and lead to a deeper understanding of strategic choices.
