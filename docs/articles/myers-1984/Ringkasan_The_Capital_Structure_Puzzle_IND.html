1. Research Question and Significance

The central research question posed in this article is: Why has finance theory, despite its major advances in understanding capital markets and asset valuation, had such scant impact on strategic planning? Stewart C. Myers of the MIT Sloan School of Management frames this as an intellectual and practical puzzle with significant implications for how firms allocate capital across lines of business. Strategic planning is defined broadly as the process of deciding how to commit the firm's resources across lines of business; the financial side of strategic planning allocates a particular resource: capital.

Three competing explanations are proposed and examined:

  • Cultural/linguistic divide: Finance theory and traditional strategic planning approaches are separated by differences in language and organizational "culture," making communication and mutual understanding inherently difficult.
  • Misapplication of DCF: Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis may have been misused in strategic applications, leading to poor results and subsequent rejection of the method by strategic planners.
  • Intrinsic failure of DCF: DCF analysis may fail in strategic applications even when properly applied—a deeper, more troubling possibility that points to limitations in the financial theory itself.

Myers does not claim these three explanations are exhaustive. Rather, he treats them as partially true, converging explanations for an observed and costly disconnect between two fields that ought to be mutually reinforcing.

The importance of this question is substantial. Capital investment decisions are among the most consequential actions a firm can take. If finance theory—the discipline most directly concerned with valuation and risk—is being ignored or misapplied in strategic contexts, firms may systematically misallocate capital, destroy shareholder value, and harm long-term competitiveness. Conversely, if strategic planning contains insights that finance theory overlooks, then finance theory itself must be extended. Either way, understanding the gap is a prerequisite for improving both corporate practice and financial theory.

Example: The Strategic Override of Financial Analysis

Myers observes that when low net present value (NPV) projects are nurtured "for strategic reasons," strategic analysis overrides financial measures of value. Conversely, projects with apparently high NPVs are passed over when they do not fit the firm's strategic objectives. This conflict is treated as a "fact of life" rather than an anomaly demanding reconciliation.

Interpretation: In practice, financial and strategic analyses often yield conflicting answers, and the conflict is accepted rather than resolved.

Practical lesson: A firm cannot maximize value unless financial and strategic analyses are integrated; allowing one to override the other without explicit reconciliation creates hidden assumptions and potential value destruction.

Analogy: Two Navigators, One Ship

Imagine a ship where one navigator uses a compass (finance theory) and another uses the stars (strategic planning). Each method can work, but if they disagree and no one asks why, the ship may sail confidently in the wrong direction. The compass and the stars are measuring the same reality; disagreement signals an error in one—or both—methods, not a fact of nature.

Key Insight

The gap between finance theory and strategic planning is not merely a communication problem—it may be partly a genuine limitation of standard DCF methods when applied to strategic investments with embedded options. This third explanation is the most intellectually significant and sets up the article's central theoretical contribution.

Key Takeaways

  • Finance theory has had limited impact on strategic planning despite its intellectual advances.
  • Three explanations are offered: cultural differences, DCF misuse, and intrinsic DCF limitations.
  • All three explanations are partially true; the challenge is understanding how they combine.

2. Core Concepts of Finance Theory

To understand the gap, Myers first establishes the minimum set of principles that constitute "finance theory" as relevant to capital investment decisions. The central mental model treats each investment project as a mini-firm, all-equity financed. If the mini-firm's stock could be actively traded, its market price would represent its present value; net present value (NPV) is calculated by subtracting the required investment outlay at time zero.

This logic leads directly to the use of valuation models consistent with how similar securities are priced in capital markets. The theory is usually condensed into a single model: discounted cash flow (DCF).

Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Present Value Formula
\[ PV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

Symbol explanation:

  • PV = present (market) value of the project.
  • Ct = forecasted incremental cash flow after corporate taxes, strictly speaking the mean of the distribution of possible cash flows in period t.
  • T = project life; CT includes any salvage value.
  • r = opportunity cost of capital, defined as the equilibrium expected rate of return on securities equivalent in risk to the project being valued.

Mathematical relationship: Present value is the sum of expected future cash flows, each discounted back to the present at the opportunity cost of capital. The discount rate reflects the riskiness of the cash flow stream: riskier projects have higher r, which reduces present value for a given set of cash flows.

Interpretation: PV tells the manager what investors would be willing to pay today for the project's future cash flows, given alternative investment opportunities of equal risk in the capital market.

Practical use: NPV equals PV less the cash outlay required at t = 0. A positive NPV means the project creates value; a negative NPV means it destroys value.

Assumption/caution: The formula requires a meaningful estimate of r, and Ct must represent the mean of possible outcomes, not a "best guess" or conservative estimate. Additionally, the formula presumes that the project's risk profile is constant over time or properly averaged by a single r.

Net Present Value (NPV)
\[ NPV = PV - I_0 \]

Here I0 is the cash outlay required at t = 0. NPV measures the increment to firm value from undertaking the project. A positive NPV indicates that the project is worth more than it costs; a negative NPV indicates value destruction.

Key property—value additivity: Since present values add, the value of the firm equals the sum of the values of all its mini-firms. If the DCF formula works for each project separately, it should work for any collection of projects, a line of business, or the firm as a whole.

Practical implication: A firm or line of business consists of intangible as well as tangible assets, and growth opportunities as well as assets in place. Intangible assets and growth opportunities are reflected in stock prices and can, in principle, be valued in capital budgeting. Projects that bring tangible assets or growth opportunities to the firm receive correspondingly higher NPVs.

Opportunity cost of capital (OCC): The expected rate of return available on securities of equivalent risk in the capital market. It varies from project to project. In principle, each project has its own cost of capital; in practice, firms group similar projects into risk classes and apply a common cost of capital to all projects in a class.

Critical principle: The opportunity cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source. Financing decisions generally have a second-order impact on value; more value is created on the left-hand side of the balance sheet (real assets) than on the right-hand side (financing claims). The advantage, if any, of departing from all-equity financing is typically adjusted for through a somewhat lowered discount rate.

Example: Accounting vs. Economic Value

Suppose a positive NPV project sharply reduces book earnings in its early stages. Finance theory recommends forging ahead, trusting investors to see through accounting bias to the project's true value. Empirical evidence cited by Myers indicates that investors do see through accounting biases; they do not naively look at last quarter's or last year's earnings per share. If they did, all stocks would sell at the same price-earnings ratio.

Interpretation: Cash flows and risk—not accounting allocations—drive security prices.

Practical lesson: Managers should not reject a value-creating project solely because it depresses near-term book earnings.

Analogy: The Mini-Firm Mental Model

Think of a standalone machine inside a factory. If the machine were a separate company with its own stock ticker, investors could trade it, and its price would reveal its value. The DCF formula simulates this hypothetical market price. The factory owner can then compare the machine's market value to its installation cost to decide whether buying it is a good deal.

Key Insight

The entire edifice of finance theory relevant to capital budgeting rests on a simple but powerful idea: project value = the market price the project would command if independently traded, and this price equals the present value of expected cash flows discounted at an appropriate opportunity cost of capital. Any tool that fails to capture this logic—or misapplies it—will mislead managers.

Key Takeaways

  • Each project can be treated as a mini-firm; its value is its hypothetical market price.
  • NPV = PV − I₀; positive NPV creates value, negative NPV destroys value.
  • The opportunity cost of capital depends on use of funds, not source; financing is second-order.
  • Cash flows, not accounting allocations, determine value.

3. The Gap Between Finance Theory and Strategic Planning

Myers deliberately resists describing strategic planning as "capital budgeting on a grand scale." His reason is that capital budgeting in practice is a bottom-up process: the aim is to find and undertake specific assets or projects worth more than they cost. Picking valuable pieces does not guarantee maximum value for the whole. Piecemeal, bottom-up capital budgeting is not strategic planning.

Nevertheless, capital budgeting techniques ought to work for the whole as well as for the parts. A strategic commitment of capital to a line of business is itself an investment project. If management invests, it must believe the value of the firm increases by more than the amount of capital committed—otherwise it is throwing money away. Thus, an implicit estimate of NPV underlies every strategic investment decision.

However, most strategic planners are not guided by modern finance tools. Strategic and financial analyses are not reconciled, even when both address the same major project. The conflict between financial and strategic analyses is treated as a fact of life, not as an anomaly demanding reconciliation.

Symptoms of the gap:

1. Focus on accounting measures. Many managers worry about a strategic decision's impact on book rate of return or earnings per share. If they are convinced the plan adds to the firm's value, its impact on accounting figures should be irrelevant.

2. Diversification to reduce risk. Some managers pursue diversification to reduce risk "as they see it." But investors see a firm's risk differently. In capital markets, diversification is cheap and easy; investors who want to diversify can do so on their own at virtually no cost. Corporate diversification is redundant; the market will not pay extra for it. If the market were willing to pay a premium for diversified firms, closed-end funds would sell at premiums over their net asset values, and conglomerates would be worth more to investors than their components traded separately. In reality, closed-end funds sell at discounts, and conglomerates appear to sell at discounts too, though the latter is hard to prove since a conglomerate's components are not traded separately.

3. The "cash cow" misconception. Much strategic planning literature views firms as having a fixed stock of capital, so that "cash cows" are needed to finance investment in rapidly growing lines of business. Myers points out that the firms that pioneered strategic planning actually had easy access to capital markets, as do almost all public companies. Firms may not like the price they pay for capital, but that price is the opportunity cost of capital—the proper standard for new investment. Internal capital market constraints are often self-imposed organizational devices, not genuine external financing limits.

Example: The Cash Cow Trap

A firm with a mature, slow-growth "cash cow" division and a rapidly growing technology division might believe it cannot invest in the growth division without first harvesting the cash cow. But if the firm is publicly traded and creditworthy, it can raise capital in external markets at the opportunity cost of capital.

Interpretation: The cash cow logic is an illusion for firms with capital market access.

Practical lesson: The proper question is whether the growth division's expected returns exceed the opportunity cost of capital—not whether internally generated funds are available.

Analogy: Diversifying Your Own Portfolio

Imagine going to a restaurant and ordering a "surf and turf" platter because you want both steak and lobster in one meal, and you think this is efficient. But you could instead order just a steak and open a can of lobster meat at home. The restaurant's combination plate provides no benefit that you could not create yourself; likewise, corporate diversification adds no value that investors cannot replicate with a few stock purchases.

Key Insight

The market does not pay a premium for corporate diversification because investors can diversify their own portfolios cheaply and easily. Managers who diversify "to reduce risk" are solving a problem that investors have already solved for themselves, at no benefit to shareholders.

Key Takeaways

  • Strategic planning is not just "capital budgeting on a grand scale"—it is a top-down process.
  • Book rate of return and EPS impacts are irrelevant to value if NPV is understood.
  • Corporate diversification does not create value; investors can diversify internally.
  • Cash cow financing logic is flawed for firms with access to capital markets.

4. Two Cultures and One Problem

Myers proposes that finance theory and strategic planning could be viewed as "two cultures" examining the same problem from different angles. Perhaps only differences in language and approach make them appear incompatible. If so, better communication and a determined effort to reconcile them could bridge the gap. But he offers a deeper analysis suggesting the gap is not merely linguistic.

Consider what can go wrong with standard DCF analyses of a series of major projects:

  • Even careful analyses are subject to random error. There is a 50 percent probability of a positive NPV for a truly borderline project.
  • Firms must guard against these errors dominating project choice.

Smart managers apply the following check: In long-run competitive equilibrium, all projects earn zero NPV. Therefore, a positive NPV must be explained by either (a) a short-run deviation from equilibrium, or (b) some permanent competitive advantage. If neither explanation applies, the positive NPV is suspect. Conversely, a negative NPV is suspect if a competitive advantage or short-run deviation from equilibrium favors the project.

In other words, smart managers do not accept positive (or negative) NPVs unless they can explain them economically.

Strategic planning may serve to implement this check. Strategic analyses look for market opportunities (deviations from equilibrium) and try to identify the firm's competitive advantages. Turn the logic around: strategic analysis that does not explicitly compute NPVs shows "absolute faith in Adam Smith's invisible hand." If a firm finds a favorable deviation from long-run equilibrium, or identifies a competitive advantage, then efficient investment in that line must offer profits exceeding the opportunity cost of capital. The manager "knows" the NPV is positive without calculating it.

The trouble: strategic analyses are also subject to random error. Mistakes are made in identifying competitive advantages or out-of-equilibrium markets. Therefore, strategic analysts should calculate NPVs explicitly, at least as a check, and strategic analysis and financial analysis ought to be explicitly reconciled. Few firms attempt this, which suggests the gap is more than just "two cultures and one problem."

Zero-NPV Equilibrium Condition
\[ NPV = 0 \quad \text{in long-run competitive equilibrium} \]

Meaning: In a perfectly competitive market, competition eliminates economic rents over time. Thus, any project's NPV tends toward zero in long-run equilibrium. A positive NPV must be traceable to a temporary disequilibrium (market opportunity) or a durable competitive advantage.

Practical use: This condition provides an intellectual discipline for managers: before accepting a positive NPV, identify the economic source of value; before rejecting a negative NPV, consider whether a competitive advantage or disequilibrium justifies it.

Assumption/caution: The condition is a long-run equilibrium concept. In the short run, deviations occur and can persist for years in imperfectly competitive markets.

Example: Explaining a Positive NPV

A firm identifies a new market niche with high projected returns. The DCF analysis produces a positive NPV. The smart manager asks: "Why is this project positive NPV? What barrier to entry protects it, or what temporary imbalance in supply and demand created this opportunity?" If no answer is available, the positive NPV may be an artifact of forecasting error.

Interpretation: Positive NPV must be economically explainable, not just numerically computed.

Practical lesson: Use strategic analysis (competitive advantage, market disequilibrium) as a validation test for financial analysis, and vice versa.

Analogy: The Card Counter's Discipline

A skilled card counter at a blackjack table does not bet large simply because a computer model says the odds have shifted. He must also understand why the odds shifted—which cards have been played. Without that understanding, his model may be responding to noise, not signal. Similarly, NPVs must be explained economically; otherwise they may be numerical noise.

Key Insight

The zero-NPV equilibrium condition links finance and strategy: it tells managers that positive NPV must be justified by a competitive advantage or a short-run deviation from equilibrium—concepts that strategic analysis is designed to identify. Financial and strategic analyses are therefore complementary, not rival, tools. Each serves as a check on the other.

Key Takeaways

  • DCF is subject to random error; borderline projects have a 50% chance of showing positive NPV.
  • Long-run competitive equilibrium implies zero NPV for all projects.
  • Positive NPVs must be explained by competitive advantage or disequilibrium.
  • Strategic analysis and financial analysis should be explicitly reconciled as complementary checks.

5. Misuse of Finance Theory in Practice

The gap between strategic and financial analysis may reflect misapplication of finance theory. Some firms do not attempt to use theory for strategic investments; others try but make systematic mistakes. Myers identifies several common errors, each creating a bias against long-lived, capital-intensive projects—precisely the projects strategic planning should care about most.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR) Ranking Error
\[ IRR_A > IRR_B \quad \nRightarrow \quad NPV_A > NPV_B \]

Symbol explanation: IRR is the discount rate that makes the project's NPV equal to zero. The notation indicates that Project A having a higher IRR than Project B does not imply Project A has a higher NPV.

Interpretation: Competing projects are often ranked by IRR rather than NPV. It is easier to earn a high percentage IR on a short-lived, small project than on a long-lived, capital-intensive one. Long-lived projects tend to be placed lower on the list even when their NPV is substantial.

Practical use: Firms should rank by NPV or by NPV per dollar invested when capital is rationed, not by IRR alone.

Assumption/caution: IRR fails when cash flows change sign more than once (multiple IRRs) and when projects differ in scale or timing. Even with a strictly limited pool of capital, NPV per dollar invested, or linear programming techniques (when capital is rationed in more than one period), should be used.

Inconsistent treatment of inflation: A surprising number of firms treat inflation inconsistently in DCF calculations. High nominal discount rates are used, but cash flows are not fully adjusted for future inflation. Thus accelerating inflation makes projects—especially long-lived ones—look less attractive even if their real value is unaffected.

Unrealistically high discount rates: Some firms use discount rates that are too high even after proper inflation adjustment. This may reflect ignorance of normal capital market returns. Specific errors include:

  • Premiums added for risks that can easily be diversified away in stockholders' portfolios.
  • Rates raised to offset optimistic biases of project sponsors. This adjustment works only if the bias increases geometrically with the forecast period; if it does not, long-lived projects are penalized.
  • Some projects are unusually risky at inception but only of normal risk once start-up is successfully passed. It is easy to classify them as "high-risk" and add a start-up risk premium to the discount rate for all future cash flows. The premium should apply only to the start-up period; applied after that, safe short-lived projects are artificially favored.

Organizational problems in capital budgeting: Capital budgeting is usually a bottom-up process. Proposals originate in the organization's midriff and survive a trip to the top, gaining approval at each stage. Political alliances form, cash flow forecasts are bent to meet known standards, and answers (not necessarily the right ones) are worked out for anticipated challenges. Most projects reaching the top appear to meet management's profitability standards.

Brealey and Myers's Second Law
\[ \text{Pr(positive NPV } | \text{ project reaches top)} = \text{constant} \]

The Second Law is stated by Brealey and Myers as: "The proportion of proposed projects having positive NPV is independent of top management's estimate of the opportunity cost of capital."

Interpretation: This witty "law" captures how forecast manipulation in bottom-up processes can make project acceptance probabilities independent of the discount rate hurdle. Project sponsors learn what numbers are needed and supply them.

Practical use: Top management should be skeptical of project-level NPV forecasts, particularly when the same forecasts have passed through multiple layers of organizational approval. Independent verification and incentive alignment are necessary.

Example: The Start-Up Risk Premium Mistake

A biotechnology project is highly uncertain during its two-year clinical trial phase but would face normal commercial risk once approved. Management classifies the entire project as "high risk" and applies a 25% discount rate to all future cash flows. This overstates risk for cash flows beyond year 2 and unfairly penalizes the project compared to a short-lived project with the same early-stage risk.

Interpretation: The risk premium should be applied to the start-up period only.

Practical lesson: Apply risk-adjusted discounting only to the phase of the project where the risk actually exists.

Analogy: The Last Mile Problem

Suppose you must drive a mountain pass with one treacherous stretch of icy road. Applying a "mountain driving" speed limit to the entire 500-mile trip would make you absurdly slow on the safe highways beyond the pass. Similarly, applying a start-up risk premium to all future cash flows penalizes long-lived projects for early-stage risk they will not bear.

Key Insight

Many "failures" of DCF in strategic applications are actually failures of implementation—ranking by IRR, inconsistent inflation treatment, and excessive discount rates that bias against long-lived projects. Fixing these implementation errors is necessary before concluding that DCF is intrinsically inadequate for strategy.

Key Takeaways

  • IRR ranking can mislead; use NPV or NPV per dollar when capital is rationed.
  • Inflation must be treated consistently in discount rates and cash flows.
  • High discount rates that include diversifiable-risk premiums or unlimited optimism offsets penalize long-lived projects.
  • Start-up risk premiums should apply only to the start-up period.
  • Organizational gaming can make NPV forecasts unreliable.

6. Four Difficulties in Applying DCF to Strategy

Now consider a firm that understands finance theory, applies DCF correctly, and has overcome the human and organizational problems that bias cash flows and discount rates. Even then, Myers argues, an intelligent application of DCF will encounter four chief problems in strategic settings:

  1. Estimating the discount rate.
  2. Estimating the project's future cash flows.
  3. Estimating the project's impact on the firm's other assets' cash flows (cross-sectional links between projects).
  4. Estimating the project's impact on the firm's future investment opportunities (time-series links between projects).

The first three are difficult but not fatal; the fourth is the most serious for financial strategy.

6.1 Estimating the Opportunity Cost of Capital

The opportunity cost of capital is difficult to measure because it is an expected rate of return—an expectation of investors, which cannot be directly observed. However, extensive evidence exists on past average rates of return in capital markets and the corporate sector; no long-run trends in "normal" rates are evident. Reasonable ballpark estimates can be obtained if obvious traps (improper risk or inflation adjustments) are avoided. Myers states: "Estimating cash flows properly is more important than fine-tuning the discount rate."

6.2 Forecasting Cash Flow

It is impossible to forecast most projects' actual cash flows accurately. DCF calculations do not require perfectly accurate forecasts, however—they require accurate assessments of the mean of possible outcomes. Operating managers can make reasonable subjective forecasts of operating variables they are responsible for (operating costs, market growth, market share), but find it difficult to translate this knowledge into a cash flow forecast for year seven. Difficulties arise because:

  • The operating manager is asked to look into a distant future he is not accustomed to considering.
  • He is asked to express the forecast in accounting variables rather than operating variables.
  • Incorporating macroeconomic forecasts is difficult.

As a result, long-run forecasts often become mechanical extrapolations of short-run trends, overlooking long-run competitive, inflationary, and technological pressures. A better framework is needed to help operating managers apply practical knowledge and to explicitly incorporate macroeconomic trends. But ultimately, forecasting is intrinsically difficult.

6.3 Cross-Sectional Relationships Between Cash Flows

Tracing cross-sectional relationships between project cash flows is also intrinsically difficult. The problem may be worsened by inappropriate project definitions or boundaries for lines of business. Defining business units properly is one of the "tricks" of successful strategic planning. These problems are faced by strategic planners regardless of whether they use financial theory; they are not flaws in the theory itself. Therefore, they do not explain the gap between finance theory and strategic planning.

6.4 Time-Series Links Between Projects

The fourth problem—the link between today's investments and tomorrow's opportunities—is much more difficult. Suppose a firm invests in a negative NPV project in order to establish a foothold in an attractive market. A valuable second-stage investment is used to justify the immediate project. The second stage must depend on the first: if the firm could take the second project without having taken the first, the future opportunity should have no impact on the immediate decision. However, if tomorrow's opportunities depend on today's decisions, there is a time-series link between projects.

At first glance, this may appear to be just another forecasting problem: estimate cash flows for both stages and calculate the NPV for the two-stage combined project. But you would not get the right answer. The second stage is an option, and conventional DCF does not value options properly.

Stage 1 Investment as an Option
\[ \text{Stage 1 Value} = NPV_1 + \text{Call Option Value on Stage 2} \]

Symbol explanation: NPV₁ is the net present value of the first-stage project taken alone. The call option value on Stage 2 represents the value of the right—but not the obligation—to undertake the second-stage investment later.

Interpretation: The firm is not committed to undertake Stage 2. It will go ahead if Stage 1 works and the market is still attractive. If Stage 1 fails or the market sours, the firm can stop and cut its losses. Investing in Stage 1 purchases an intangible asset: a call option on Stage 2.

Practical use: If the option's present value offsets the first stage's negative NPV, the first stage is justified.

Assumption/caution: This logic applies only when the second-stage opportunity is contingent on the first-stage investment. If Stage 2 is available independently of Stage 1, the option value should not affect the Stage 1 decision.

Example: Foothold Investment

A pharmaceutical firm invests in a small research unit (Stage 1) with a negative NPV of -$5 million, because the research may produce a patentable drug (Stage 2) worth $100 million if successful. The firm is not obligated to develop the drug; it holds an option. If the research fails or the market changes, it abandons the project.

Interpretation: The Stage 1 investment's total value = -$5 million + option value. If the option value exceeds $5 million, the investment is justified.

Practical lesson: Conventional DCF that ignores the option value would reject the project; a correct strategic analysis must include the option.

Analogy: The Reservation Deposit

Paying a refundable deposit for the right to buy a house later is like buying an option. You lose the deposit if you do not buy, but you gain the right to buy if the house appreciates or your circumstances change. The deposit's value is not just the eventual purchase price—it is the value of the right to choose. Similarly, many strategic investments purchase the right to choose future investments.

Key Insight

The time-series link between projects is the most important element of financial strategy. When tomorrow's opportunities depend on today's decisions, the firm holds a real option, and conventional DCF—which assumes the firm is committed to a fixed course of action—systematically undervalues such investments.

Key Takeaways

  • Estimating the discount rate is difficult but manageable with capital market data.
  • Cash flow forecasting is intrinsically difficult; focus on the mean of possible outcomes.
  • Cross-sectional links are a project-definition problem, not a theory flaw.
  • Time-series links create real options that standard DCF cannot value.

7. Real Options and the Limits of DCF

Myers explains the limits of DCF by considering its application to four types of securities:

  1. Fixed-income securities (bonds, preferred stock): DCF is standard and works well.
  2. Relatively safe stocks paying regular dividends: DCF is sensible and widely used.
  3. Companies with significant growth opportunities: DCF is less helpful. The model can be "stretched" to say that Apple Computer's stock price equals the present value of dividends the firm may eventually pay, but a more informative decomposition is needed.
  4. Traded options (calls and puts): DCF is never used; option valuation formulas look nothing like DCF.
Stock Price Decomposition with PVGO
\[ P_0 = \frac{EPS}{r} + PVGO \]

Symbol explanation:

  • P₀ = current stock price.
  • EPS = normalized current earnings per share.
  • r = opportunity cost of capital.
  • PVGO = net present value of future growth opportunities.

Mathematical relationship: The first term, EPS/r, is the value of the firm as a "cash cow"—the present value of a perpetuity of current earnings. The second term, PVGO, captures the additional value created by future investments.

Interpretation: PVGO is the present value of a portfolio of options—the firm's options to invest in second-stage, third-stage, or even later projects. For a firm like Apple Computer in 1984, PVGO accounts for a substantial fraction of total market value.

Practical use: This decomposition alerts managers to the idea that for growth firms, value is largely option value, not the present value of cash flows from assets-in-place.

Assumption/caution: EPS must be "normalized" (not temporarily distorted). The formula presumes the source is a stable, ongoing business with identifiable growth opportunities.

Corporate analogs to the four security types:

  1. Safe flows (e.g., cash flows from financial leases): Few problems using DCF.
  2. Cash cows—relatively safe businesses held for cash generation rather than strategic value: DCF applies readily. It also works for "engineering investments" such as machine replacements, where the main benefit is reduced cost in a clearly defined activity.
  3. Businesses with substantial growth opportunities or intangible assets: DCF is less helpful; this is where options account for a large fraction of value.
  4. Pure research and development: DCF is no help at all. The value of R&D is almost all option value.

The theory of option valuation has been worked out in detail for securities—puts, calls, warrants, convertibles, and bond call options. The same solution techniques should apply to the real options held by firms. Myers cites two preliminary applications:

  • Offshore oil/gas exploration leases: The option value comes from the lessee's right to delay drilling and development decisions until after observing reserve extent and future oil prices.
  • Abandonment value: An active second-hand market increases an asset's value because it gives the owner a put option to bail out of a poorly performing project.

These real options last longer and are more complex than traded calls and puts. The terms must be extracted from the economics of the problem, and realistic descriptions usually require numerical methods for valuation. Nevertheless, option pricing methods hold great promise for strategic analysis because the time-series links between projects are the most important part of financial strategy.

Example: Offshore Lease Valuation

In valuing a federal lease for offshore oil or gas exploration, the lessee can delay drilling and development decisions until after observing the actual extent of reserves and the future level of oil prices. This right to delay and decide sequentially is an option. Standard DCF—which requires assuming one specific production schedule—cannot properly capture the value of this flexibility.

Interpretation: The option value arises from the ability to adapt decisions to information revealed over time.

Practical lesson: When strategic investments include flexibility (delay, expand, abandon, switch), option valuation methods are necessary.

Analogy: Real Estate Option

Buying land in a developing area is like buying a call option on future development opportunities. The land's value is not only the current rental income—it is the right to build later if the neighborhood appreciates. Standard real estate appraisal that ignores development potential undervalues the land, just as DCF undervalues growth companies.

Key Insight

When a large fraction of a business's value comes from growth opportunities or intangible assets, that value is primarily option value. DCF, which values a fixed stream of cash flows, cannot capture the value of the firm's ability to adapt, expand, delay, or abandon investments as information unfolds.

Key Takeaways

  • DCF works well for bonds, safe dividend stocks, cash cows, and engineering investments.
  • DCF fails for options, growth businesses, R&D, and intangible assets.
  • P₀ = EPS/r + PVGO: growth value is option value.
  • Real option applications (offshore leases, abandonment value) demonstrate how option pricing can be applied to corporate assets.

8. Lessons for Corporate Strategy

Myers draws an explicit lesson for corporate strategy: when time-series links between projects are important, strategy should be viewed as managing the firm's portfolio of real options. The financial planning process may be thought of as three tasks:

  1. Acquiring options—either by investing directly in R&D, product design, cost or quality improvements, or as a by-product of direct capital investment (e.g., investing in a Stage 1 project with negative NPV in order to open the door for Stage 2).
  2. Abandoning options that are too far "out of the money" to justify the carrying cost.
  3. Exercising valuable options at the right time—that is, buying the cash-producing assets that ultimately yield positive net present value.

This perspective reframes strategy from a static plan to a dynamic process of creating, maintaining, and exercising flexibility.

There is also a lesson for current applications of finance theory to strategy. Several new approaches to financial strategy use a simple, traditional DCF model of the firm. These approaches are likely to be more useful for cash cows than for growth businesses with substantial risk and intangible assets. The option value of growth and intangibles is not ignored by good managers even when conventional financial techniques miss them; these values are often brought in as "strategic factors" dressed in non-financial clothes. Dealing with time-series links between capital investments—and the option value these links create—is often left to strategic planners.

Example: Acquiring, Abandoning, and Exercising Options

Acquire: A firm invests in a pilot production line with negative standalone NPV to gain manufacturing expertise that opens the door to a future product family.

Abandon: The firm discontinues an R&D program when early results suggest the underlying technology will not reach commercial viability.

Exercise: After a successful trial launch, the firm commits to full-scale production because market conditions remain favorable.

Interpretation: Each decision is a real option action.

Practical lesson: The firm's strategy is the portfolio of these options and the discipline to manage them.

Analogy: A Venture Capital Portfolio

A venture capital firm does not fund every start-up to completion. It provides small initial sums (acquiring options), stops funding failures early (abandoning options), and puts large follow-on capital into winners (exercising options). The VC's returns come from managing a portfolio of options, not from treating each investment as a standalone, irreversible commitment.

Key Insight

Strategic planning should be understood as the management of a portfolio of real options: acquiring new options, abandoning those that no longer justify their carrying costs, and exercising valuable options at the right time. This framework unifies finance theory and strategic analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Corporate strategy = managing the firm's portfolio of real options.
  • Three tasks: acquire options, abandon out-of-the-money options, exercise valuable options.
  • Simple DCF-based strategic models work for cash cows but understate value for growth businesses.
  • The option value of growth and intangibles is often treated as "strategic factors" in non-financial language.

9. Bridging the Gap

Myers summarizes by asking how the present gap between finance theory and strategic planning might be bridged. His answer is two-sided:

Strategic planning needs finance. Present value calculations are needed as a check on strategic analysis, and vice versa. However, standard DCF techniques tend to understate the option value attached to growing, profitable lines of business. Corporate finance theory requires extension to deal with real options.

On the financial side, two tasks are required:

  1. Apply existing finance theory correctly. Avoid IRR ranking, inflation inconsistency, unrealistic discount rates, and misapplied risk premiums.
  2. Extend the theory. The most promising line of research is to use option pricing theory to model the time-series interactions between investments.

Both sides should make a conscious effort to reconcile financial and strategic analysis. Although complete reconciliation will rarely be possible, the attempt should uncover hidden assumptions and bring a generally deeper understanding of strategic choices. The gap may remain, but with better analysis on either side of it.

For managers: Use DCF rigorously for what it is good at (cash cows, engineering investments, safe flows) and recognize that for growth businesses, intangible assets, and R&D, a significant portion of value is option value that DCF will understate. Use strategic analysis as a check on DCF (via competitive advantage and equilibrium logic) and use DCF as a check on strategic intuition. For theorists: The most important frontier is the application of option pricing theory to real assets. The preliminary applications (offshore leases, abandonment value) demonstrate feasibility. The challenge is adapting option models to complex, long-horizon real-world contracts that require numerical methods.

Example: Applying Both Tools

A firm considering entry into a new geographic market faces two valuation layers. First, DCF analysis of the expected cash flows from entering today yields a modest positive NPV. Second, option analysis reveals that the entry creates a platform for follow-on products and for abandoning the market if conditions worsen. The total strategic value = DCF value + option value.

Interpretation: DCF alone understates value when flexibility exists; option analysis captures the additional value.

Practical lesson: Present both DCF and option value explicitly in strategic investment proposals.

Analogy: Two Instruments Needed for Navigation

A pilot uses both radar (for current conditions) and a flight plan (for strategic route). Radar alone cannot tell you whether the destination is right; the flight plan alone cannot avoid a mountain in the path. Finance theory and strategic planning are two instruments that should be read together, each correcting the other's blind spots.

Key Insight

Bridging the gap requires both the correct application of existing finance theory and an extension of that theory through option pricing. Neither finance nor strategy alone is sufficient; each must serve as a check on the other.

Key Takeaways

  • Apply existing finance theory correctly before blaming the theory.
  • Extend finance theory using option pricing to model time-series links between investments.
  • Both financial and strategic analyses should be explicitly reconciled to uncover hidden assumptions.
  • Complete reconciliation is rare; but deeper understanding is attainable.

10. Practical Cautions and Common Errors

Myers's article provides an unusually rich catalog of practical errors that managers (and theorists) make when applying finance theory. The following cautions synthesize the source's warnings, organized by theme, to help readers avoid the book's most common mistakes.

1. Do not rank on IRR. An investment's rate of return says nothing about the absolute value created. Long-lived, capital-intensive projects with substantial NPV can have modest IRRs and be incorrectly rejected if ranked against small, quick-payback projects. If capital is genuinely rationed, use NPV per dollar invested or linear programming—not IRR.

2. Treat inflation consistently. If nominal discount rates are used, cash flows must be forecast in nominal terms (including inflation). Mixing real cash flows with nominal discount rates systematically penalizes long-lived projects.

3. Do not use unrealistically high discount rates. Diversifiable risk does not warrant a premium; an optimism-adjustment premium only works if forecast bias grows geometrically with forecast horizon (which is implausible); and start-up risk premiums should apply only to the start-up period, not to all future cash flows.

4. Distinguish accounting from value. Book rate of return and EPS impact are not measures of value creation. Investors see through accounting biases; stock prices reflect cash flow fundamentals and growth opportunities.

5. Do not assume diversification creates value. Investors can diversify their own portfolios cheaply. Corporate diversification does not reduce the cost of capital, and closed-end fund discounts and conglomerate discounts are empirical evidence that diversification is not valued.

6. Do not confuse DCF's arithmetic with economic explanation. A positive NPV must be justified by a short-run deviation from equilibrium or a competitive advantage. Strategic analysis provides this justification; DCF alone does not.
