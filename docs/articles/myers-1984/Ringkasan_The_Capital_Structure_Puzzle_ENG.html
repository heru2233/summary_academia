1. Research Question and Background

Myers (1984) poses a foundational question at the intersection of corporate finance and strategic management: why has modern finance theory had so little impact on strategic planning, despite major advances in understanding how capital markets work and how risky real and financial assets are valued? The paper is not merely an academic exercise; its purpose is to identify the causes of this gap and to propose how it might be closed, both by applying existing theory correctly and by extending theory to handle strategic problems it currently cannot address.

Strategic planning, as defined in the article, is "the process of deciding how to commit the firm's resources across lines of business." The financial side of strategic planning allocates a particular resource: capital. Thus, strategic planning and finance theory are naturally connected: any strategic commitment of capital implies an implicit estimate of whether the value of the firm will increase by more than the amount of capital committed. Finance theory, which explains how real and financial assets are valued, should therefore have direct application to the financial side of strategic planning.

Yet in practice, finance theory and strategic planning operate in separate spheres. Projects with low net present value (NPV) are nurtured "for strategic reasons," while projects with apparently high NPV are rejected if they do not fit the firm's strategic objectives. When financial and strategic analyses conflict, the conflict is treated as a fact of life rather than an anomaly demanding reconciliation. The paper offers three candidate explanations for this gap:

Explanation 1: Two cultures. Finance theory and strategic planning may be separated by differences in language and "culture." If this is the problem, better communication could bridge the gap.

Explanation 2: Misuse of finance theory. Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis may have been misapplied in strategic contexts and, consequently, is not accepted. If this is the problem, correcting practice would help.

Explanation 3: Intrinsic failure of DCF. DCF may fail in strategic applications even when properly applied, because it cannot value the real options created by time-series links between investments. If this is the problem, finance theory itself must be extended.

Myers is explicit that each explanation is partly true, and the three taken together do not necessarily add up to the whole truth. But the third explanation receives the most sustained analytical attention and is the paper's most original contribution: conventional discounted cash flow analysis does not correctly value growth opportunities and intangible assets, because these are fundamentally option values.

Example

Model/result: A firm nurtures a low-NPV project "for strategic reasons." Its strategic analysis identifies a market opportunity or competitive advantage and concludes the project must be valuable, overriding the NPV calculation. Interpretation: The strategic analysis is implicitly claiming that the project creates an option or growth opportunity that the DCF calculation missed. Practical lesson: The conflict between finance and strategy is not necessarily a sign that one side is wrong; it may indicate that the financial analysis failed to capture option value, or that the strategic analysis rests on an unverified assumption about future opportunities.

Analogy

Finance theory and strategic planning resemble two cultures looking at the same mountain from opposite sides. Each sees a different landscape, speaks a different language, and produces maps that look incompatible. Yet both are mapping the same terrain: the firm's future value. Until they share a common framework, each side will continue to produce plans and valuations that cannot be reconciled with the other.

Key Insight

The gap between finance theory and strategic planning is not merely a communication problem; it reflects both misapplication of valid theory and genuine limitations of that theory when applied to strategic choices involving growth options. The resolution requires correcting practice and extending theory, with option pricing theory as the most promising extension.

Key Takeaways

  • Strategic planning is fundamentally about committing the firm's capital across lines of business, which implies an implicit NPV estimate for every strategic investment.
  • Finance theory has advanced greatly but has had scant impact on strategic planning.
  • Three explanations exist: cultural/linguistic differences, misuse of DCF, and intrinsic failure of DCF to value options.
  • All three explanations are partly true; the paper argues that the option-valuation limitation is the deepest problem.

2. Core Finance Theory: Net Present Value and Discounted Cash Flow

To define what is meant by "finance theory," Myers sketches the concepts most relevant to capital investment decisions. The core idea is to treat each investment project as a mini-firm: an all-equity-financed enterprise whose stock could, hypothetically, be actively traded. If we know what the mini-firm's stock would sell for, we know its present value, and therefore the project's present value. Net present value (NPV) is obtained by subtracting the required investment from this present value.

This approach directs managers to use the valuation model that best explains the prices of similar securities. In practice, the theory is typically compressed into a single model: discounted cash flow (DCF). The central equation is presented below.

Discounted Cash Flow Formula
\[ PV = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

Symbols and their meaning:

  • PV = present (market) value of the project or asset.
  • Ct = forecasted incremental cash flow after corporate taxes in period t. Strictly speaking, this is the mean of the distribution of possible cash flows, not a single best-guess or conservative estimate. The expected value matters because investors price assets based on expected outcomes, not worst-case or optimistic scenarios.
  • T = project life; CT includes any salvage value at the end of the project.
  • r = the opportunity cost of capital, defined as the equilibrium expected rate of return on securities equivalent in risk to the project being valued. It is the rate of return investors could earn elsewhere by taking on the same level of risk.

Mathematical relationship: The present value is a sum of expected future cash flows, each discounted at the opportunity cost of capital. The further in the future a cash flow occurs (larger t), the less it contributes to present value for any positive r. Higher risk projects have higher r, reducing present value for any given expected cash flow. NPV equals PV minus the cash outlay required at t = 0.

Interpretation in plain terms: A project is worth the amount an investor would pay today for the stream of cash flows it is expected to generate, given comparable risk. The firm should undertake a project when its present value exceeds its cost, i.e., when NPV > 0.

Practical use: Since present values add, the value of the firm equals the sum of the values of all its mini-firms. If DCF works for each project separately, it should work for any collection of projects, a line of business, or the firm as a whole. In practice, firms group projects into risk classes and use the same cost of capital for all projects in a class. The opportunity cost of capital for a line of business or the firm is a value-weighted average of the costs of capital of its constituent projects.

Important assumptions and cautions: The opportunity cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source. In most cases, financing has a second-order impact on value: "You can make much more money through smart investment decisions than smart financing decisions." Any advantage from departing from all-equity financing is typically adjusted for through a somewhat lowered discount rate. Also, finance theory stresses cash flow and fundamentals; it should not be deflected by accounting allocations, except to the extent they affect cash taxes. The firm's investment opportunities compete with securities that stockholders can buy directly; investors will invest in the firm only if it can do better, risk considered, than they can do on their own.

Example

Model/result: Suppose a positive-NPV project sharply reduces book earnings in its early years. Finance theory recommends forging ahead, trusting investors to see through the accounting bias to the project's true value. Interpretation: Book earnings are an accounting construct; what matters for value is the incremental cash flow stream and its risk. Practical lesson: Empirical evidence indicates investors do see through accounting biases; if they did not, all stocks would sell at the same price-earnings ratio. Managers should base investment decisions on expected cash flows and the opportunity cost of capital, not on near-term accounting results.

Analogy

Think of each project as an apple tree. The DCF formula asks: what would people pay today for this tree, given the expected harvest of apples (cash flows) each year, the risk that frost or pests might spoil the crop, and the returns available from planting other trees (competing investments)? The value of the tree is not determined by how the apples are counted in the farmer's ledger (accounting), but by the actual expected apples and their timing and risk.

Key Insight

DCF is a bottom-up, project-by-project valuation tool built on value additivity. It defines the opportunity cost of capital by the risk of the project's cash flows, not by who supplies the financing or what the accounting statements say. When applied correctly, it should not bias decisions against long-lived projects or in favor of accounting results.

Key Takeaways

  • Each project is a mini-firm; its present value is what its stock would sell for if actively traded.
  • NPV = present value of expected incremental after-tax cash flows minus the required investment.
  • The discount rate r is the opportunity cost of capital: the equilibrium expected return on securities of equivalent risk.
  • Value additivity means DCF works for collections of projects, lines of business, and the whole firm.
  • Financing is second-order; the opportunity cost of capital depends on the use of funds, not the source.

3. The Gap Between Finance Theory and Strategic Planning

Myers resists describing strategic planning as "capital budgeting on a grand scale." The reason is that capital budgeting in practice is a bottom-up process: proposals originate throughout the organization and are aggregated upward. The aim is to find and undertake specific assets or projects that are worth more than they cost. But picking valuable pieces does not ensure maximum value for the whole. Piecemeal, bottom-up capital budgeting is not strategic planning.

Nevertheless, capital budgeting techniques ought to work for the whole as well as the parts. A strategic commitment of capital to a line of business is itself an investment project. If management invests, it must believe the value of the firm increases by more than the amount of capital committed; otherwise it is throwing money away. Therefore, every strategic decision contains an implicit NPV estimate, whether or not it is calculated explicitly.

Despite this link, most strategic planners are not guided by the tools of modern finance. The article documents several symptoms of the gap:

  • Preoccupation with accounting variables: Many managers worry about a strategic decision's impact on book rate of return or earnings per share. If they are convinced the plan adds to firm value, its impact on accounting figures should be irrelevant.
  • Corporate diversification to reduce risk: Some managers pursue diversification to reduce risk as they see it. But in capital markets, diversification is cheap and easy; investors who want to diversify do so on their own. Corporate diversification is redundant, and the market will not pay extra for it. If it would, closed-end funds would sell at premiums over net asset value, and conglomerate firms would be worth more to investors than their components traded separately. In reality, closed-end funds sell at discounts, and conglomerates appear to sell at discounts too.
  • The "fixed capital stock" fallacy: Some strategic planning literature views firms as having a fixed stock of capital, so "cash cows" are needed to finance investment in rapidly growing lines of business. In fact, the firms that pioneered strategic planning had easy access to capital markets, as do almost all public companies. Firms may not like the price they pay for capital, but that price is the opportunity cost of capital — the proper standard for new investment.

Myers also introduces an intellectual bridge between the two cultures. Smart managers apply a check to DCF results: because all projects have zero NPV in long-run competitive equilibrium, a positive NPV must be explained either by a short-run deviation from equilibrium or by some permanent competitive advantage. If neither explanation applies, the positive NPV is suspect. Conversely, a negative NPV is suspect if a competitive advantage or short-run deviation from equilibrium favors the project. Strategic analysis can implement this check by looking for market opportunities (deviations from equilibrium) and identifying competitive advantages.

Turned around, strategic analysis that does not explicitly compute NPVs shows "absolute faith in Adam Smith's invisible hand": if a firm finds a favorable deviation from equilibrium or identifies a competitive advantage, then efficient investment in that line must offer profits exceeding the opportunity cost of capital. No NPV calculation is needed. The trouble is that strategic analyses are also subject to random error. Mistakes are made in identifying competitive advantages or out-of-equilibrium markets. Therefore, strategic analysts should calculate NPVs explicitly, at least as a check; strategic and financial analyses ought to be explicitly reconciled. Few firms attempt this, which suggests the gap is more than just "two cultures and one problem."

Example

Model/result: A firm identifies a growing market and a proprietary technology that gives it a cost advantage. Strategic analysis concludes the market entry is attractive. Financial analysis is not performed. Interpretation: The strategic analysis implicitly assumes the competitive advantage translates into cash flows whose present value exceeds the investment cost. But if the strategic diagnosis is wrong — if the advantage is illusory or the market equilibrium adjusts faster than expected — the investment will destroy value. Practical lesson: NPV should be calculated explicitly to discipline strategic judgment; both strategic and financial analyses are error-prone, so each should be used as a check on the other.

Analogy

Imagine a navigator who refuses to use a compass, insisting that he can read the stars. When the sky is clear, his judgment is excellent. But on an overcast night, his errors compound. Strategic analysis without NPV is like navigating by the stars alone: powerful when the economic logic is transparent, but unreliable when conditions are uncertain. The compass of DCF, even if imperfect, provides a check that prevents gross navigational errors.

Key Insight

In long-run competitive equilibrium, all projects earn zero NPV. Therefore, a positive NPV must be justified by a short-run deviation from equilibrium or a permanent competitive advantage — and a negative NPV is suspect when such an advantage exists. This logical bridge connects strategic analysis (which identifies advantages and opportunities) with financial analysis (which quantifies their value). The two disciplines should be complementary checks on each other, not competing frameworks.

Key Takeaways

  • Strategic planning is not simply "capital budgeting on a grand scale"; it is a top-down process that piecemeal capital budgeting cannot replicate.
  • Every strategic investment decision contains an implicit NPV estimate.
  • Common strategic concerns — book earnings, diversification, fixed capital constraints — are largely irrelevant from a finance theory perspective.
  • Positive NPV must be explainable by disequilibrium or competitive advantage; strategic analysis can provide this explanation.
  • Both methods are subject to error, so explicit reconciliation is needed — yet few firms attempt it.

4. Misuse of Finance Theory in Practice

A second explanation for the gap is that finance theory is frequently misapplied. Some firms do not try to use theory at all; others try but make systematic errors. These errors create a real bias against long-lived, capital-intensive projects — the very projects that strategic planning is often most concerned with. The misapplications are not harmless technicalities; "departing from theoretically-correct valuation procedures often sacrifices the long-run health of the firm for the short, and makes capital investment choices arbitrary or unpredictable." Over time, the sacrifices appear as disappointing growth, eroding market share, and loss of technological leadership.

Four classes of misuse are identified.

4.1 Ranking on Internal Rate of Return (IRR). Competing projects are often ranked by internal rate of return rather than NPV. The IRR measures "bang per buck" on a DCF basis, and firms may prefer it because they believe capital is limited. But most large firms have free access to capital markets at the opportunity cost of capital; internal limits on capital expenditure are set to control an organization too eager to spend money, not because funds are physically unavailable. Using IRR to rank projects is biased: it is easier to earn a high rate of return if project life is short and investment is small. Long-lived, capital-intensive projects tend to be pushed down the list even when their NPV is substantial. The theoretically correct approach under capital rationing is to rank by NPV per dollar invested, or to use linear programming when capital is rationed in more than one period.

4.2 Inconsistent treatment of inflation. A surprising number of firms use high nominal discount rates while failing to adjust cash flow forecasts fully for future inflation. This inconsistency makes projects — especially long-lived ones — look less attractive when inflation accelerates, even though their real value is unchanged. Correct practice requires either real cash flows discounted at real rates, or nominal cash flows discounted at nominal rates; mixing the two distorts project ranking.

4.3 Unrealistically high discount rates. Even after proper inflation adjustment, some firms use discount rates that are too high. This reflects ignorance of normal capital market returns and several specific mistakes:

  • Premiums are added for risks that can easily be diversified away in stockholders' portfolios. Since investors can eliminate such risks at no cost, the market does not pay a premium for bearing them.
  • Rates are raised to offset the optimistic biases of managers sponsoring projects. This adjustment works only if the optimism bias increases geometrically with the forecast period. If it does not, long-lived projects are unfairly penalized.
  • Some projects are unusually risky at inception but normal-risk once the start-up is successfully passed. Classifying the whole project as "high-risk" and adding a start-up premium to all future cash flows artificially favors safe, short-lived projects. The risk premium should be applied to the start-up period only.

4.4 Organizational distortion of capital budgeting. Capital budgeting is a bottom-up process. Proposals originate in the organization's "midriff" and must survive the trip to the top, gaining approval at each stage. In the process, political alliances form, and cash flow forecasts are bent to meet known standards. Answers — not necessarily the right ones — are prepared for anticipated challenges. Most projects that reach the top seem to meet profitability standards. Brealey and Myers's Second Law summarizes the consequence: "The proportion of proposed projects having positive NPV is independent of top management's estimate of the opportunity cost of capital." In other words, when forecasts are flexible and organizational dynamics dominate, the discount rate becomes an ineffective screening device.

Finally, Myers suggests that top managers may not understand the logic of DCF deeply enough to trust it or use it without mistakes. They understand the arithmetic but not the underlying logic. The widespread use of unrealistically high discount rates and the distrust of stock market volatility are symptoms. Yet market volatility is "the natural result of a rational market," and it may be easier to underestimate the sophistication of the stock market than to accept its verdict on the firm's performance.

Example

Model/result: Two projects compete for funds. Project A has a short life, small capital outlay, and an IRR of 25%. Project B is long-lived, capital-intensive, with an IRR of 15% but a much higher NPV. The firm ranks by IRR and chooses A. Interpretation: Project A produces a high percentage return on a small base; Project B produces more total value because it deploys more capital over a longer period. Ranking by IRR ignores scale and timing. Practical lesson: Use NPV, or NPV per dollar invested when capital is genuinely rationed; IRR ranking systematically favors small, quick-payback projects and underinvests in long-term strategic capacity.

Analogy

Ranking projects by IRR alone is like choosing investments based on percentage yields without considering the amount invested. A savings account paying 5% on $1,000 yields $50 per year; a bond paying 3% on $100,000 yields $3,000. The percentage rate is informative only when combined with scale. IRR ranking mistakes the rate of return for the total wealth created.

Key Insight

Misapplied DCF creates a systematic bias against long-lived, capital-intensive projects: IRR ranking, inconsistent inflation treatment, and inflated discount rates all shorten the effective investment horizon. Correcting these errors is a necessary precondition for reconciling finance and strategy. But even perfect application of DCF will not solve the deeper problem — its inability to value real options.

Key Takeaways

  • IRR ranking is biased against long-lived, capital-intensive projects; use NPV or NPV per dollar invested under rationing.
  • Discount rates and cash flow forecasts must be consistent in their treatment of inflation.
  • Do not add premiums for diversifiable risk; investors can eliminate such risk on their own.
  • Raising the discount rate to offset optimism penalizes long-lived projects unless the bias grows geometrically with time.
  • Start-up risk premia should apply only to the start-up period, not to all future cash flows.
  • Brealey and Myers's Second Law: organizational dynamics can make the discount rate irrelevant as a screening device.

5. Four Intrinsic Problems in Applying DCF to Strategy

Even when finance theory is understood and applied correctly, and even when organizational biases are overcome, Myers argues that DCF analysis still faces four intrinsic problems in strategic applications. These are not mere implementation difficulties; they are structural limitations of the method. The four problems are:

Problem 1: Estimating the opportunity cost of capital. The discount rate r is an expected rate of return, which is inherently unobservable. One cannot conduct a survey to extract probability distributions from investors' minds. However, extensive evidence exists on past average rates of return in capital markets and in the corporate sector, and no long-run trends in "normal" rates of return are evident. Reasonable ballpark cost-of-capital estimates are obtainable if obvious traps (improper risk or inflation adjustments) are avoided. Myers's judgment: estimating cash flows properly is more important than fine-tuning the discount rate.

Problem 2: Forecasting future cash flows. DCF calculations do not require accurate forecasts of actual cash flows; they require accurate assessments of the mean of possible outcomes. Operating managers can often make reasonable subjective forecasts of operating variables — operating costs, market growth, market share — at least for the future they are actually responsible for. But translating this knowledge into a cash flow forecast for, say, year seven is difficult for several reasons: the manager is asked to think about a distant future unfamiliar to him; he is required to express the forecast in accounting rather than operating variables; and incorporating macroeconomic forecasts is hard. Consequently, long-run forecasts often become mechanical extrapolations of short-run trends, and the long-run pressures of competition, inflation, and technical change are overlooked. Myers notes this is intrinsically difficult — "especially when your boss is watching you do it" — but a better framework could help managers apply their practical knowledge.

Problem 3: Estimating cross-sectional relationships between cash flows. The cash flows of one project can affect those of other projects the firm already owns or will own, and tracing these cross-sectional links is intrinsically difficult. Inappropriate project definitions or boundaries for lines of business make it harder; defining business units properly is one of the tricks of successful strategic planning. However, this problem is not a flaw in finance theory: any approach to strategic analysis must confront the same interdependence between projects. Thus it does not explain the gap between finance and strategy.

Problem 4: The time-series link between today's investments and tomorrow's opportunities. This is the deepest problem. Suppose a firm invests in a negative-NPV project to establish a foothold in an attractive market; a valuable second-stage investment is used to justify the immediate project. The second stage must depend on the first: if the firm could take the second project without having taken the first, then the future opportunity should have no impact on the immediate decision. But if tomorrow's opportunities depend on today's decisions, there is a time-series link between projects.

Naively, one might expect to solve this by forecasting cash flows for both stages and computing the combined NPV. This would not give the right answer, because the second stage is an option, and conventional DCF does not value options properly. The firm is not committed to undertake the second stage. It will proceed if the first stage succeeds and the market remains attractive; if the first stage fails or the market sours, the firm can stop after Stage 1 and cut its losses. Investing in Stage 1 therefore purchases an intangible asset: a call option on Stage 2. If the option's present value offsets the first stage's negative NPV, the first stage is justified.

Problems 1 through 3 are difficult but tractable and not unique to finance theory. Problem 4 is qualitatively different: it identifies a class of value — option value — that DCF cannot capture, no matter how carefully it is applied.

Example

Model/result: A pharmaceutical firm invests in a development project with negative NPV based on the drug's expected commercial sales alone. The investment also creates know-how and regulatory experience that enable a second-generation product only if the first succeeds. Interpretation: The first project is Stage 1; the second-generation product is Stage 2, an option the firm obtains by investing in Stage 1. DCF analysis of Stage 1 alone shows negative NPV; DCF of both stages together also undervalues the combined investment because it treats Stage 2 as a commitment rather than a choice. Practical lesson: The value of the strategic option must be estimated separately — using option pricing logic, not DCF — and added to the Stage 1 NPV.

Analogy

Purchasing Stage 1 is like buying a lottery ticket, but a special kind: the ticket does not force you to claim the prize. If the winning number comes up and the prize is attractive, you claim it; if the number fails, you discard the ticket and lose only its purchase price. A standard DCF calculation, applied to both stages together, mistakenly assumes you must claim the prize regardless of the outcome. The option value of the ticket lies precisely in your right to choose after seeing the result.

Key Insight

The time-series link between projects creates option value, and conventional DCF does not value options properly. The first three problems — discount rate estimation, cash flow forecasting, and cross-sectional links — are difficult but not fundamental flaws in theory. The fourth problem is the critical limitation: it requires a new valuation technology.

Key Takeaways

  • Ballpark cost-of-capital estimates are achievable; cash flow estimation matters more than fine-tuning the discount rate.
  • DCF requires the mean of the distribution of future cash flows, which is difficult for operating managers to produce for distant periods.
  • Cross-sectional links between project cash flows are hard to measure but are not a fatal flaw — every method faces them.
  • The time-series link between investments creates real option value that DCF systematically misses.
  • A negative-NPV Stage 1 project can be justified if it purchases a call option on a valuable Stage 2, provided that option value exceeds the loss.

6. The Option Value of Future Investment Opportunities

Myers develops the option-based critique of DCF by examining its applicability across different types of assets. Think first of four types of securities:

  • Bonds, preferred stocks, and other fixed-income securities: DCF is the standard valuation approach. Cash flows are contractually specified, so discounting them at an appropriate rate is natural and reliable.
  • Relatively safe stocks paying regular dividends: DCF is sensible and widely used. The dividend stream is predictable enough that present value calculations are meaningful.
  • Companies with significant growth opportunities: DCF is not as helpful. One can stretch the model and say that Apple Computer's stock price equals the present value of the dividends it may eventually pay, but it is far more insightful to decompose the price differently (see the formula below).
  • Traded calls and puts: DCF is never used. Finance theory supplies option valuation formulas that work, but these formulas look nothing like DCF.

The corporate analogs map directly onto this spectrum. DCF is fine for safe cash flows such as financial leases; it works well for "cash cows" — relatively safe businesses held for the cash they generate — and for "engineering investments" like machine replacements, where the main benefit is reduced cost in a clearly defined activity. But DCF is less helpful in valuing businesses with substantial growth opportunities or intangible assets, and it is no help at all for pure research and development, whose value is almost entirely option value.

Stock Price Decomposition with Growth Opportunities
\[ P_0 = \frac{EPS}{r} + PVGO \]

Symbols and their meaning:

  • P0 = the firm's current stock price.
  • EPS = normalized current earnings per share — the earnings the firm would generate if it simply maintained its current operations.
  • r = the opportunity cost of capital for the firm's equity.
  • PVGO = the net present value of future growth opportunities.

Mathematical relationship: The first term, EPS/r, is the value of a level perpetuity equal to current normalized earnings: it is what the stock would be worth if the firm produced its current earnings forever and never grew. The second term, PVGO, is the additional value attributable to the firm's future investment opportunities. The equation is an identity that decomposes the stock price into "value of assets in place" plus "value of growth options."

Interpretation in plain terms: For growth firms, a large fraction of the stock price is PVGO — the present value of the firm's options to invest in second-stage, third-stage, or even later projects. These options arise from the time-series links between investments discussed in the previous section. The equation makes explicit that much of a growth firm's market value is option value, not the capitalized value of current earnings.

Practical use: The decomposition alerts managers and analysts to the source of value. If PVGO dominates the price, then conventional DCF-based financial strategy — which focuses on the EPS/r component — is likely to understate value and misguide investment decisions. The implied growth options must be valued with option pricing techniques.

Important assumption: EPS must be "normalized" to remove transitory effects; r reflects the risk of the firm's equity. The equation does not claim that PVGO can be measured precisely with DCF; on the contrary, PVGO is the present value of a portfolio of options, and options require dedicated valuation methods.

Example

Model/result: For a high-growth firm like Apple Computer in the 1980s, the stock price is far above EPS/r. The gap is PVGO. Interpretation: Investors are not paying only for the earnings the firm currently generates; they are paying for the suite of future investment opportunities the firm is expected to identify and exploit. Practical lesson: A strategic plan whose DCF analysis ignores PVGO will undervalue growth businesses. Financial strategy built on a simple DCF model of the firm is likely to be more useful for cash cows than for growth businesses with substantial risk and intangible assets.

Asset Type Appropriate Valuation Method Why
Bonds, preferred stock DCF Contractually fixed cash flows, low uncertainty
Safe dividend-paying stocks DCF Relatively predictable dividend streams
Growth companies DCF + option valuation (PVGO decomposition) Value includes options to invest in future stages
Traded calls and puts Option pricing formulas only Payoff is nonlinear, contingent on future prices
Financial leases (safe flows) DCF Contractual, low-risk cash flows
"Cash cows" / engineering investments DCF Value is in expected operating cash flows
Businesses with growth opportunities / intangibles DCF understates; option methods needed Large fraction of value is option value
Pure research and development Option valuation only Value is almost all option value

The table summarizes the central methodological argument: the applicability of DCF declines as the proportion of option value in the total increases. The paper then cites two preliminary applications of option pricing theory to real corporate assets:

Offshore oil and gas leases. The value of a federal lease for offshore exploration derives partly from the lessee's right to delay the decisions to drill and develop, and to make these decisions after observing the extent of reserves and future oil prices. This right is a series of call options. The option value is significant and cannot be captured by DCF analysis of a single expected development plan.

Abandonment or salvage value. An active second-hand market for an asset increases its value, other things equal. The second-hand market gives the asset owner a put option — the right to sell (bail out of) a poorly performing project. This put option increases the value of any project whose future cash flows might disappoint. Myers and Majd's work shows how option pricing theory can quantify this effect.

Real options, however, are more complex than traded options. They last longer; their terms must be extracted from the economics of the problem at hand; and realistic descriptions usually lead to complex implied contracts requiring numerical methods for valuation. Nevertheless, option pricing methods "hold great promise for strategic analysis" because the time-series links between projects are the most important part of financial strategy.

Analogy

Trying to value a growth company with DCF alone is like trying to measure a mountain's height with a thermometer. The thermometer is a fine instrument for temperature, but it is the wrong tool for altitude. DCF is a fine instrument for valuing known streams of cash flows, but it is the wrong tool for valuing the right — but not the obligation — to make future investments. The choice of instrument must match the nature of what is being measured.

Key Insight

For firms whose value consists largely of growth opportunities, the stock price decomposition shows that PVGO — the present value of a portfolio of real options — is a major component of market value. Conventional DCF cannot price this component, which explains why finance theory "missed the boat" in strategic applications even when correctly applied.

Key Takeaways

  • The stock price can be decomposed into the value of a level perpetuity of current earnings (EPS/r) plus the net present value of future growth opportunities (PVGO).
  • PVGO is the present value of a portfolio of options to invest in future projects; it is option value, not DCF value.
  • DCF is appropriate for bonds, safe dividend stocks, cash cows, and engineering investments, but understates the value of growth businesses, intangible assets, and R&D.
  • Real options have been successfully valued in offshore lease decisions (call options) and abandonment decisions (put options).
  • Real options are longer-lived and more complex than traded options, requiring numerical methods for valuation.

7. Lessons for Corporate Strategy

Myers draws a direct normative conclusion: when time-series links between projects are important, strategy should be understood as managing the firm's portfolio of real options. This reframing has three practical implications for the strategic planning process.

Lesson 1: Acquiring options. Firms acquire options either by investing directly in research and development, product design, cost or quality improvements, or as a by-product of direct capital investment. The Stage 1 project with negative NPV discussed earlier is the canonical example: the investment opens the door to Stage 2, and the option value of that door may justify the first stage even when its standalone NPV is negative. R&D is the purest case: its value is almost entirely option value because it creates the right, but not the obligation, to develop and commercialize new products or processes.

Lesson 2: Abandoning options. Not all options are worth keeping. Firms should abandon options that are too far "out of the money" to pay to keep. This is the real-option counterpart of letting an exchange-traded option expire worthless. An option's value falls as the underlying project becomes less attractive; holding it consumes resources (money, management attention, organizational focus). The discipline of option management requires periodically reviewing the strategic portfolio and eliminating options whose exercise price and remaining life no longer justify their carrying cost.

Lesson 3: Exercising valuable options at the right time. When the underlying conditions are favorable — the first stage worked and the market is still attractive — the firm should exercise its options by buying the cash-producing assets that ultimately deliver positive NPV. Timing matters: exercising too early commits resources before uncertainty is resolved; exercising too late may allow competitors to preempt the opportunity. Option thinking makes the timing decision explicit.

Myers also warns that some new approaches to financial strategy use a simple, traditional DCF model of the firm. These approaches are likely to be more useful for cash cows than for growth businesses with substantial risk and intangible assets. The option value of growth and intangibles is not ignored by good managers, even when conventional financial techniques miss them; managers bring these values in as "strategic factors," dressed in non-financial clothes. But this implicit treatment is not enough. Dealing explicitly with time-series links between capital investments, and with the option value these links create, should be part of strategic planning — and "new developments in finance theory promise to help."
