Introduction

Climate change is reshaping agricultural production systems worldwide by increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, disrupting value chains, and heightening price volatility in agri‑food markets (Abbass et al., 2022). While much of the adaptation literature focuses on production‑side interventions such as improved seed varieties, irrigation, and conservation agriculture, the resilience of agricultural systems also depends on how farmers market their produce. The choice of marketing channel influences transaction costs, income stability, and ultimately food security. In Tanzania, rice is a strategic commodity for food security and economic development, yet smallholder rice farmers face numerous marketing challenges, including limited bargaining power, poor infrastructure, and information asymmetry. This study investigates the determinants of marketing channel selection among smallholder rice farmers in Kilombero District, explicitly incorporating climate‑change risk perception and access to climate information services alongside traditional socioeconomic and institutional factors.

Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

The analysis is grounded in Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) as developed by Williamson (1985). TCE posits that economic actors select governance mechanisms—spot market exchange, long‑term contracts, or associative organizations—that minimize the sum of production and transaction costs. Transaction costs encompass activities such as information search, negotiation, contract enforcement, and quality inspection. In smallholder rice systems, these costs are exacerbated by poor infrastructure, remoteness, limited access to reliable market information, and weak bargaining power (Key et al., 2000). Climate change intensifies these challenges by causing yield volatility, shifting harvest times, and degrading product quality, which in turn raises search costs (finding buyers), negotiation costs (price fluctuations), and enforcement costs (delivery disruptions).

Within this framework, independent variables such as education, extension services, access to credit, and climate‑risk perception can be viewed as factors that reduce specific transaction‑cost components. Education and extension lower information and search costs; credit eases liquidity constraints in negotiations; climate‑risk perception influences the evaluation and enforcement of contracts. Farmers therefore evaluate marketing channels not only by the price received but also by the channel’s ability to reduce transaction costs. For instance, farm‑gate sales minimize transport and search costs but often yield lower prices; local markets offer higher prices but involve greater transportation effort and risk of unsold stock; cooperative marketing shares transportation, bargaining, and enforcement costs among members, lowering per‑capita transaction costs despite organizational overhead; urban/wholesale markets provide the highest returns but require large volumes, high quality, and reliable transport.

The conceptual framework (Figure 1 in the original article) links socioeconomic factors (education, farm size, gender), institutional support (extension services, credit access, farmer organization membership), channel characteristics (distance, price, collective bargaining), and climate‑change perception to marketing‑channel decisions. It posits that improved education, larger farms, better extension and credit services, strong cooperative membership, and accurate climate‑risk perception increase the likelihood of participating in high‑value channels (urban/wholesale and cooperative markets), whereas greater market distance and female‑headed households reduce that likelihood.

Research Methodology

Description of the Study Area

Kilombero District lies in the Morogoro Region of Tanzania, spanning latitudes 7°40′–9°40′ S and longitudes 35°50′–37°00′ E, covering approximately 14,918 km². The climate is tropical savanna with a wet season (November–May) and a dry season (June–October). Average annual rainfall ranges from 1,200 mm to 1,800 mm, and mean temperatures vary between 20 °C and 30 °C. The district’s Kilombero Valley contains fertile alluvial soils, flat topography, and irrigation potential from the Kilombero River, making it a national key rice‑producing zone. Most rice is cultivated under rain‑fed conditions with limited irrigation technology, and seasonal flooding is common, providing a suitable context for studying climate‑change adaptation.

Rationale for Selection of Study Area

Three reasons guided the choice of Kilombero District: (1) it is one of Tanzania’s leading rice‑producing areas, contributing significantly to national food security and household incomes; (2) the district offers a diverse range of marketing outlets—from farm‑gate to regional and urban markets—allowing comparison of market participation; and (3) smallholder farmers in the area are already experiencing increasing climatic adversity, including erratic rainfall, floods, and droughts, which affect production and marketing decisions. The selected wards (Kidatu, Mlimba, Kisawasawa, Msolwa Station) and one purposefully chosen village per ward were also based on the presence of active farmer groups and cooperatives, enabling an examination of institutional support’s impact on channel choice under climate variability.

Philosophy and Design

The study adopted a pragmatist research philosophy, treating the research problem as the focal point and allowing the integration of quantitative and qualitative methods. Pragmatism emphasizes practical consequences and methodological pluralism, making it suitable for exploring the multidimensional decision‑making of smallholder rice farmers who balance economic, social, and environmental factors under a changing climate. The quantitative component was informed by positivism, focusing on structured measurement of variables such as rice market price, distance to market, and number of extension visits. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically to capture meanings, experiences, and contextual insights. Triangulation between methods enhanced credibility and reliability.

Sampling Procedures

The sampling frame consisted of all smallholder rice farmers in the selected wards. Village Executive Officers assisted in identifying rice‑farming households. Using Cochran’s (1977) formula with a 95 % confidence level and a 5 % margin of error, the required sample size was calculated as 384 farmers. The total number of households across the four villages was 2,000, and the sample was proportionally allocated to each village based on household counts. Simple random sampling was employed to select respondents from household lists; when the household head was unavailable, another knowledgeable adult member was interviewed. For qualitative data, purposive sampling selected 12 key informants—agricultural extension agents, marketers, and cooperative chairpersons—based on their involvement in cooperative management, urban market activities, wholesale market participation, and awareness of climate‑change adaptation strategies and rice value chains.

Data Collection Methods

A mixed‑methods design employed five techniques: household surveys, key informant interviews (KIIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), direct observations, and document review. The household survey used structured questionnaires administered face‑to‑face to 384 randomly selected farmers, comprising mainly closed‑ended items for statistical analysis and a few open‑ended questions for clarification. Twelve KIIs, each lasting 45–60 minutes, provided in‑depth contextual information. Eight FGDs (two per village) were conducted, with separate men’s and women’s groups of eight participants each, to explore collective experiences regarding marketing‑channel selection and adaptation strategies. Direct observations at farms and sales points utilized a structured checklist, supplemented by photographs for validation. Document analysis included policy papers, extension manuals, and previous research reports.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were coded and entered into SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means) characterized socioeconomic profiles, marketing‑channel usage, and channel contributions to climate‑change adaptation. Inferential analysis employed binary logistic regression. The dependent variable was a dummy indicating participation in high‑value channels (urban/wholesale or cooperative = 1) versus low‑value channels (farm‑gate or local market = 0). Independent variables included age, gender, education level, household size, farm size, distance to market, access to extension services, membership in farmer organizations, perception of climate change, and access to credit. The logistic regression model estimated the log‑odds of high‑value channel participation as a function of these predictors. Odds ratios (Exp(β)) were derived to interpret the effect of a one‑unit change in each independent variable. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Qualitative data from FGDs, KIIs, and field notes were transcribed verbatim, coded inductively, and refined through constant comparison. Themes were identified, and triangulation across data sources ensured credibility.

Ethical Issues

Ethical clearance was obtained from the University of Dar es Salaam Research Ethics Review Committee (RERC) under reference AD25/67‑D. Additional permissions were secured from the Office of the Deputy Vice Chancellor (Research) and the Kilombero District Executive Director. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants after a full briefing on the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential uses of findings. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. All data were anonymized to protect privacy, and respondents were asked for consent to publish anonymized quotes. The study adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Marketing Channels Used by Smallholder Rice Farmers

Four primary marketing channels were identified: farm‑gate sales, village/local markets, cooperative or association‑based marketing, and urban/wholesale markets. Farm‑gate sales involve direct transactions with traders or middlemen at the farm shortly after harvest, offering immediate cash flow and low post‑harvest handling costs but typically lower prices due to weak farmer bargaining power and intermediary margins. Village/local markets are informal community‑level outlets where farmers sell directly to consumers, small traders, or retailers; they require more transportation effort and time but can yield better prices than farm‑gate sales for farmers with small surpluses and reliance on local demand. Cooperative marketing entails farmers pooling their produce through producer organizations to achieve better prices, improved bargaining power, access to credit, and shared information; success depends on the viability of farmer organizations and the ability to meet collective quantity or quality standards. Urban/wholesale markets are formal, distant outlets where wholesalers, millers, or large buyers purchase rice in cities or towns; they offer the highest returns but demand large volumes, high quality, and reliable transport services.

Table 2 presents the distribution of farmers across these channels. Village/local markets were the most frequently used channel, accounting for 27.6 % of sales (106 farmers). Cooperative marketing followed closely at 25.0 % (96 farmers). Urban/wholesale markets represented 24.5 % (94 farmers), and farm‑gate sales made up 22.9 % (88 farmers). The relatively even spread indicates that farmers adopt a diversified approach, selecting channels based on immediate cash needs, transaction costs, and expected returns.

Determinants of Marketing Channel Selection

Binary logistic regression was used to identify factors influencing the probability of participating in high‑value channels (urban/wholesale or cooperative). Table 3 shows the estimated coefficients, standard errors, odds ratios, and significance levels.

Education level emerged as a strong positive determinant (β = 0.192, p = 0.001). Each additional year of schooling increased the odds of using high‑value channels by 21 % (Exp(β) = 1.21). This reflects the role of education in enhancing farmers’ ability to process market information, negotiate effectively, and adopt improved marketing strategies.

Participation in farmer organizations also significantly increased the likelihood of high‑value channel use (β = 0.382, p = 0.032), with odds 1.46 times higher for members. Membership provides collective bargaining power, access to group‑based credit or savings, and assistance in meeting buyer quality and quantity requirements.

Farm size exerted a positive influence (β = 0.145, p = 0.002). Each additional acre raised the odds of high‑value channel participation by 16 % (Exp(β) = 1.16), consistent with economies of scale that enable larger farmers to absorb transaction costs and meet the volume demands of distant markets.

Access to extension services was a significant predictor (β = 0.628, p = 0.005). Farmers receiving extension advice were 1.87 times more likely to engage in high‑value channels (Exp(β) = 1.87). Extension improves knowledge of quality standards, post‑harvest handling, and market‑linked agronomic practices, thereby reducing information and enforcement costs.

Access to credit significantly boosted high‑value channel participation (β = 0.572, p = 0.017), increasing the odds by 77 % (Exp(β) = 1.77). Credit alleviates liquidity constraints, enabling farmers to cover transport, storage, and marketing expenses that would otherwise be prohibitive.

Perception of climate change also positively influenced channel choice (β = 0.395, p = 0.006). Farmers who perceived greater climate risk were 1.48 times more likely to use high‑value channels, likely because they seek stable demand and guaranteed prices as a risk‑management strategy.

Gender showed a significant effect: male‑headed households had 1.62 times higher odds of accessing high‑value channels than female‑headed households (β = 0.482, p = 0.038), reflecting underlying sociocultural constraints on women’s mobility, asset ownership, and network access.

Market distance had a negative impact: each additional kilometer reduced the odds of high‑value channel participation by 10 % (β = −0.103, p = 0.004; Exp(β) = 0.90). Greater distance raises transport costs, time losses, and spoilage risk, discouraging engagement with distant urban/wholesale markets.

Age, household size, and the constant term were not statistically significant at the 5 % level.

Marketing Channels and Climate Change Adaptation

Table 4 presents descriptive percentages of farmers reporting adaptation benefits across the four marketing channels. Benefits are grouped into five dimensions: reduced post‑harvest losses, improved price stability, access to climate information, collective bargaining power, and diversification opportunities.

For reduced post‑harvest losses, 68.7 % of cooperative‑market users and 65.4 % of urban/wholesale‑market users reported benefits, compared with 42.5 % of local‑market users and 31.2 % of farm‑gate sellers. Improved price stability was reported by 72.1 % of cooperative participants and 70.5 % of urban/wholesale participants, versus 38.7 % of local‑market sellers and 24.6 % of farm‑gate sellers. Access to climate information was notably higher in organized channels: 55.6 % of cooperative users and 49.8 % of urban/wholesale users reported receiving climate‑related data, while only 18.2 % of local‑market and 12.4 % of farm‑gate farmers did so. Collective bargaining power was experienced by 71.4 % of cooperative members and 72.3 % of urban/wholesale participants, compared with 15.7 % of local‑market and 9.3 % of farm‑gate sellers. Diversification opportunities were reported by 50.7 % of urban/wholesale users, 46.2 % of cooperative users, 22.6 % of local‑market users, and 15.8 % of farm‑gate sellers.

These descriptive results indicate that farmers who accessed cooperative and urban/wholesale channels derived substantially greater adaptation benefits than those relying on farm‑gate or local markets. The organized channels appear to function as risk‑management tools, offering price stability, reduced losses, better information, and stronger bargaining power—all critical for coping with climate‑induced variability.

Discussion

The findings confirm that smallholder rice farmers in Kilombero District employ a portfolio of marketing channels, with village/local markets being the most frequently used single outlet. This pattern aligns with the preference for channels offering convenience of access and low transaction costs, especially among resource‑constrained farmers. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion of farmers continue to rely on farm‑gate sales (22.9 %), reflecting persistent market failures, inadequate infrastructure, and weak bargaining power that compel farmers to accept lower prices in exchange for immediate cash and minimal logistical effort.

The logistic regression results are consistent with prior research. Education’s positive effect echoes studies by Alene et al. (2006) and Kwaramba et al. (2023), which found that schooling enhances farmers’ capacity to interpret market signals and adopt improved practices. The significant role of farm size agrees with Rios et al. (2009), indicating that larger farms benefit from economies of scale when accessing distant, high‑value markets. The positive influence of extension services and credit access mirrors the findings of Olabanji and Chitakira (2025) and Ma et al. (2024), underscoring that technical guidance and financial resources reduce information, negotiation, and enforcement costs.

Membership in farmer organizations emerged as a critical determinant, supporting the view that cooperatives and associations lower per‑capita transaction costs through shared transportation, bargaining, and enforcement (Markelova et al., 2009). This finding also resonates with the observed resurgence of cooperative participation in the study area, suggesting that collective marketing institutions may be strengthening in response to intensifying climate‑related uncertainties.

The perception of climate change as a driver of high‑value channel use aligns with the work of Bryan et al. (2009), which linked climate‑risk perception to the adoption of agronomic innovations and strategic market participation. Farmers who perceive greater climatic risk appear to seek channels that offer price stability and guaranteed returns, using marketing as an adaptation strategy.

Gender disparities persist, with male‑headed households enjoying better access to high‑value channels. This reflects broader sociocultural norms that limit women’s mobility, asset ownership, and access to extension and credit services—a pattern documented by FAO (2021) and other gender‑focused studies in African agriculture.

Market distance exerts a strong negative influence, confirming that transport costs and associated risks remain a major barrier to accessing distant urban/wholesale markets. This finding reinforces the need for investments in rural infrastructure such as feeder roads, storage facilities, and market‑information systems to reduce the effective distance between producers and lucrative markets.

The descriptive analysis of adaptation benefits (Table 4) highlights that cooperative and urban/wholesale channels deliver superior outcomes in terms of price stability, reduced post‑harvest losses, access to climate information, collective bargaining, and diversification. These channels thus serve not only as economic conduits but also as institutional arrangements that enhance climate resilience. The results support the Transaction Cost Economics perspective: by lowering search, negotiation, and enforcement costs, organized marketing channels improve both economic returns and adaptive capacity under climatic uncertainty.

Overall, the study demonstrates that marketing‑channel choice is shaped by an interplay of socioeconomic, institutional, and climatic factors. Policies that strengthen education, extension services, credit access, farmer organizations, and rural infrastructure—while addressing gender inequities and reducing market distance—are likely to increase smallholder participation in high‑value, climate‑resilient marketing channels.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the use of purposive sampling for selecting villages and key informants may have introduced selection bias, limiting the generalizability of findings to other rice‑growing regions in Tanzania. Second, the cross‑sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships; the observed associations may be influenced by unobserved confounders. Third, reliance on self‑reported data raises the possibility of response bias, particularly for sensitive topics such as income and climate‑risk perception. Fourth, the study did not explicitly address endogeneity between variables such as access to credit, extension services, and cooperative membership and marketing‑channel choice. Future research could employ instrumental‑variable techniques or panel data to mitigate these concerns.

Conclusion and Recommendations

The study concludes that smallholder rice farmers in Kilombero District utilize multiple marketing channels, with choice determined by a combination of education, farm size, access to extension services, credit availability, participation in farmer organizations, perception of climate change, gender, and market distance. High‑value channels (urban/wholesale and cooperative markets) provide substantial adaptation benefits, including price stability, reduced post‑harvest losses, improved access to climate information, and enhanced collective bargaining power. Conversely, reliance on low‑value channels (farm‑gate and local markets) leaves farmers more exposed to price volatility, post‑harvest losses, and limited information.

Based on these findings, the following recommendations are proposed:

  • Strengthen farmer organizations and cooperatives – Provide technical and financial support to improve governance, collective bargaining capacity, and access to credit and extension services within groups.
  • Invest in rural infrastructure – Upgrade feeder roads, construct storage facilities, and develop local market centers to reduce transport costs and spoilage, thereby lowering the effective distance to high‑value markets.
  • Enhance extension services – Integrate climate‑smart agriculture and market‑information modules into extension programming to equip farmers with knowledge on quality standards, post‑harvest handling, and climate‑risk management.
  • Improve access to credit – Develop tailored credit products (e.g., warehouse receipt financing, group‑based loans) that address the liquidity needs of smallholders for transport, storage, and market participation.
  • Promote gender‑responsive interventions – Implement targeted programs that increase women’s access to land, extension, credit, and market information, and support women’s participation in farmer organizations.
  • Develop market‑information systems – Establish reliable, timely dissemination of price, demand, and climate‑forecast data through mobile platforms, community radio, and farmer‑led information hubs.
  • Encourage climate‑smart marketing practices – Encourage the adoption of practices such as collective sorting, grading, and certification that meet buyer requirements and reduce post‑harvest losses.

By implementing these measures, policymakers and development actors can foster a more inclusive, efficient, and climate‑resilient marketing environment for smallholder rice farmers in Tanzania and similar contexts.
