Enterprise Risk Management and Firm Performance: Role of the Risk Committee

This article provides a comprehensive summary of the empirical study titled “Enterprise risk management and firm performance: Role of the risk committee” by Muhammad Farhan Malik, Mahbub Zaman, and Sherrena Buckby. The paper investigates how the effectiveness of Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) influences firm performance and whether the presence of a structurally strong board‑level risk committee (BLRC) amplifies this relationship. The analysis is based on 260 firm‑year observations drawn from FTSE350 companies listed on the London Stock Exchange during 2012‑2015. The study employs Tobin’s Q as the primary performance metric, adopts the Gordon et al. (2009) ERM index to capture the effectiveness of ERM processes, and uses principal component analysis (PCA) to derive a composite measure of BLRC structural strength. The findings reveal a positive and significant association between ERM effectiveness and firm performance, and demonstrate that a strong BLRC moderates this relationship, enhancing the value‑relevance of ERM. The following sections detail the study’s motivation, theoretical background, hypothesis development, methodology, results, discussion, and conclusions.

Introduction

Motivation

The early 2000s corporate scandals and the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) of 2007‑09 intensified regulatory scrutiny of risk management practices worldwide. In the United Kingdom, the Walker Report (2009) and subsequent Financial Reporting Council (FRC) guidelines (2011, 2014a,b) urged listed firms to adopt holistic ERM frameworks and to strengthen board oversight through the creation of a board‑level risk committee. These recommendations were motivated by the belief that inefficient risk management contributed to the subprime meltdown, heightened market volatility, and severe earnings shocks. Consequently, UK firms faced increasing pressure to demonstrate robust risk governance mechanisms that could protect shareholder wealth and improve long‑term performance.

Literature Gap

While a substantial body of research examines the link between ERM and firm performance, the evidence remains mixed, particularly outside the United States. US‑based studies report both positive and null effects of ERM on market value, whereas European investigations are scarce and often limited to specific countries such as Germany and Italy. Moreover, prior work has largely treated the presence of a risk committee as a binary variable, overlooking the importance of the committee’s structural characteristics. The current study addresses these gaps by focusing on the UK setting, employing a continuous ERM effectiveness index, and examining how specific BLRC attributes—size, independence, meeting frequency, financial expertise, gender diversity, and overlapping directorships—condition the ERM‑performance relationship.

Contribution

The paper contributes to the risk management and corporate governance literature in three ways. First, it extends the ERM‑performance literature to the UK context, providing evidence that ERM is value‑relevant when measured by a multidimensional index. Second, it demonstrates that the mere existence of a BLRC does not guarantee improved ERM oversight; rather, a structurally strong BLRC—characterized by high levels of independence, diligence, financial expertise, gender diversity, and cross‑committee membership—is necessary to enhance the effectiveness of ERM processes. Third, the study introduces a PCA‑derived composite measure of BLRC strength, offering a replicable methodological approach for future research on board‑level committee effectiveness.

Background

UK Regulatory Framework

Following the GFC, UK regulators emphasized the need for a comprehensive risk management approach. The Walker Report (2009) recommended that listed firms establish a board‑level risk committee to oversee ERM activities and to ensure that risk considerations are integrated into strategic decision‑making. The FRC’s 2011 and 2014 guidelines reinforced this recommendation, advocating for a holistic ERM framework that encompasses risk identification, assessment, monitoring, and reporting. These guidelines also highlighted the importance of board diversity, particularly gender diversity, as a mechanism to mitigate groupthink and improve monitoring quality.

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)

According to the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO, 2004), ERM is a process that enables organizations to identify potential risk events, manage risks within predefined appetite and tolerance levels, and provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of organizational objectives. ERM comprises four interrelated objectives: (1) aligning risk management with strategy, (2) enhancing operational efficiency and effectiveness, (3) ensuring reliable reporting, and (4) guaranteeing compliance with applicable laws and regulations. The Gordon et al. (2009) ERM index operationalizes these objectives by constructing standardized scores for strategy, operations, reporting, and compliance, then aggregating them into a single composite measure.

Board‑Level Risk Committee (BLRC)

A BLRC is a sub‑committee of the board tasked with overseeing the firm’s ERM framework. Its core responsibilities include reviewing risk‑management policies, monitoring risk‑reporting quality, fostering a risk‑aware culture, and advising the board on risk appetite and tolerance. Unlike the traditional audit committee, which primarily focuses on financial reporting risks, the BLRC adopts a holistic view of risk, encompassing strategic, operational, compliance, and reputational dimensions. The effectiveness of a BLRC is contingent upon its structural characteristics, such as size, independence, meeting frequency, expertise of members, gender diversity, and the extent to which its members serve on other board committees.

Hypotheses Development

Impact of ERM on Firm Performance

Theoretical arguments suggest that effective ERM reduces earnings volatility, improves strategic decision‑making, enhances operational efficiency, and strengthens stakeholder confidence, thereby increasing firm value. Empirical work in the US has shown mixed results, with some studies reporting positive associations between ERM quality and market‑based performance measures, while others find insignificant effects. In the UK context, limited evidence exists, prompting the first hypothesis:

H1: Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) is positively associated with firm performance.

Role of a BLRC in the ERM‑Performance Relationship

Governance theory posits that board committees enhance monitoring effectiveness when they possess appropriate structural attributes. A strong BLRC—marked by a larger size, higher proportion of independent directors, frequent meetings, financial expertise, gender diversity, and overlapping directorships—can improve the quality of risk oversight, reduce information asymmetry, and ensure that ERM processes are aligned with strategic objectives. Consequently, the second hypothesis asserts that the positive impact of ERM on firm performance is amplified when the BLRC is structurally strong:

H2: The effectiveness of ERM on firm performance increases in the presence of a strong board‑level risk committee (BLRC).

Research Methods

Sample Selection and Data Collection

The target population consists of all FTSE350 firms listed on the London Stock Exchange (LSE) during the period 2012‑2015. The sample construction proceeded in two stages. First, firms that disclosed ERM‑related activities in their annual reports were identified via keyword searches for terms such as “enterprise risk management,” “chief risk officer,” “risk committee,” and related phrases. This step yielded 410 firm‑year observations with evidence of ERM adoption. Second, the sample was restricted to firm‑years that also reported the existence of a board‑level risk committee. After excluding observations with missing data on BLRC characteristics or control variables, the final sample comprised 260 firm‑year observations. Data were sourced primarily from corporate annual reports, supplemented by market‑based variables extracted from the Datastream database.

Variable Measurement

Firm Performance: Tobin’s Q was calculated as the market value of equity plus the book value of debt, divided by the book value of total assets. This forward‑looking metric captures investors’ expectations of future profitability and is less susceptible to short‑term managerial manipulation.

ERM Effectiveness: The Gordon et al. (2009) ERM index was adopted. Each of the four COSMO objectives—strategy, operations, reporting, and compliance—was represented by two standardized sub‑measures, which were summed to produce a composite index. Higher index values indicate more effective ERM processes.

BLRC Structural Strength: Six observable dimensions were used: committee size (RCSIZE), independence (RCIND), meeting frequency (RCMEET), financial expertise (RCFIN), gender diversity (RCFEM), and overlapping directorships with other board committees (RCOL). Principal component analysis (PCA) with varimax rotation was applied to these six variables to extract a common factor (RCPCA) that captures the underlying latent construct of BLRC strength. A dummy variable (STRONGRC) was then created, assigning a value of 1 to firms whose RCPCA score exceeds the sample median (indicating a strong BLRC) and 0 otherwise.

Control Variables: The model controls for board characteristics (size, independence, meeting frequency), firm size (natural log of total assets), leverage (ratio of total liabilities to total assets), number of business segments, extreme performance dummy (EXPERF), loss reporting dummy (LOSS), and a combined audit‑and‑risk committee dummy (ACRC). Industry dummies were included in alternate specifications to account for sector‑specific effects.

Statistical Analysis

Hierarchical multiple regression was estimated using ordinary least squares (OLS). In Step 1, the main effects of ERM and BLRC (both the continuous RCPCA factor and the dummy STRONGRC) on firm performance were examined (Models 1 and 2). Step 2 introduced interaction terms to test the moderating hypothesis (Models 3 and 4). Specifically, Model 4 includes the interaction term ERM × STRONGRC, which directly evaluates H2. All specifications controlled for the aforementioned firm‑ and board‑level variables, with and without industry fixed effects. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) remained below 3, indicating minimal multicollinearity concerns.

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Table 3a presents the descriptive statistics for the continuous and binary variables. The mean Tobin’s Q is 1.533 (median = 1.170), indicating that, on average, the sample firms are overvalued relative to their book values. The ERM index has a mean of zero (by construction, due to cross‑sectional standardization) with a standard deviation of 3.144, ranging from ‑7.720 (most ineffective) to 20.769 (most effective). Regarding BLRC characteristics, the average committee size is 4.638 directors, with a median of 4; the average number of independent directors is 4.226 (median = 4). Committees meet approximately 5.115 times per year, and contain on average 2.353 financial experts and 1.142 female directors. The average number of BLRC members serving on other board committees is 3.865. Control variables show an average board size of 10.703 directors, with 6.530 independent directors, and boards meeting 8.546 times annually. The natural log of total assets (FSIZE) averages approximately 11.63 (implying substantial firm size), while the mean leverage ratio is 0.642. About 10 % of firms exhibit extreme performance (EXPERF = 1), 10.3 % report a net loss (LOSS = 1), and 55 % have a combined audit‑and‑risk committee (ACRC = 1).

Principal Component Analysis of BLRC Measures

Table 3b outlines the PCA results. The Kaiser‑Meyer‑Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy is 0.808, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.6, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity is highly significant (χ² = 696.196, p < 0.01), confirming the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Extraction communalities range from 0.525 (RCFEM) to 0.835 (RCSIZE), indicating that the common factor accounts for a substantial proportion of variance in each BLRC attribute. The first component explains 53.98 % of the total variance, with the second component adding another 17.60 % (cumulative 71.58 %). The rotated factor loadings (Panel D) reveal strong positive correlations between the extracted factor (RCPCA) and all six BLRC variables: RCSIZE (0.899), RCIND (0.884), RCOL (0.880), RCFIN (0.675), RCFEM (0.613), and RCMEET (0.206). These loadings support the interpretation of RCPCA as a comprehensive proxy for BLRC structural strength.

Strong versus Weak BLRCs

Table 3c compares firms with strong BLRCs (RCPCA > median) against those with weak BLRCs. Panel A shows that strong BLRCs are larger (mean size = 5.515 vs. 3.761), more independent (mean independence = 5.176 vs. 3.276), meet more frequently (mean meetings = 5.715 vs. 4.515), possess more financial experts (mean = 2.992 vs. 1.715), have greater gender diversity (mean female directors = 1.676 vs. 0.607), and exhibit higher overlapping directorships (mean = 4.969 vs. 2.761). Panel C reports statistically significant mean differences for each characteristic (all p < 0.01), confirming that the stratification into strong and weak groups captures meaningful variation in BLRC structure.

Regression Analysis – Main Effects

Table 4 presents the hierarchical regression results for Tobin’s Q. In Model 1 (without industry dummies), the ERM coefficient is positive and significant at the 10 % level (β = 0.114, t = 1.869). Model 2 adds the BLRC dummy (STRONGRC) and yields a significant positive effect for both ERM (β = 0.103, t = 1.664) and STRONGRC (β = 0.185, t = 3.000). The inclusion of industry dummies in Models 3 and 4 does not alter the significance of the ERM term (β = 0.121, t = 1.988 in Model 3; β = 0.111, t = 1.793 in Model 4) or the STRONGRC term (β = 0.171, t = 2.671 in Model 4). Control variables reveal that firm size (FSIZE) exerts a strong negative influence on Q (β ≈ ‑0.53, p < 0.001), consistent with the expectation that larger firms tend to have lower market‑to‑book ratios. Leverage (LEV) is not significantly related to Q in any specification. Extreme performance (EXPERF) shows a robust positive association (β ≈ 0.22, p < 0.01), while loss reporting (LOSS) and the combined audit‑risk committee dummy (ACRC) are insignificant. The model explains approximately 22‑25 % of the variation in Q (R² ranging from 0.228 to 0.248).

Regression Analysis – Interaction Effects

Step 2 of Table 4 examines the moderating role of the BLRC. Model 3 includes the interaction term ERM × RCPCA, which is positive and significant at the 5 % level (β = 0.138, t = 2.210). Model 4 replaces the continuous interaction with ERM × STRONGRC, yielding a positive and significant coefficient at the 10 % level (β = 0.120, t = 1.750). The main effect of ERM remains significant in Model 3 (β = 0.176, t = 2.638) but becomes non‑significant in Model 4 when the interaction term is entered (β = 0.056, t = 0.799), suggesting that the impact of ERM on Q is contingent upon the presence of a strong BLRC. The interaction terms increase the model’s explanatory power modestly (ΔR² ≈ 0.009‑0.015), and the associated F‑statistics for the change in R² are significant (p < 0.05). Similar patterns emerge when the dependent variable is replaced by the market‑to‑book ratio (MTB) in Table 5, reinforcing the robustness of the findings.

Additional Analyses

The authors conducted several robustness checks, including alternative measures of ERM (e.g., a dummy variable indicating ERM disclosure), different specifications of BLRC strength (e.g., equal‑weight indexing), and subsample analyses separating financial from non‑financial firms. The core results remained unchanged: ERM effectiveness is positively linked to firm performance, and this relationship is strengthened when the BLRC exhibits high structural strength. No evidence of multicollinearity or heteroskedasticity was found that would threaten the validity of the inferences.

Discussion

Interpretation of Findings

The study’s results support H1, indicating that more effective ERM processes are associated with higher firm performance, as captured by Tobin’s Q. This aligns with the theoretical view that ERM reduces uncertainty, improves strategic alignment, and enhances operational efficiency, thereby increasing shareholder wealth. The positive and significant coefficient for ERM persists across model specifications, underscoring the reliability of the Gordon et al. (2009) index as a measure of ERM effectiveness in the UK context.

Regarding H2, the interaction analysis reveals that the beneficial effect of ERM on performance is amplified when the BLRC is structurally strong. The significant positive interaction terms (ERM × RCPCA and ERM × STRONGRC) suggest that the value‑relevance of ERM is not automatic; it depends on the quality of the oversight mechanism. A strong BLRC—characterized by greater size, independence, meeting frequency, financial expertise, gender diversity, and cross‑committee membership—appears to enhance the monitoring and implementation of ERM policies, thereby translating ERM effectiveness into superior market performance. Conversely, a weak BLRC fails to provide sufficient oversight, leaving the potential benefits of ERM unrealized.

The control variable results offer additional insights. The strong negative impact of firm size (FSIZE) on Q is consistent with prior literature documenting a size‑related discount in market valuations. The insignificance of leverage (LEV) may reflect the sample’s relatively homogeneous debt levels or the countervailing effects of tax shields versus financial distress risk. The positive coefficient for extreme performance (EXPERF) indicates that firms with exceptionally high earnings per share enjoy a market premium, while loss reporting (LOSS) does not exert a significant downward pressure on Q, possibly because losses are often transitory or offset by growth expectations. The combined audit‑and‑risk committee dummy (ACRC) remains insignificant, suggesting that merging the audit and risk functions does not, in this sample, confer additional performance benefits beyond those captured by the BLRC strength measure.

Theoretical Implications

The findings extend the contingency‑based perspective of Gordon et al. (2009) by demonstrating that the match between ERM effectiveness and firm performance is contingent not only on external factors (e.g., environmental uncertainty, industry competition) but also on internal governance structures, specifically the BLRC. This reinforces the argument that board committees are not merely ceremonial; their structural attributes directly influence the efficacy of risk‑management processes. Moreover, the study validates the use of PCA to derive a latent governance factor, offering a methodological advancement for future research on board‑level committee effectiveness.

Practical Implications

For practitioners, the results imply that merely establishing a board‑level risk committee is insufficient to reap the rewards of ERM. Organizations should invest in developing a BLRC with the following characteristics: (1) adequate size to ensure diverse perspectives, (2) a high proportion of independent directors to mitigate management influence, (3) regular meetings to sustain oversight, (4) inclusion of financial experts to enhance the quality of risk analysis, (5) gender diversity to promote constructive debate and reduce groupthink, and (6) encouragement of overlapping directorships to foster coordination with other board committees. Regulators and standard‑setting bodies may consider emphasizing these structural attributes in guidelines for risk‑committee composition, moving beyond binary presence/absence requirements.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant caution. First, the sample is confined to UK FTSE350 firms during 2012‑2015, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other jurisdictions, time periods, or smaller firms. Second, the reliance on annual‑report disclosures for measuring ERM and BLRC characteristics introduces potential measurement error if firms engage in symbolic or ceremonial reporting without substantive changes in practice. Third, while the PCA‑derived factor captures shared variance among the six BLRC dimensions, it does not elucidate the relative importance of each attribute; future research could employ alternative weighting schemes or qualitative case studies to unpack these dynamics. Fourth, the study uses Tobin’s Q and MTB as performance proxies, which, although widely accepted, may not fully capture long‑term value creation associated with ERM. Finally, the cross‑sectional nature of the data, despite the panel structure, restricts causal inferences; longitudinal or experimental designs would be beneficial to establish causality more definitively.

Conclusion

Summary

This study investigates the relationship between Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) effectiveness and firm performance, with a particular focus on the moderating role of the board‑level risk committee (BLRC). Using a sample of 260 firm‑year observations from FTSE350 companies listed on the London Stock Exchange (2012‑2015), the paper finds that ERM is positively and significantly associated with Tobin’s Q. Moreover, the positive impact of ERM on performance is strengthened when the BLRC exhibits structural strength—defined by larger size, greater independence, more frequent meetings, higher financial expertise, enhanced gender diversity, and increased overlapping directorships. The mere presence of a BLRC does not guarantee improved ERM oversight; rather, a substantively strong BLRC is necessary to translate ERM effectiveness into superior market performance.

Future Research

Future work could extend the analysis in several directions. Researchers might examine whether the findings hold in other institutional contexts, such as the United States, Europe, or emerging markets, where regulatory expectations for risk committees differ. Incorporating dynamic panel techniques or instrumental variable approaches could help address endogeneity concerns and strengthen causal claims. Additionally, exploring the impact of specific ERM components (e.g., risk‑culture initiatives, scenario analysis, or cyber‑risk management) on performance, and how these are mediated by BLRC attributes, would provide finer‑grained insights. Qualitative investigations, including interviews with risk‑committee members and chief risk officers, could illuminate the mechanisms through which structural characteristics influence ERM implementation and monitoring effectiveness. Finally, expanding the set of BLRC characteristics to include attributes such as risk‑committee chair expertise, turnover rates, or the use of external risk consultants could enrich the understanding of governance quality in risk oversight.
