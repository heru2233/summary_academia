Introduction and Research Background

Interpretation

This seminal meta-analytic study by Jiang, Lepak, Hu, and Baer (2012) addresses a fundamental question in strategic human resource management: How exactly do human resource management practices influence organizational outcomes? The authors recognize that while extensive research has demonstrated positive relationships between HR systems and organizational performance, the mechanisms through which these effects occur remain fragmented and poorly understood. The study aims to integrate multiple theoretical perspectives—the behavioral perspective, human capital theory, and the resource-based view of the firm—to create a comprehensive mediating model that explains the pathways from HR practices to financial outcomes. The authors argue that previous research has typically focused on either employee behaviors or employee competencies, but rarely both simultaneously. By drawing on the ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) model, they propose that HR systems can be decomposed into three distinct dimensions—skill-enhancing, motivation-enhancing, and opportunity-enhancing practices—each of which may have differential effects on proximal outcomes (human capital and employee motivation) and, ultimately, on distal outcomes (voluntary turnover, operational outcomes, and financial outcomes). The study uses meta-analytic structural equation modeling to test these relationships across 116 articles representing 120 independent samples and 31,463 organizations.

Core Research Question
How do the three dimensions of HR systems (skill-enhancing, motivation-enhancing, opportunity-enhancing) differentially influence human capital and employee motivation, and how do these proximal outcomes mediate the relationships between HR dimensions and distal organizational outcomes (voluntary turnover, operational outcomes, financial outcomes)?

Example

Consider a manufacturing company that implements a new HR system. The company invests in rigorous selection and extensive training programs (skill-enhancing), implements performance-based pay and promotion opportunities (motivation-enhancing), and creates cross-functional teams and employee involvement programs (opportunity-enhancing). The study asks whether these three sets of practices affect employee skills and motivation differently, and how these effects ultimately translate into lower turnover, higher productivity, and better financial performance.

Analogy

Think of an HR system as a three-engine aircraft. The skill-enhancing practices are like the engine that builds the aircraft's capabilities—its speed and range. The motivation-enhancing practices are like the fuel system that provides energy and incentives for the aircraft to perform. The opportunity-enhancing practices are like the navigation and control systems that allow the aircraft to use its capabilities effectively. Each engine serves a different function, and all three are needed for optimal flight performance. The study examines how each engine contributes to the aircraft's performance metrics, from immediate operational efficiency to long-term profitability.

Key Insight

The key insight of this study is that HR systems are not monolithic—different components of HR systems serve different functions and have different effects on organizational outcomes. Skill-enhancing practices are primarily about building human capital, while motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices are more about shaping employee attitudes and behaviors. Understanding these differential effects is crucial for both theory development and practical HR system design. The study also emphasizes that the path from HR to financial performance is indirect and sequential: HR practices first affect proximal outcomes (human capital and motivation), which then affect turnover and operational outcomes, which ultimately affect financial performance.

Key Takeaways

  • The study integrates behavioral, human capital, and resource-based perspectives to create a comprehensive mediating model of HRM effects.
  • HR systems are decomposed into three dimensions: skill-enhancing, motivation-enhancing, and opportunity-enhancing practices.
  • The research question focuses on differential effects of HR dimensions and the sequential mediating pathways to financial outcomes.
  • The study uses meta-analytic SEM across 116 articles and 31,463 organizations.

Theoretical Foundations: The AMO Model and Multiple Perspectives

Interpretation

The theoretical foundation of this study rests on three key pillars. First, the ability-motivation-opportunity (AMO) model, originally developed by Appelbaum, Bailey, Berg, and Kalleberg (2000), posits that employee performance is a function of three essential components: ability (skills and knowledge), motivation (willingness to exert effort), and opportunity (the chance to use skills and motivation). Extending this logic to the organizational level, HR systems designed to maximize employee performance can be viewed as comprising three dimensions: skill-enhancing practices (recruitment, selection, training), motivation-enhancing practices (performance management, compensation, incentives, benefits, promotion, job security), and opportunity-enhancing practices (job design, work teams, employee involvement, information sharing). Second, the behavioral perspective of HRM argues that organizations use HR practices to encourage productive employee behaviors that align with organizational goals. Third, human capital theory and the resource-based view emphasize that employees' knowledge, skills, and abilities are valuable, rare, inimitable, and nonsubstitutable resources that can provide competitive advantage. The authors integrate these perspectives to propose that HRM influences organizational outcomes through two parallel pathways: one through employee motivation and behaviors, and another through human capital development. The study also draws on Dyer and Reeves's (1995) categorization of organizational outcomes into three groups: HR outcomes (skills, attitudes, turnover), operational outcomes (productivity, quality, service, innovation), and financial outcomes (sales growth, return on assets, profitability).

Organizational Performance = f(Skill-Enhancing HR, Motivation-Enhancing HR, Opportunity-Enhancing HR)

Proximal ← → Distal

Example

A hospital wants to improve patient care quality. According to the AMO model, the hospital needs to: (1) ensure nurses and doctors have the necessary skills through rigorous hiring and training (ability), (2) motivate them to perform at their best through performance bonuses and career advancement opportunities (motivation), and (3) provide them with the autonomy, resources, and decision-making authority to apply their skills effectively (opportunity). The study examines how these three dimensions of HR practices work together and differentially contribute to outcomes like patient satisfaction (operational) and hospital profitability (financial).

Analogy

Imagine a high-performance sports team. The skill-enhancing practices are like the training regimen and talent scouting that build players' abilities. The motivation-enhancing practices are like the salary, bonuses, and recognition that drive players to give their best effort. The opportunity-enhancing practices are like the team's offensive and defensive systems that allow players to use their skills effectively during games. A team needs all three components to win championships, but each component contributes differently: training builds capability, incentives drive effort, and systems enable execution. The study applies this logic to organizations, examining how each type of HR practice contributes to organizational success.

Key Insight

The critical theoretical insight is that previous research has typically adopted either a behavioral perspective (focusing on motivation and behaviors) or a human capital perspective (focusing on skills and abilities), but rarely both. By integrating these perspectives within the AMO framework, the study provides a more complete picture of how HRM influences organizational outcomes. The three theoretical perspectives are complementary rather than competing: the behavioral perspective explains how HR practices shape employee actions, human capital theory explains how HR practices build valuable competencies, and the resource-based view explains why these competencies can be a source of sustainable competitive advantage. Together, they suggest that HRM affects financial performance through multiple, simultaneous pathways.

Key Takeaways

  • The AMO model provides the organizing framework for decomposing HR systems into three dimensions: skill-enhancing, motivation-enhancing, and opportunity-enhancing practices.
  • The behavioral perspective emphasizes that HR practices shape employee role behaviors and actions.
  • Human capital theory and the resource-based view emphasize that HR practices build valuable, rare, and inimitable employee competencies.
  • Organizational outcomes are categorized as HR outcomes (proximal), operational outcomes (intermediate), and financial outcomes (distal).
  • The study integrates multiple theoretical perspectives to provide a more complete understanding of HRM effects.

Decomposing HR Systems into Three Dimensions

Interpretation

The authors argue that while employees are exposed to HR systems as a whole, the components of these systems are not necessarily equivalent in their impact. Most prior research has treated HR systems as additive indices, where each practice is assumed to have equal weight and identical effects. However, recent research challenges this assumption, suggesting that different sets of HR practices may impact outcomes in heterogeneous ways. Drawing on the AMO model, the authors categorize HR practices into three distinct dimensions. Skill-enhancing HR practices are designed to ensure that organizations have appropriately skilled employees. These include comprehensive recruitment (attracting a large and qualified applicant pool), rigorous selection (using validated tests and interviews to identify the best candidates), and extensive training (providing both general and organization-specific skills). Motivation-enhancing HR practices are implemented to enhance employee motivation and include developmental performance management (goal-setting, feedback, and performance reviews), competitive compensation (above-market pay), incentives and rewards (bonuses, profit-sharing, stock options), extensive benefits (health insurance, retirement plans), promotion and career development (internal advancement opportunities, career ladders), and job security (employment guarantees, no-layoff policies). Opportunity-enhancing HR practices are designed to empower employees to use their skills and motivation to achieve organizational objectives. These include flexible job design (enriched jobs with autonomy and variety), work teams (self-managed teams, cross-functional teams), employee involvement (participation in decision-making, quality circles), and information sharing (open communication about company performance, strategy, and goals). The three-dimensional framework is superior to unidimensional or two-dimensional frameworks because it captures the distinct functions of different HR practices and allows for the examination of differential effects.

Example

A technology company implements a comprehensive HR system. Under skill-enhancing practices, they use aptitude tests and structured interviews for selection and provide 80 hours of annual training per employee. Under motivation-enhancing practices, they offer above-market salaries, annual bonuses tied to performance, stock options, 401(k) matching, and clear promotion paths. Under opportunity-enhancing practices, they organize employees into self-managed agile teams, hold weekly town hall meetings to share company performance data, and encourage employees to participate in strategic planning. The study examines how these three sets of practices differentially affect employee skills and motivation, and how these effects cascade to influence turnover, productivity, and profitability.

Analogy

Think of an HR system as a three-legged stool. The skill-enhancing leg represents the organization's investment in employee capabilities—the tools and knowledge workers need. The motivation-enhancing leg represents the incentives and rewards that drive effort—the reason workers want to perform. The opportunity-enhancing leg represents the structures and systems that allow workers to apply their skills—the platform for action. If any leg is missing or weak, the stool becomes unstable. However, each leg also serves a distinct function: one provides capability, one provides drive, and one provides enablement. The study examines how each leg contributes differently to the stool's overall stability and performance.

Key Insight

The key insight is that treating HR systems as a single additive index obscures important differences in how various HR practices function. Skill-enhancing practices are primarily about building human capital—they directly increase the knowledge, skills, and abilities of the workforce. Motivation-enhancing practices are primarily about shaping employee attitudes and effort—they create the desire to perform. Opportunity-enhancing practices are primarily about enabling performance—they provide the structures and autonomy for employees to apply their skills and motivation. By decomposing HR systems into these three dimensions, researchers can better understand the specific mechanisms through which HRM influences organizational outcomes. This decomposition also has practical implications: organizations can strategically invest in different HR dimensions depending on their specific goals and challenges.

Key Takeaways

  • HR systems are not monolithic; different components serve different functions and have different effects.
  • Skill-enhancing practices include recruitment, selection, and training—they build employee capabilities.
  • Motivation-enhancing practices include performance management, compensation, incentives, benefits, promotion, and job security—they drive employee effort.
  • Opportunity-enhancing practices include job design, work teams, involvement, and information sharing—they enable employees to use their skills.
  • The three-dimensional framework allows for the examination of differential effects of HR dimensions on outcomes.

Differential Effects of HR Dimensions on Human Capital and Motivation

Interpretation

The authors propose that the three HR dimensions have differential relationships with two key proximal outcomes: human capital and employee motivation. Human capital refers to the collective knowledge, skills, and abilities of an organization's workforce. Employee motivation refers to the direction, intensity, and duration of employees' effort, manifested through positive work attitudes (job satisfaction, organizational commitment, perceived organizational support) and work behaviors (organizational citizenship behavior). The authors hypothesize that all three HR dimensions are positively related to both human capital and motivation, but with different magnitudes. Specifically, skill-enhancing HR practices are expected to have a stronger relationship with human capital than motivation-enhancing or opportunity-enhancing practices. This is because recruitment, selection, and training directly optimize the levels and types of employee skills. In contrast, motivation-enhancing practices (compensation, benefits) and opportunity-enhancing practices (work teams, involvement) have less direct relationships with human capital—they may help attract and retain skilled employees or provide opportunities for skill development, but their primary function is not skill building. Conversely, skill-enhancing practices are expected to have a weaker relationship with employee motivation than the other two dimensions. Motivation-enhancing practices directly provide extrinsic motivation by linking effort to rewards. Opportunity-enhancing practices generate intrinsic motivation by providing autonomy, challenge, and meaningful work. Skill-enhancing practices, while they may enhance motivation indirectly through career development and self-efficacy, have a more distal and contingent effect on motivation. These hypotheses are grounded in the AMO framework, social exchange theory, and the norm of reciprocity.

Hypotheses for Differential Effects
H1a-c: All three HR dimensions positively relate to human capital.

H2a-b: Skill-enhancing practices relate more strongly to human capital than motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices.

H3a-c: All three HR dimensions positively relate to employee motivation.

H4a-b: Skill-enhancing practices relate less strongly to employee motivation than motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices.

Example

Consider a consulting firm. The firm's skill-enhancing practices (rigorous case interviews, MBA hiring, extensive training programs) directly build the analytical and problem-solving skills of its consultants. These practices have a strong effect on human capital. However, they may have a weaker direct effect on motivation—consultants may be skilled but not necessarily motivated unless the firm also provides performance bonuses, promotion opportunities, and challenging projects. In contrast, the firm's motivation-enhancing practices (performance bonuses, partner track, recognition programs) directly drive motivation by linking effort to valued rewards. These practices have a strong effect on motivation but may have a weaker direct effect on human capital—they don't directly teach skills, though they may help attract and retain skilled people. The study tests these differential effects empirically.

Analogy

Think of the three HR dimensions as different types of fertilizer for a garden. Skill-enhancing practices are like nitrogen-rich fertilizer that directly promotes leaf growth (human capital). Motivation-enhancing practices are like phosphorus-rich fertilizer that promotes root development and flowering (motivation). Opportunity-enhancing practices are like potassium-rich fertilizer that promotes overall plant health and fruit development (enablement). All three fertilizers contribute to plant growth, but each has a primary function. Nitrogen is best for leaf growth, phosphorus is best for flowering, and potassium is best for overall health. The study examines which fertilizer is most effective for which type of growth outcome.

Key Insight

The critical insight is that different HR dimensions serve different functions and have different strengths. Organizations cannot assume that all HR practices are equally effective for all outcomes. Skill-enhancing practices are the primary drivers of human capital development, while motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices are the primary drivers of employee motivation. This has important implications for HR system design: organizations need to strategically invest in the right mix of HR practices depending on whether their primary goal is to build skills, enhance motivation, or both. The differential effects also suggest that using a single additive index of HR practices may mask important nuances in how HR systems function.

Key Takeaways

  • All three HR dimensions are positively related to both human capital and employee motivation.
  • Skill-enhancing practices have a stronger relationship with human capital than motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices.
  • Skill-enhancing practices have a weaker relationship with employee motivation than motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices.
  • Motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices are the primary drivers of employee motivation.
  • The differential effects highlight the importance of decomposing HR systems into subdimensions.

Mediating Mechanisms: From HR to Financial Outcomes

Interpretation

The authors propose a sequential mediating model in which the three HR dimensions influence financial outcomes through a chain of intermediate variables. The model posits that human capital and employee motivation mediate the effects of HR dimensions on voluntary turnover and operational outcomes, which in turn mediate the effects on financial outcomes. Specifically, the authors hypothesize that human capital and employee motivation are negatively related to voluntary turnover. Employees with higher skills are more capable of meeting job demands, receiving positive evaluations, and obtaining promotions, making them less likely to leave. Employees with higher motivation are more committed to the organization and less likely to quit. Both human capital and employee motivation are also hypothesized to be positively related to operational outcomes (productivity, quality, service, innovation). Skilled employees perform better, and motivated employees exert more effort and engage in discretionary behaviors that enhance operational performance. In turn, voluntary turnover is hypothesized to be negatively related to financial outcomes because turnover incurs costs (recruitment, training, lost productivity) and disrupts operations. Operational outcomes are hypothesized to be positively related to financial outcomes because productivity, quality, and service directly drive profitability. The authors propose that the intermediate outcomes partially mediate the relationships between HR dimensions and financial outcomes, acknowledging that there may be other pathways (e.g., through organizational capital, social capital) not captured in the model. This sequential mediating model provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how HRM translates into financial performance.

Sequential Mediating Model
HR Dimensions → Human Capital & Motivation → Voluntary Turnover & Operational Outcomes → Financial Outcomes

H5a-b: Human capital and motivation mediate the negative relationships between HR dimensions and voluntary turnover.

H6a-b: Human capital and motivation mediate the positive relationships between HR dimensions and operational outcomes.

H7: Human capital, motivation, voluntary turnover, and operational outcomes partially mediate the positive relationships between HR dimensions and financial outcomes.

Example

A retail chain implements a new HR system. The skill-enhancing practices (better hiring, training) improve employee knowledge about products and customer service. The motivation-enhancing practices (bonuses, recognition) increase employee commitment and effort. The opportunity-enhancing practices (empowerment, information sharing) enable employees to make decisions that benefit customers. These improvements in human capital and motivation lead to lower turnover (employees are more satisfied and less likely to leave) and better operational outcomes (higher sales per employee, higher customer satisfaction scores). Lower turnover and better operations then lead to higher profitability and return on assets. The study tests whether this sequential chain of effects holds empirically.

Analogy

Think of the HR-to-financial performance pathway as a river system. The three HR dimensions are like the headwaters and tributaries that feed the river. Human capital and motivation are like the main channels that carry the water downstream. Voluntary turnover is like a diversion that reduces the flow, while operational outcomes are like the turbines that generate power. Financial outcomes are like the electricity produced. The water must flow through each stage to generate power. If the headwaters are strong (good HR practices), the channels are full (high human capital and motivation), the diversions are minimal (low turnover), and the turbines are efficient (high operational performance), then the electricity output (financial performance) will be maximized. The study examines the strength of each link in this chain.

Key Insight

The key insight is that the path from HR practices to financial outcomes is indirect and sequential. HR practices do not directly cause financial performance; rather, they create the conditions for performance by building human capital, enhancing motivation, reducing turnover, and improving operations. This means that organizations should not expect immediate financial returns from HR investments. The effects take time to manifest as they flow through the causal chain. The sequential model also implies that there are multiple leverage points for intervention: organizations can improve financial outcomes by enhancing any of the intermediate variables. The partial mediation hypothesis acknowledges that there are other pathways (e.g., through organizational culture, social capital, innovation) that may also contribute to financial performance.

Key Takeaways

  • The path from HR to financial outcomes is indirect and sequential, flowing through multiple intermediate variables.
  • Human capital and motivation mediate the effects of HR dimensions on voluntary turnover and operational outcomes.
  • Voluntary turnover and operational outcomes mediate the effects on financial outcomes.
  • The model proposes partial mediation, acknowledging other pathways from HR to financial performance.
  • The sequential model provides multiple leverage points for organizational intervention.

Meta-Analytic Methodology

Interpretation

The authors employed a rigorous meta-analytic structural equation modeling (SEM) approach to test their hypotheses. They conducted a comprehensive literature search using PsycINFO, Web of Science, and ProQuest Digital Dissertations databases, searching for studies published before May 2011. They also searched conference programs from the Academy of Management and the Society of Industrial and Organizational Psychology from 2000 to 2010, reviewed reference lists of prior reviews, and solicited unpublished studies through professional listservs. Four inclusion criteria were applied: (1) studies had to examine relationships at the organizational level (establishment, business unit, or firm), not individual or cross-level relationships; (2) studies had to emphasize the use of HR practices/systems, not their effectiveness or value; (3) studies had to report at least one correlation among individual HR practices and organizational outcomes; and (4) when the same sample was used in multiple articles, only the most informative one was included. The final sample comprised 116 articles representing 120 independent samples and 31,463 organizations. The authors coded 14 HR practices into three dimensions and categorized organizational outcomes into five categories: human capital, employee motivation, voluntary turnover, operational outcomes, and financial outcomes. They used meta-analytic techniques to correct for measurement error and sampling error, calculated composite correlations for HR dimensions and outcome categories, and used random-effects models to account for heterogeneity. The harmonic mean of sample sizes was used in SEM analyses to provide conservative parameter estimates.

Meta-Analytic SEM Procedure
Step 1: Literature search → 116 articles, 120 samples, 31,463 organizations

Step 2: Coding of 14 HR practices into 3 dimensions and outcomes into 5 categories

Step 3: Reliability correction for measurement error (Hunter & Schmidt, 2004)

Step 4: Composite correlation calculation using Hunter & Schmidt formula

Step 5: Random-effects model for sampling error correction

Step 6: SEM analysis with harmonic mean sample size (n = 3,724)
Composite Correlation Formula
rXY = Σrxiyj / [√(n + n(n-1)r̄xixj) × √(m + m(m-1)r̄yiyj)]

Example

To calculate the relationship between skill-enhancing HR practices and employee motivation, the authors first identified all studies that reported correlations between individual skill-enhancing practices (recruitment, selection, training) and individual motivation-related outcomes (satisfaction, commitment, perceived organizational support). They corrected each correlation for measurement error using the reliability estimates reported in the studies or imputed from the weighted mean of available reliabilities. They then used the composite formula to combine these correlations into a single meta-analytic estimate. This process was repeated for each pair of HR dimensions and outcome categories. The resulting meta-analytic correlation matrix was then used in SEM to test the hypothesized mediating model.

Analogy

Think of meta-analytic SEM as a way of assembling a jigsaw puzzle. Each individual study is like a single puzzle piece that provides a partial picture of the relationship between two variables. The meta-analysis is like collecting all the pieces from many different puzzle sets. The reliability correction is like cleaning and polishing each piece to make the image clearer. The composite correlation is like grouping related pieces together to form larger sections of the puzzle. The SEM analysis is like fitting all the sections together to reveal the complete picture. The harmonic mean is like using a conservative estimate of the puzzle's overall size to ensure the final image is not distorted. The result is a comprehensive, statistically rigorous picture of how HR practices influence organizational outcomes.

Key Insight

The key methodological insight is that meta-analytic SEM allows researchers to test complex theoretical models that cannot be tested in any single study. By combining data from multiple studies, the authors were able to examine the full mediating chain from HR dimensions to financial outcomes, including the differential effects of the three HR dimensions. The use of reliability corrections, random-effects models, and harmonic mean sample sizes provides conservative and robust estimates. The comprehensive literature search, including unpublished studies, reduces publication bias. The coding of HR practices into three dimensions based on the AMO framework allows for the examination of differential effects that would be obscured in a unidimensional approach.

Key Takeaways

  • The study used meta-analytic SEM to test the mediating model across 116 articles and 31,463 organizations.
  • Comprehensive literature search included published and unpublished studies, reducing publication bias.
  • Reliability corrections and random-effects models provided conservative and robust estimates.
  • The harmonic mean sample size (n = 3,724) was used in SEM analyses.
  • The coding of HR practices into three dimensions enabled the examination of differential effects.

Empirical Results and Findings

Interpretation

The meta-analytic results provide strong support for the study's hypotheses. First, the meta-analytic correlations (Table 1) show that all three HR dimensions are positively related to human capital (skill-enhancing: rc = .42, motivation-enhancing: rc = .38, opportunity-enhancing: rc = .30) and employee motivation (skill-enhancing: rc = .32, motivation-enhancing: rc = .43, opportunity-enhancing: rc = .41). The regression analyses (Table 2) confirm the differential effects: skill-enhancing practices have a significantly stronger relationship with human capital (β = .29) than motivation-enhancing (β = .22) and opportunity-enhancing practices (β = .07), and skill-enhancing practices have a significantly weaker relationship with employee motivation (β = .07) than motivation-enhancing (β = .29) and opportunity-enhancing practices (β = .25). Relative weight analyses show that skill-enhancing practices explain 48% of the variance in human capital, while motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices explain 36% and 16% respectively. For employee motivation, motivation-enhancing practices explain 45% of the variance, opportunity-enhancing practices explain 38%, and skill-enhancing practices explain only 17%. The SEM results (Figure 2) support the sequential mediating model. All three HR dimensions have significant indirect effects on financial outcomes through human capital, motivation, voluntary turnover, and operational outcomes. The final model explains 22% of the variance in human capital, 25% in motivation, 18% in voluntary turnover, 22% in operational outcomes, and 26% in financial outcomes. The post hoc analysis shows that the three-dimensional model fits the data better than a unidimensional HPWS model (AIC: 190.32 vs. 450.51).

Example

To interpret the practical significance of the results, consider a company that increases its use of skill-enhancing HR practices by one standard deviation. The model predicts that this increase would lead to a 0.29 standard deviation increase in human capital, a 0.07 standard deviation increase in employee motivation, and through these mediating variables, a 0.12 standard deviation direct increase in financial outcomes plus additional indirect effects. The total effect of skill-enhancing practices on financial outcomes is 0.13. Similarly, a one standard deviation increase in motivation-enhancing practices would lead to a 0.29 standard deviation increase in motivation and a 0.21 standard deviation increase in human capital, with a total effect on financial outcomes of 0.18. These effects are meaningful in practical terms, especially when accumulated across multiple organizations and over time.

Analogy

Think of the results as a medical diagnosis of an organization's HR system. The meta-analytic correlations are like vital signs that indicate the overall health of the system. The regression coefficients are like diagnostic tests that reveal the specific functioning of each component. The SEM path coefficients are like a circulatory system diagram that shows how blood flows from the heart (HR practices) through the arteries (human capital and motivation) to the organs (turnover, operations) and finally sustains the body (financial performance). The results show that the system is functioning as expected: the heart pumps blood, the arteries carry it, the organs receive it, and the body benefits. However, different arteries carry different amounts of blood: the skill-enhancing artery carries more blood to the human capital organ, while the motivation-enhancing artery carries more blood to the motivation organ.

Key Insight

The key empirical insight is that the three-dimensional model fits the data better than a unidimensional HPWS model, confirming that decomposing HR systems into subdimensions is theoretically and empirically valuable. The differential effects are confirmed: skill-enhancing practices are the primary drivers of human capital, while motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices are the primary drivers of motivation. The sequential mediating model is supported: HR practices affect financial outcomes indirectly through human capital, motivation, turnover, and operational outcomes. The direct effects of skill-enhancing and motivation-enhancing practices on financial outcomes suggest that there are additional pathways not captured by the mediating variables. The final model explains a substantial portion of variance in outcomes (R² = .26 for financial outcomes), indicating that the model has good explanatory power.

Key Takeaways

  • Skill-enhancing practices have the strongest relationship with human capital (β = .29) and the weakest with motivation (β = .07).
  • Motivation-enhancing and opportunity-enhancing practices have the strongest relationships with motivation (β = .29 and .25 respectively).
  • The sequential mediating model is supported: HR → Human Capital & Motivation → Turnover & Operations → Financial Outcomes.
  • The three-dimensional model fits better than a unidimensional HPWS model.
  • The final model explains 26% of the variance in financial outcomes.

Discussion and Theoretical Contributions

Interpretation

The authors discuss the theoretical contributions of their findings in detail. First, the study integrates multiple theoretical perspectives (behavioral perspective, human capital theory, resource-based view) to provide a more complete understanding of how HRM influences organizational outcomes. By demonstrating that HRM relates to financial performance both through employee motivation (behavioral pathway) and through human capital (resource-based pathway), the study shows that these perspectives are complementary rather than competing. This integration addresses a long-standing fragmentation in the literature and provides a foundation for future research that simultaneously considers multiple mediating mechanisms. Second, the study extends prior research by examining the multidimensionality of organizational outcomes and the sequential nature of HRM effects. The findings support the theoretical proposition that HRM first affects proximal outcomes (human capital, motivation), which then affect intermediate outcomes (turnover, operations), which ultimately affect distal outcomes (financial performance). This sequential model provides a more nuanced understanding of the HRM-performance relationship and suggests that researchers should be careful about the time lags they use in their studies. Third, the study challenges the common assumption that all components of HR systems have equivalent effects on outcomes. The differential effects of the three HR dimensions suggest that using additive indices of HR practices may mask important heterogeneity. This has implications for both theory development (researchers should theorize about specific dimensions rather than HR systems as a whole) and methodology (researchers should report correlations for individual practices and dimensions, not just overall system indices). The post hoc analysis showing that the three-dimensional model fits better than the unidimensional model provides strong evidence for the value of decomposition.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

Interpretation

The authors acknowledge several limitations of their study and suggest directions for future research. First, the meta-analytic approach relies on the existing literature, which may have limitations in terms of construct measurement, research design, and sample characteristics. The authors note that most studies in the sample are cross-sectional, which limits causal inference. Future research should use longitudinal designs to establish causal relationships and examine the temporal dynamics of the mediating model. Second, the study focuses on human capital and employee motivation as mediating mechanisms, but there may be other important mediators not considered, such as organizational capital (internal fit, flexibility), social capital (networks, relationships), and innovation. The direct effects of skill-enhancing and motivation-enhancing practices on financial outcomes suggest that there are indeed other pathways to explore. Third, the study combines data across different industries, organization sizes, and contexts. Future research should examine whether the relationships vary across different contexts (e.g., manufacturing vs. service, large vs. small firms, different national cultures). Fourth, the study uses broad categories of HR practices and outcomes, which may mask important nuances within categories. For example, different types of training (general vs. specific) or compensation (individual vs. team-based) may have different effects. Future research should examine these nuances. Fifth, the study does not examine the potential interactions or synergies among the three HR dimensions. Future research should explore whether the effects of one dimension depend on the levels of the other dimensions. Finally, the authors note that the meta-analytic correlations show significant heterogeneity (Q statistics are significant), suggesting that there are moderators of the relationships. Future research should identify and test these moderators.

Conclusion

Interpretation

This meta-analytic study by Jiang, Lepak, Hu, and Baer (2012) makes a significant contribution to the strategic human resource management literature by providing a comprehensive and integrated understanding of how HRM influences organizational outcomes. Drawing on the ability-motivation-opportunity model, the behavioral perspective, human capital theory, and the resource-based view, the authors develop and test a sequential mediating model in which three dimensions of HR systems—skill-enhancing, motivation-enhancing, and opportunity-enhancing practices—differentially affect human capital and employee motivation, which in turn affect voluntary turnover and operational outcomes, and ultimately financial outcomes. The meta-analytic results, based on 116 articles representing 120 independent samples and 31,463 organizations, provide strong support for the model. The findings demonstrate that decomposing HR systems into subdimensions is theoretically and empirically valuable, as the three dimensions have differential effects on proximal outcomes. The study also shows that the path from HR practices to financial performance is indirect and sequential, flowing through multiple intermediate variables. The integrated model provides a more complete and nuanced understanding of the HRM-performance relationship than previous research, which has typically focused on single theoretical perspectives or single outcomes. The study has important implications for both theory and practice, and it opens up numerous avenues for future research. The authors conclude by emphasizing the importance of adopting a multidimensional, multi-mediator, and multi-outcome perspective when examining the effects of HRM on organizational outcomes.
